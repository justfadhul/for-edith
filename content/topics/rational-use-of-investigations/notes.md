## In a nutshell

Every investigation is a question you ask the child's body. A good question has an answer that **changes what you do**. Rational use of investigations means choosing **the right test, for the right child, at the right time, at the right level of care**, and interpreting it properly. In Uganda this matters more than anywhere: families often pay for tests out of pocket, laboratories at many facilities are thin, results come back slowly, and blood is precious in a small, anaemic child.

Over-investigation wastes money, delays treatment, takes blood from children who have little to spare, exposes them to radiation and produces false positives that lead to unnecessary treatment. Under-investigation misses treatable disease: the mRDT-negative child with meningitis, the "malaria" that was a UTI, the HIV-exposed infant never tested.

> [!PEARL]
> Before you write any request form, say three things to yourself: **"What do I think this is (and how likely)? What will this test show if I am right? What will I do differently with that result?"** If you cannot answer the third question, do not send the test.

> [!UGANDA]
> This topic follows the **Uganda Clinical Guidelines (UCG) 2023**, the **WHO Pocket Book of Hospital Care for Children (2013)**, the **WHO 2024 pneumonia and diarrhoea guideline**, the **WHO guidelines for malaria (September 2026 edition)**, the **WHO 2022 TB guidelines for children and adolescents**, the **Uganda consolidated HIV guidelines (2022)** and **NICE** guidance on fever (NG143) and UTI (NG224). Laboratory capacity follows the Uganda tiered system (HC II to national referral) and the **national sample-transport (hub) network** that carries samples such as EID dried blood spots and Xpert specimens to testing sites.

> [!EXAM]
> The classic viva: "A febrile 2-year-old has a negative malaria RDT. What do you do?" **Believe a good-quality negative RDT** (sensitivity >95% for falciparum at clinical parasite densities), **do not give antimalarials**, and **look for another cause** (pneumonia, otitis, UTI, meningitis, typhoid, measles). Repeat the test or do microscopy only if the illness is severe or the clinical picture strongly suggests malaria.

## Definitions & classification

| Term | Definition | Bedside meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Sensitivity** | Proportion of people **with** the disease who test positive | A very sensitive test, when **negative**, helps **rule out** (SnOut) |
| **Specificity** | Proportion of people **without** the disease who test negative | A very specific test, when **positive**, helps **rule in** (SpIn) |
| **Positive predictive value (PPV)** | Proportion of positive tests that are true positives | **Rises with prevalence**: falls when you test low-risk children |
| **Negative predictive value (NPV)** | Proportion of negative tests that are true negatives | **Falls as prevalence rises** |
| **Pre-test probability** | How likely the disease is before the test (from prevalence, history and examination) | The single most important factor in interpreting a result |
| **Likelihood ratio (LR)** | LR+ = sensitivity ÷ (1 − specificity); LR− = (1 − sensitivity) ÷ specificity | LR+ >10 or LR− <0.1 shift probability a lot; LR 0.5–2 barely moves it |
| **Post-test probability** | Probability after the result | Pre-test odds × LR = post-test odds |
| **Reference interval ("normal range")** | The central 95% of results in healthy people of that age | **5% of healthy children are "abnormal"** on any one test; with 20 tests, most healthy children have at least one abnormal value |
| **Screening test** | Testing apparently well people to find early disease (e.g. newborn sickle cell screening, HIV testing of exposed infants) | Needs high sensitivity, low cost, and an effective intervention |
| **Diagnostic test** | Testing a child with symptoms to confirm or exclude a diagnosis | Chosen by pre-test probability |
| **Monitoring test** | Repeated to track disease or treatment (viral load, Hb in severe malaria, sodium in DKA) | Only as often as the result can change management |
| **Point-of-care test (POCT)** | A test done at the bedside with an immediate result (glucose, HemoCue Hb, mRDT, HIV RDT, urine dipstick, POC CD4, TB LAM) | Ideal for low-resource settings when quality-assured |
| **Gold (reference) standard** | The best available test for a condition (blood culture for bacteraemia, CSF culture for meningitis, Hb electrophoresis for SCD) | Often slow, expensive or unavailable |

### Types of investigation by purpose

1. **Diagnostic**: does the child have malaria, UTI, TB, HIV?
2. **Severity and complications**: how sick is this child? (glucose, Hb, SpO₂, electrolytes, creatinine, lactate)
3. **Baseline before treatment**: creatinine before gentamicin in a sick child, LFTs before TB drugs in liver disease, Hb before AZT.
4. **Monitoring response and toxicity**: Hb after transfusion, sodium in DKA, viral load on ART.
5. **Screening**: newborn sickle cell screening, HIV testing of exposed infants, TB contact screening, MUAC screening.

## Epidemiology & Uganda context

- **What is available where (typical)**:

| Level | Tests usually available |
|---|---|
| **VHT / HC II** | Malaria RDT, MUAC tape, ORS-based assessment, HIV rapid test (outreach), respiratory rate timer |
| **HC III** | Malaria microscopy and RDT, HIV RDT, Hb (HemoCue or colorimetric), urine dipstick, pregnancy test, RPR/syphilis RDT, sputum smear or sample referral for Xpert, glucose (often) |
| **HC IV / general hospital** | FBC (sometimes), blood grouping and cross-match, Xpert MTB/RIF, CD4/POC CD4, basic biochemistry (variable), ultrasound and X-ray (variable), CSF microscopy and Gram stain, sickling test |
| **Regional referral hospital** | FBC, U&E, creatinine, LFTs, CRP (variable), blood culture (some), X-ray, ultrasound, ECG, sometimes echocardiography and CT |
| **National referral (Mulago, Mengo, Lubaga, Nsambya, Mbarara/Gulu teaching hospitals)** | Cultures and sensitivities, CT, MRI (Mulago), Hb electrophoresis/HPLC, blood gases, echocardiography, TB culture and line-probe assays (via national TB reference lab), HIV viral load and EID PCR (via CPHL), histology |

- **The hub system**: samples for **HIV DNA PCR (dried blood spots), viral load, Xpert and TB culture** travel from lower facilities via **district hubs** to the **Central Public Health Laboratories (CPHL)** and the **National TB Reference Laboratory**. Results return by SMS/printer or the hub rider. **Turnaround of days to weeks** means you must **track results** and recall the family; many EID results are never collected.
- **Out-of-pocket costs**: in private wings and private labs, families pay for every test. A "full panel" on a febrile child can cost more than the treatment. Public facilities are free in principle but often out of reagents, sending families to private labs.
- **Overuse is common**: routine "widal" tests for typhoid (poor sensitivity and specificity in endemic areas), stool microscopy "for worms" in every child, repeated malaria smears after an RDT-confirmed diagnosis, and routine CXR for every cough.
- **Underuse is also common**: blood glucose not checked in convulsing children, HIV status unknown on discharge, urine never tested in febrile infants, and TB never investigated in a child with weight loss.

## Principles

### 1. Probability thinking (Bayes at the bedside)

A result changes your estimate; it rarely gives certainty. Start with the **pre-test probability** (from local prevalence + history + examination), and let the test move it.

**Worked example: malaria RDT in two settings** (assume sensitivity 95%, specificity 95%)

| Setting | Pre-test probability | Of 1,000 febrile children: true + / false + | PPV | NPV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High-transmission district (e.g. northern Uganda, rainy season) | 50% | 475 / 25 | **95%** | 95% |
| Low-transmission Kampala suburb | 5% | 47.5 / 47.5 | **50%** | >99.7% |

In Kampala a positive RDT is a coin toss for being the true cause, while a **negative RDT almost excludes malaria**. In a high-transmission area, a positive RDT is usually right, but **asymptomatic parasitaemia is common**: the child may have malaria parasites *and* pneumonia or meningitis. Always examine for other causes.

### 2. Test thresholds

- **Treatment threshold**: the probability above which you would treat without testing (e.g. a convulsing, comatose febrile child in a high-transmission area gets **IV artesunate immediately**, test or no test).
- **Testing threshold**: the probability below which you would neither test nor treat (e.g. a well child with a cold in Kampala does not need a malaria test unless febrile).
- **Test when the probability lies between the two** and the test is good enough to move it across a threshold.

### 3. Every test has harms

| Harm | Example |
|---|---|
| **Blood loss** | Daily FBC, U&E and CRP in a preterm baby → iatrogenic anaemia and transfusion |
| **Pain and distress** | Repeated venepuncture, LP; children remember |
| **Delay** | Waiting for a CT before LP or antibiotics in suspected meningitis; waiting for a smear before treating severe malaria |
| **False positives** | Widal "positive" → unnecessary antibiotics; HRP2 RDT positive after recent treatment → unnecessary antimalarials; contaminated blood culture (coagulase-negative staph) → vancomycin |
| **Overdiagnosis / incidentalomas** | A CT done for minor head injury finds an arachnoid cyst → anxiety and follow-up scans |
| **Radiation** | A head CT ≈ 1–2 mSv (about 6–12 months of background radiation); children are more radiosensitive; repeated CTs measurably increase cancer risk |
| **Cost** | Money spent on tests is money not spent on drugs, food or transport back for review |
| **Stigma / disclosure** | HIV or TB testing without proper counselling and consent |

### 4. Blood volume in small children

- Total blood volume: **neonate 80–90 mL/kg**, child about **80 mL/kg**, adult 70 mL/kg (UCG table). A 1 kg preterm has about **85 mL** of blood; a 1 mL sample is >1% of it.
- Common limits: take **no more than about 1–5% of blood volume in 24 hours** and **about 10% over 8 weeks** for non-urgent tests.
- **Batch tests**, use **paediatric (microtainer) tubes**, use **capillary samples** for glucose, Hb, bilirubin and some chemistry, and record the volume taken in neonates.

### 5. Sample quality makes or breaks the result

| Problem | Effect | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| **Haemolysis** (squeezed heel, small needle, forceful syringe) | **Falsely high potassium**, high LDH, interferes with bilirubin | Free-flowing samples, wider needle, gentle transfer |
| **Sample taken from the drip arm** | High glucose, low Hb and electrolytes diluted | Sample from the other arm or stop the drip |
| **Delay before glucose measurement** | Glycolysis lowers glucose **≈5–7% per hour** at room temperature | Measure at the bedside or use fluoride tubes |
| **Clotted EDTA sample** | Falsely low platelets | Mix gently, fill to the line |
| **EDTA contamination of biochemistry tube** | **High K, very low Ca** | Correct order of draw |
| **Bag urine** | Contamination (false-positive UTI) | Clean-catch, catheter or suprapubic aspirate for culture |
| **Blood culture underfilled or contaminated** | Missed bacteraemia or false positives | 1–3 mL in infants (≥1 mL), skin antisepsis, single venepuncture |
| **Unlabelled or mislabelled sample** | Wrong patient's result → wrong treatment | Label at the bedside, two identifiers |

### 6. Normal values change with age

| Test | Neonate | Infant | Child | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hb (g/dL)** | 14–22 at birth | **Nadir 9–11 at 2–3 months** (physiological anaemia) | Anaemia (WHO 2024): **<10.5 at 6–23 months; <11.0 at 24–59 months; <11.5 at 5–11 years**; <12.0 at 12–14 years | Do not transfuse a well 2-month-old with Hb 9.5 |
| **WBC (× 10⁹/L)** | 9–30 (first days) | 6–18 | 5–15 | **Lymphocyte predominance** from about **1 week to 4–5 years**; neutrophil predominance before and after |
| **Platelets (× 10⁹/L)** | 150–450 | 150–450 | 150–450 | Low in malaria, sepsis, HIV, DIC |
| **Creatinine (µmol/L)** | Reflects mother's in first days | ≈20–35 | ≈25–60 (rising with muscle mass) | "Normal adult" creatinine of 80 in a toddler is **renal failure** |
| **ALP (U/L)** | High | High | **High in growth spurts** (up to 2–4× adult) | Not liver disease; very high or with low phosphate → rickets |
| **Glucose (mmol/L)** | Treat **<2.6** | Treat <2.5 (WHO), <3.0 (UCG) | Same | SAM: treat <3.0 |
| **Heart rate, RR, BP** | See General Examination topic | | | Age-specific norms |

## Clinical acumen

### Focused history

The history sets the pre-test probability, which decides what to test. Ask:

- **Duration of fever and pattern**: fever ≥7 days → typhoid, TB, UTI, abscess, HIV, endocarditis, malignancy; brief fever with coryza → viral.
- **Previous antimalarial or antibiotic treatment** (and when): HRP2 RDTs remain positive for weeks; partial antibiotic treatment changes CSF and cultures.
- **Danger signs**: convulsions, unable to drink, vomiting everything, lethargy → bedside glucose, Hb, malaria test, consider LP and blood culture.
- **Exposure**: TB contact (household cough), HIV status of mother and breastfeeding, sick contacts with diarrhoea, travel to a high-malaria area, animals (brucellosis), unsafe water (typhoid, cholera).
- **Urinary symptoms** in older children; foul urine, poor feeding or fever without a focus in infants → urine.
- **Bleeding, bruising, pallor, bone pain**: FBC and film (leukaemia, sickle cell, ITP).
- **Weight loss and night sweats**: TB, HIV, malignancy.
- **Immunisation status**: an unimmunised child with fever and cough may have measles or pertussis; Hib/PCV coverage changes meningitis risk.
- **What tests have already been done**, at which facility, and the results (avoid repeating a valid test).

### Examination

- **ABCD and vital signs**: decide urgency (bedside tests now, the rest later).
- **Pallor** (palms, conjunctivae): Hb now; severe pallor plus distress → cross-match.
- **Jaundice**: in neonates, bilirubin (TcB/TSB) with the hour of life; in older children, think malaria haemolysis, sickle cell, hepatitis.
- **Focus of infection**: ears, throat, chest, abdomen, joints, skin, meninges (neck stiffness, bulging fontanelle), urine. A clear focus often means fewer tests.
- **Signs of chronic illness**: wasting, stunting, clubbing, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, oral thrush (HIV), BCG scar.
- **Neurology**: focal signs, papilloedema or deteriorating consciousness change the LP decision.

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **Do not delay treatment for tests** in: shock, hypoglycaemia, severe malaria, suspected meningitis or sepsis, status epilepticus, severe pneumonia with hypoxaemia. Take a quick sample if you can (glucose, Hb, RDT, blood culture), then treat.
> - **A critical result** (glucose <2.5, K >6.5 or <2.5, Na <125 or >155, Hb <5, platelets <20, positive blood culture or CSF Gram stain) must be **acted on immediately** and communicated to the clinician by phone, not left in a pile.
> - **A result that does not fit the child** (K⁺ 7.8 in a well, chatty child; Hb 3 in a pink, active child): **repeat before acting** (haemolysis, drip-arm sample, wrong patient).
> - **LP is contraindicated** (or delayed) with: signs of raised intracranial pressure (papilloedema, focal signs, abnormal posturing, irregular breathing, unequal pupils), coma with GCS <8 or deteriorating consciousness, shock, coagulopathy/low platelets, skin infection over the site. **Give antibiotics first.**
> - **A negative test that contradicts a strong clinical picture** (negative Xpert in a child with classic TB; negative RDT in a comatose child with retinopathy): treat on clinical grounds and seek more evidence.

### Thinking like a clinician

- **"Will this result change what I do?"** A FBC in a child with uncomplicated malaria rarely changes management; an Hb does if pale.
- **"What will I do if it is positive, and if it is negative?"** If you would do the same either way, don't send it.
- **"Is there a cheaper, faster bedside test that answers the question?"** HemoCue instead of full FBC for Hb; urine dipstick in older children; RDT instead of microscopy for diagnosis.
- **"Is this a screening question or a diagnostic one?"** HIV testing of every admitted child of unknown status is policy (screening); TB tests are diagnostic in a symptomatic child.
- **"Am I testing the child or treating my anxiety?"** Defensive "panels" do not protect children; good clinical reassessment does.
- **"Who will follow up this result?"** A test whose result nobody reads is pure harm. Write pending results in the discharge plan.
- **Common student mistakes**: ordering a Widal test for fever; ordering a CXR for every cough; repeating smears daily after an RDT-confirmed malaria; doing an HIV antibody test on a 9-month-old and calling it negative or positive; sending bag urine for culture; doing a sickling test on a 3-month-old; forgetting glucose in a convulsing child; delaying antibiotics until after a CT in meningitis.

## Clinical workup

This is the stepwise approach to deciding which investigations a sick child needs, in the order you would really do it on the ward.

### Step 0: First 5 minutes

1. **Triage (ETAT)**: emergency signs (airway, breathing, circulation/shock, coma, convulsions, severe dehydration) → treat now.
2. **Bedside tests that change emergency treatment**: **SpO₂** (oxygen if <90%), **glucose** (dextrose if low), **Hb/HemoCue** (blood if very low), **malaria RDT** (artesunate if severe malaria).
3. **Take samples at the time of cannulation** (one stab): blood culture (if available and it will not delay antibiotics), a small EDTA tube (FBC/cross-match) and a plain tube (chemistry), then start treatment.
4. **Do not send the child for imaging** (CT, X-ray) until stabilised.

### Step 1: Focused history

- [ ] **Duration and pattern of illness**: acute vs chronic tests.
- [ ] **Danger signs**: decide bedside tests now.
- [ ] **Previous treatment and tests** (antimalarials, antibiotics, recent transfusion): affects RDT, cultures, CSF and Hb interpretation.
- [ ] **HIV status** (mother and child), **TB contact**, **immunisation**, **nutrition**: decide HIV PCR/RDT, TB work-up, malnutrition labs.
- [ ] **Family history**: sickle cell disease, bleeding disorders, G6PD deficiency (neonatal jaundice).
- [ ] **What the family can afford** and how far they live (can they return for results?).

### Step 2: Focused examination

- **General**: pallor, jaundice, oedema, wasting (MUAC), lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, rash.
- **Focus of infection** (ears, throat, chest, abdomen, urine, joints, skin, meninges).
- **Neurological**: consciousness (AVPU/Blantyre coma score), neck stiffness, fontanelle, focal signs, papilloedema (LP decision).
- **Key negatives to document**: "no neck stiffness, fontanelle flat, no focal signs" justifies not doing an LP; "no chest signs, SpO₂ 98%" justifies no CXR.

### Step 3: Bedside tests

| Test | When | Interpretation tips |
|---|---|---|
| **Pulse oximetry** | Every child with cough, difficult breathing, or any danger sign | **<90% → oxygen** (WHO); <94% in emergencies (ETAT); poor trace with cold hands, nail polish, movement |
| **Blood glucose (glucometer)** | Convulsions, coma, lethargy, SAM, neonates, severe malaria, quinine, sepsis | Treat <2.5 (WHO) / <3.0 (UCG) mmol/L; neonates <2.6 |
| **Hb (HemoCue) or PCV** | Pallor, severe malaria, sickle cell crisis, before transfusion | Severe anaemia: Hb <5 (WHO severe malaria), transfusion thresholds Hb <4 or <6 with distress |
| **Malaria RDT** | Every febrile child in Uganda (test before treat) | HRP2 stays positive 2–5 weeks after treatment; *hrp2/3* deletions cause false negatives in some regions |
| **Urine dipstick** | Febrile child ≥3 months without a focus, older children with urinary symptoms, nephrotic/nephritic syndrome, DKA | Nitrite + leucocyte esterase positive → likely UTI (send culture); both negative → UTI unlikely in a verbal child; protein 3+ with oedema → nephrotic; glucose and ketones → DKA |
| **HIV rapid test** | All children of unknown status ≥18 months, and mothers of younger children | Positive in a child <18 months shows **exposure**, not infection: send DNA PCR |
| **Bedside clotting test / bleeding time** | Snakebite, suspected DIC | Clot not formed in 20 min → coagulopathy |
| **Transcutaneous bilirubin (TcB)** | Visible neonatal jaundice | Confirm with serum bilirubin if near treatment threshold |
| **MUAC** | Every child 6–59 months | <11.5 cm SAM; 11.5–12.5 MAM |

### Step 4: Laboratory & imaging

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Malaria microscopy (thick and thin films)** | Severe malaria, suspected treatment failure, recurrent fever within 4 weeks of treatment, species identification (HC III and above) | Parasite density; **>10% parasitaemia or >250,000/µL = hyperparasitaemia** (UCG 2023; WHO severe malaria definition >10%); gametocytes; non-falciparum species | Confirms; monitors clearance (fall by 75% at 48 h); a positive smear after day 3 suggests failure |
| **Full blood count + film** | Severe pallor, bleeding/bruising, suspected leukaemia, sepsis in neonates, HIV on AZT, fever >7 days (HC IV and above) | Microcytic anaemia (iron deficiency), macrocytosis (folate/B12, AZT), blasts (leukaemia), sickle cells, thrombocytopenia (malaria, sepsis, ITP) | Transfusion, iron, referral to haematology/oncology |
| **Blood culture** | Suspected sepsis, meningitis, typhoid, febrile neonates and young infants (regional/national referral; some HC IV) | Organism + sensitivity; CoNS in one bottle is usually contamination | Narrow or change antibiotics; duration |
| **CRP** | Neonatal sepsis (serial), fever without source in infants (where available) | <10 mg/L normal; serial normal CRP at 24–48 h helps stop antibiotics in neonates | Rule-out tool; never use a single CRP to withhold antibiotics from a sick child |
| **Lumbar puncture (CSF)** | Suspected meningitis without contraindication; febrile infants <1 month; prolonged/focal febrile seizures | See CSF table below | Antibiotic choice and duration (bacterial 10–21 days), TB meningitis treatment, cryptococcal antigen in HIV |
| **Urine microscopy and culture** | Febrile infants <3 months; suspected UTI with positive dipstick; recurrent UTI | ≥10⁵ CFU/mL single organism (clean catch); ≥10⁴ (catheter); any growth (suprapubic) | Antibiotic choice; imaging for atypical/recurrent UTI |
| **Electrolytes, urea, creatinine** | Severe dehydration, SAM with complications (where available), meningitis, DKA, AKI, severe malaria, prolonged IV fluids | Na <130 (SIADH, hypotonic fluids), K abnormalities, high creatinine | Fluid type and rate, potassium replacement, gentamicin dosing |
| **LFTs** | Jaundice beyond the newborn period, hepatomegaly, TB drugs with symptoms, suspected hepatitis | Conjugated vs unconjugated; transaminases | Hepatitis vs haemolysis vs obstruction; stop hepatotoxic drugs |
| **Neonatal bilirubin (total serum)** | Jaundice in the first 24 h, near-threshold TcB, prolonged jaundice (>14 days term) | Plot on hour-specific nomogram (AAP 2022) | Phototherapy, exchange transfusion; conjugated >25 µmol/L (or >20% of total) → biliary atresia work-up |
| **HIV DNA PCR (EID)** | HIV-exposed infants at 6 weeks, 9 months and 6 weeks after weaning; any sick exposed infant <18 months | Positive = infected (confirm with a second sample but **start ART immediately**) | ART, cotrimoxazole |
| **Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra** | Presumptive TB (cough >2 weeks, poor weight gain, contact) on **sputum/induced sputum, gastric aspirate, NPA, stool**, lymph node aspirate, CSF | MTB detected ± rifampicin resistance | Start TB treatment (RR-TB → MDR centre); negative does not exclude TB |
| **Urine LF-LAM** | Children with HIV and TB symptoms, advanced HIV disease, or seriously ill | Positive supports TB | Start TB treatment |
| **Chest X-ray** | Severe pneumonia not improving at 48 h, suspected effusion/empyema, pneumothorax, foreign body, TB, heart failure (HC IV and above) | Consolidation, effusion, cavities, hilar nodes, miliary pattern, cardiomegaly | Drainage, TB treatment, cardiac referral; **not routine** for non-severe pneumonia, bronchiolitis or asthma |
| **Ultrasound** | Abdominal pain/mass, intussusception, effusions, liver abscess, cranial US through the fontanelle in neonates, focused cardiac/lung POCUS | Target sign (intussusception), abscess, hydronephrosis | Surgery, drainage, referral; no radiation |
| **CT / MRI head** | Focal neurology, suspected abscess or tumour, trauma meeting decision-rule criteria (e.g. PECARN), not for simple febrile seizures (national/regional referral) | Mass, abscess, haemorrhage, hydrocephalus | Neurosurgery; CT should never delay antibiotics |
| **Sickle cell confirmation** (Hb electrophoresis/HPLC, isoelectric focusing, validated POC test) | Newborn screening, suspected SCD, positive sickling test | HbSS, HbAS, HbSC | Penicillin V, folic acid, hydroxyurea, clinic enrolment |
| **Blood group and cross-match** | Before transfusion | Compatibility | Safe transfusion |
| **Stool microscopy** | Bloody diarrhoea (amoebae), persistent diarrhoea, suspected giardia or worms causing symptoms | Trophozoites, cysts, ova | Metronidazole/albendazole; **not routine** in acute watery diarrhoea |

### Step 5: Putting it together

**Model summary with an investigation plan:** "Brian, a **9-month-old, 7.8 kg** (WFL Z-score −1), fully immunised, HIV-exposed (mother on TLD, suppressed), still breastfeeding, with **fever for 2 days and one 3-minute generalised convulsion**, now alert and playing, no neck stiffness, fontanelle flat, no focus on examination. **Bedside**: glucose 5.2 mmol/L, **RDT positive**, Hb 10.1 g/dL, SpO₂ 98%. **Plan**: treat as **uncomplicated malaria with a febrile convulsion** (oral AL 1 tablet, paracetamol), observe; **no LP** (well, alert, no meningeal signs, immunised), **no CT, no electrolytes**; **urine dipstick** is reasonable (infant with fever); check that his **9-month HIV DNA PCR** has been done (he is due) and record it in the plan."

**Worked example 1: interpreting a CSF**

| Result | Child A (2 years) | Child B (3 years, HIV-negative) | Child C (14 months, weeks of fever) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Appearance | Turbid | Clear | Clear/slightly yellow |
| WBC | **1,800**, 90% neutrophils | **80**, 90% lymphocytes | **220**, 80% lymphocytes |
| Protein | **2.1 g/L** | 0.5 g/L | **2.8 g/L** |
| CSF:blood glucose | **0.2** | 0.7 | **0.25** |
| Gram stain | Gram-positive diplococci | None | None (ZN negative) |
| **Interpretation** | **Bacterial meningitis (pneumococcal)** | **Viral meningitis** (or partially treated bacterial: consider history) | **TB meningitis** until proven otherwise (lymphocytes, very high protein, low glucose, subacute) |
| **Action** | Ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg daily × 10–14 days | Supportive; if antibiotics already given before LP, treat as bacterial | TB treatment (2HRZE/10HR) + prednisolone; Xpert Ultra on CSF; CXR; HIV test |

**Worked example 2: an FBC in a pale toddler**
A 2-year-old with Hb 6.8 g/dL, **MCV 58 fL**, MCH low, **RDW high**, platelets 520, normal WBC, no blasts, film: hypochromic microcytes and pencil cells. Mentzer index (MCV ÷ RBC count, e.g. 58 ÷ 3.6 = 16) **>13 suggests iron deficiency** (thalassaemia trait usually <13). History: drinks 1.5 L of cow's milk a day, little meat. **Iron deficiency anaemia** (thrombocytosis is common). **Plan**: oral iron 3 mg/kg/day elemental, deworm, reduce cow's milk, recheck Hb in 4 weeks (expect a rise of ≥1 g/dL); no transfusion (well compensated, no distress).

**Worked example 3: a "hyperkalaemia" that wasn't**
A well 5-month-old with gastroenteritis has a K⁺ of **7.4 mmol/L** on a capillary heel-prick sample; ECG normal, child alert. **Interpretation**: **haemolysed capillary sample** (squeezing) until proven otherwise. **Action**: repeat a free-flowing venous sample before any treatment; result 4.6 mmol/L.

## Differential diagnosis

Interpreting common abnormal results: what the result could mean, and the test that separates the possibilities.

| Abnormal result | Main possibilities in a Ugandan child | Distinguishing test / feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive malaria RDT** | Acute malaria; recent treated malaria (HRP2 persistence); asymptomatic parasitaemia with another illness | Microscopy (density, ring stages), timing of last treatment, look for another focus |
| **Negative malaria RDT in a febrile child** | Pneumonia, otitis, UTI, meningitis, typhoid, viral illness, measles, TB; rarely *hrp2*-deleted or non-falciparum malaria | Examination for focus, urine, LP if indicated, microscopy if severe |
| **Microcytic anaemia** | Iron deficiency (diet, hookworm), thalassaemia trait, anaemia of chronic disease (TB, HIV), lead poisoning | Ferritin (raised by inflammation), RDW, Mentzer index, Hb electrophoresis |
| **Normocytic anaemia with high reticulocytes** | Haemolysis (malaria, SCD crisis, G6PD deficiency, post-artesunate), acute bleeding | Film (sickle cells, bite cells), bilirubin, LDH, G6PD (after recovery), DAT |
| **Macrocytic anaemia** | Folate deficiency (SCD, malnutrition), B12 deficiency, AZT, hydroxyurea | Folate/B12 levels, drug history |
| **Thrombocytopenia** | Malaria, sepsis/DIC, HIV, ITP, leukaemia, dengue, hypersplenism | Film, other cell lines (pancytopenia → marrow), clinical context |
| **Very high WBC (>50 × 10⁹/L)** | Leukaemia, pertussis (lymphocytosis), severe bacterial infection | Film (blasts), clinical context; refer if blasts |
| **Hyponatraemia (<130 mmol/L)** | SIADH (meningitis, pneumonia), hypotonic IV fluids, diarrhoea losses, SAM (total body sodium high), adrenal insufficiency | Fluid history, urine sodium/osmolality, volume status |
| **Raised creatinine** | Pre-renal (dehydration), severe malaria AKI, HUS, nephrotoxins (gentamicin, NSAIDs), glomerulonephritis, obstruction | Urine dipstick and microscopy (blood, protein, casts), ultrasound, response to fluids |
| **Proteinuria 3+** | Nephrotic syndrome, glomerulonephritis, fever/exercise (transient), UTI | Urine protein:creatinine ratio, albumin, BP, haematuria |
| **CSF lymphocytosis** | Viral meningitis, TB meningitis, cryptococcal meningitis (HIV), partially treated bacterial meningitis | Protein, glucose ratio, India ink/CrAg, Xpert Ultra on CSF, history of antibiotics |
| **Conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia in an infant** | Biliary atresia, neonatal hepatitis (infection, CMV, syphilis), metabolic disease | Pale stools, dark urine, ultrasound, refer early (Kasai before 60 days) |

## Investigations

The core paediatric tests: what to look for and why.

| Test | What you are looking for | Why (and pitfalls) |
|---|---|---|
| **Malaria RDT (HRP2 / pLDH)** | *P. falciparum* antigen | Fast, cheap, at all levels; HRP2 persists after treatment; pLDH clears faster |
| **Malaria microscopy** | Species, density, stages | Gold standard in the field; depends on the microscopist; needed to monitor severe malaria |
| **HemoCue Hb** | Haemoglobin | Bedside transfusion decisions; capillary samples can read high or low if squeezed |
| **Glucose** | Hypo/hyperglycaemia | Cheapest life-saving test; check every convulsing or unconscious child |
| **FBC and film** | Anaemia type, infection, platelets, blasts | Age-specific normal values; lymphocytosis normal in young children |
| **Blood culture** | Bacteraemia | Take before antibiotics if no delay; 1–3 mL in infants; Salmonella, pneumococcus, *S. aureus*, Gram-negatives common in Uganda |
| **CSF** | Meningitis type | Cell count within 1 h (cells lyse); paired blood glucose |
| **Urine (dipstick, microscopy, culture)** | UTI, glomerular disease, DKA | Collection method matters; bag urine only for dipstick |
| **Electrolytes/creatinine** | Fluid and renal status | Haemolysis raises K; age-specific creatinine |
| **HIV tests** | Exposure (antibody) vs infection (PCR) | <18 months: PCR; ≥18 months: antibody algorithm |
| **TB tests** | Xpert Ultra (sputum, gastric, NPA, stool), LF-LAM, TST/IGRA, CXR | Paucibacillary disease: negative tests common; use the WHO treatment-decision algorithm |
| **Sickle tests** | Sickling, electrophoresis/HPLC, POC | Sickling test unreliable <6 months and cannot distinguish trait |
| **G6PD** | Deficiency | False normal during acute haemolysis (young cells): retest 2–3 months later |
| **CXR** | Consolidation, effusion, TB, heart size | Inter-observer variation; does not distinguish bacterial from viral pneumonia |
| **Ultrasound** | Abdomen, effusions, cranial (neonates), POCUS | Radiation-free first choice |
| **Widal test** | (Typhoid antibodies) | **Poor sensitivity and specificity in endemic areas: avoid**; use blood culture where available, otherwise treat clinically |

### Normal CSF values and patterns (RCH Melbourne clinical guideline)

| | Neutrophils (× 10⁶/L) | Lymphocytes (× 10⁶/L) | Protein (g/L) | CSF:blood glucose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Normal, <1 month** | 0 (up to 5% of cells may be neutrophils) | <22 | <1.0 | ≥0.6 |
| **Normal, >1 month** | 0 | ≤5 | <0.4 | ≥0.6 |
| **Bacterial meningitis** | 100–10,000 | <100 | >1.0 | <0.4 |
| **Viral meningitis** | <100 | 10–1,000 | 0.4–1.0 | Usually normal |
| **TB meningitis** | <100 | 50–1,000 | 1.0–5.0 | <0.3 |

A **traumatic tap** adds about 1 white cell per 500–1,000 red cells; partially treated bacterial meningitis can look "viral". **Cryptococcal meningitis** (HIV) can have near-normal cells: always do **CrAg** (or India ink) in HIV-positive children with headache or altered consciousness.

### HIV testing in children (Uganda)

| Age / situation | Test | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| HIV-exposed infant, **6 weeks** (or first contact) | **DNA PCR** (DBS via hub) | Birth PCR for high-risk infants where available; POC EID in some sites |
| **9 months** | Second DNA PCR | |
| **6 weeks after breastfeeding stops** | Third DNA PCR (final outcome) | Breastfeeding is advised up to 1 year or beyond with the mother on ART |
| **18 months** | Rapid antibody test | Negative and not breastfed for 6 weeks → HIV-negative |
| Child ≥18 months, unknown status | Antibody algorithm (rapid tests) | Confirm before ART |
| Any positive PCR | **Start ART the same day** and send a confirmatory sample | Do not wait for the confirmation |
| Child on ART | **Viral load** at 6 months then 6–12-monthly | VL >1,000 → intensive adherence counselling, repeat after 3 months |

### TB investigations in children (WHO 2022; Uganda NTLP)

- **Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra** is the initial test: **sputum** (expectorated or induced), **gastric aspirate**, **nasopharyngeal aspirate** or **stool** (now widely used in Uganda for young children), plus **lymph node aspirate** or **CSF** for extrapulmonary TB.
- **Urine LF-LAM** for children living with HIV who have TB symptoms, advanced HIV disease or are seriously ill.
- **TST or IGRA** show **infection**, not disease; useful for contacts and supportive evidence.
- **CXR**: hilar or mediastinal nodes, miliary pattern, cavities, effusion.
- Because children have **paucibacillary disease**, many are **bacteriologically negative**: WHO 2022 **treatment-decision algorithms** (symptoms, CXR, risk factors) allow treatment of children <10 years with presumptive pulmonary TB without bacteriological confirmation.

## Management

Management here means **using investigations well in the care of the child**: choosing a test plan, acting on results, and avoiding harm.

### The rational test plan: a stepwise algorithm

1. **Stabilise first** (ETAT). Bedside glucose, Hb, RDT, SpO₂ only; treat emergencies.
2. **Form a working diagnosis and two or three differentials**, with rough probabilities.
3. **For each test you are considering, ask**: Is it needed now, later, or never? Will a positive or negative result change management? Is there a cheaper bedside alternative? Can the family afford it? Who will follow up the result?
4. **Take samples in one go**, smallest volumes, correct tubes and labels, with a clear clinical question on the form ("fever 10 days, ?typhoid" is more useful than "FBC please").
5. **Start treatment** that cannot wait.
6. **Review results on the same day**; act on critical results immediately; **repeat implausible results** before acting.
7. **Document** results and their interpretation in the notes; **pending results go on the discharge summary** with who will act on them.
8. **Stop monitoring tests** when the child is improving and the result will no longer change management.

### Rational tests for common presentations

**Fever without a focus (child 3 months–5 years, alert, no danger signs)**
1. **Malaria RDT** (all levels).
2. **Urine dipstick** (and culture if positive or <3 months), especially in girls and infants.
3. **No** routine FBC, CRP, blood culture, CXR or Widal.
4. Fever ≥7 days → FBC, blood culture (typhoid), urine, HIV test, TB screen, examine again (abscess, endocarditis, malignancy).
5. **Infants <3 months** (NICE NG143): FBC, blood culture, CRP, urine microscopy and culture, CXR only if respiratory signs, **LP in infants <1 month** and in older infants who are unwell or have abnormal blood results; treat with parenteral antibiotics.

**Convulsion with fever**
1. **Glucose** and **malaria RDT** immediately.
2. **LP** if: meningeal signs, **<12 months** with incomplete Hib/PCV immunisation or pretreated with antibiotics (consider), prolonged or focal seizure, not back to normal within 1 hour, or petechial rash (after stabilisation; antibiotics first if delayed).
3. Simple febrile seizure, alert child, immunised: **no CT, no EEG, no routine electrolytes**.
4. Afebrile seizure or status: glucose, sodium, calcium (neonates, rickets), and consider imaging later.

**Cough and fast breathing**
1. **SpO₂** (oxygen if <90%), count RR for a full minute.
2. Non-severe pneumonia: **no CXR, no FBC** (WHO 2024). Treat with oral amoxicillin.
3. Severe or not improving at 48 h: **CXR** (effusion, empyema, pneumatocele, TB), **HIV test**, blood culture where available.
4. Cough >2 weeks, weight loss, contact: **TB work-up** (Xpert Ultra on sputum/gastric/NPA/stool, CXR).
5. Bronchiolitis and asthma: **no routine CXR or bloods**.

**Diarrhoea and dehydration**
1. Assess dehydration clinically (Plans A–C).
2. **No routine stool microscopy or culture** in acute watery diarrhoea.
3. **Electrolytes** only in severe dehydration needing IV fluids for long, SAM with complications (where available), altered consciousness, or seizures.
4. **Bloody diarrhoea**: treat empirically for shigella; stool microscopy for amoebae if not improving.
5. Persistent diarrhoea (≥14 days): HIV test, stool microscopy (giardia, cryptosporidium), nutritional assessment.

**Pallor**
1. **HemoCue Hb** now; **malaria RDT**.
2. Severe anaemia with distress → **grouping and cross-match**.
3. FBC and film where available (type of anaemia, platelets, blasts), sickling/electrophoresis if SCD suspected (≥6 months for sickling test), stool for hookworm in older children.
4. Recheck Hb after transfusion and at 2–4 weeks (and after IV artesunate for delayed haemolysis).

**Neonatal jaundice**
1. Visual assessment is unreliable in dark skin: use **TcB** where available and **total serum bilirubin** when near the treatment threshold or jaundiced in the **first 24 h**.
2. Plot by **hours of age** on the nomogram for gestation and risk factors (**AAP 2022**: thresholds are higher than older charts but neurotoxicity risk factors such as prematurity, haemolysis, G6PD deficiency, sepsis, albumin <3.0 g/dL lower them).
3. Jaundice <24 h or rapidly rising: **blood groups (mother and baby), DAT, FBC/film, G6PD**, sepsis screen.
4. Jaundice >14 days (term): **conjugated bilirubin**, stool colour; pale stools → urgent biliary atresia work-up.

**Suspected HIV**
1. Child <18 months: **DNA PCR**; ≥18 months: rapid test algorithm.
2. Positive: start ART, CD4 (advanced disease screening: **CrAg** if CD4 low in adolescents, **TB LAM**), baseline creatinine if TDF.
3. On ART: viral load monitoring per schedule.

### Acting on results

- **Critical results**: act at once, document the time, inform the senior and the family.
- **Unexpected results**: ask whether the result fits the child; check identity, sample quality, timing; **repeat** before major decisions.
- **Normal results in a sick child**: do not be falsely reassured (early sepsis, early meningitis, paucibacillary TB). Reassess clinically.
- **Incidental findings**: record, explain and arrange appropriate follow-up; avoid cascades of further tests unless they change care.
- **Communicating results**: explain to caregivers what the test showed and what it means; HIV and other sensitive results need private counselling and, for adolescents, attention to consent and confidentiality.

### Reducing waste (Choosing Wisely-type rules for paediatrics)

1. **No Widal test** for typhoid in endemic settings.
2. **No routine CXR** for bronchiolitis, asthma or non-severe pneumonia.
3. **No CT or EEG** for a simple febrile seizure; **no CT for minor head injury** in a child at low risk by a decision rule (PECARN).
4. **No daily "routine bloods"** in a stable child; no daily FBC in preterms without indication.
5. **No stool microscopy** for every child with acute watery diarrhoea.
6. **No antimalarials for RDT-negative fever** (test-and-treat), and no repeated smears after a confirmed diagnosis unless severe or failing.
7. **No bag urine for culture**.

## Complications

Complications of investigations (and of not investigating):

- **Iatrogenic anaemia** in neonates and small children from repeated sampling.
- **Pain, fear and loss of trust**; failed venepuncture attempts; haematomas; nerve injury (femoral sampling in infants).
- **LP complications**: post-LP headache (older children), back pain, rarely **coning** if done with raised ICP, infection, bleeding with low platelets.
- **Contrast reactions and sedation risks** for imaging (aspiration, apnoea).
- **Radiation-induced cancer risk** with repeated CT.
- **False-positive cascades**: unnecessary antibiotics (Widal, contaminated cultures), antimalarials (persistent HRP2), anxiety.
- **False-negative reassurance**: missed meningitis, TB or UTI because a single test was "normal".
- **Financial harm** to families (catastrophic out-of-pocket costs), delayed care-seeking next time.
- **Lost results**: EID results that never reach the family → infected infants not started on ART.

## Ward-round & exam pearls

**Model case presentation (with investigation reasoning):** "Aisha, a **3-year-old girl, 12.5 kg** (WFH Z-score 0, height-for-age −1.5), fully immunised, HIV-unexposed, with **fever for 9 days**, abdominal pain and loose stools, no cough. She looks unwell, temperature 39.2°C, HR 140, mild hepatosplenomegaly, no rash, no neck stiffness. **RDT negative twice**, Hb 10.2 g/dL, urine dipstick normal. **Working diagnosis: typhoid fever** (differentials: UTI excluded by dipstick, TB, HIV, brucellosis, malignancy). **Tests**: **blood culture** (the most useful test for typhoid; Widal not requested), FBC with film, HIV rapid test; TB symptom screen negative. Started on ceftriaxone 80 mg/kg daily pending culture."

**Viva questions with answers**

1. *"Define sensitivity and specificity, and explain SnOut and SpIn."* Sensitivity = true positives ÷ all with disease; specificity = true negatives ÷ all without disease. A highly sensitive test that is negative rules out; a highly specific test that is positive rules in.
2. *"Why does the PPV of a malaria RDT fall in Kampala?"* PPV depends on prevalence; with low prevalence, false positives make up a larger share of positive results.
3. *"Why can't you use an HIV antibody test in a 9-month-old?"* Maternal IgG antibodies cross the placenta and persist up to 18 months; a positive antibody test shows exposure, not infection. Use DNA PCR.
4. *"What are the contraindications to LP?"* Signs of raised ICP (papilloedema, focal signs, abnormal posture, unequal pupils, irregular breathing), deep or deteriorating coma, shock, coagulopathy/thrombocytopenia, local skin infection. Give antibiotics first.
5. *"Why is the Widal test unhelpful in Uganda?"* Background antibody levels from previous exposure and cross-reacting infections give false positives; early disease gives false negatives; single titres are uninterpretable.
6. *"Which test for TB in a 2-year-old who cannot produce sputum?"* Xpert Ultra on a gastric aspirate, nasopharyngeal aspirate or **stool**; plus CXR, TST/IGRA, HIV test; treat by algorithm if negative but clinically likely.
7. *"How much blood can you take from a preterm baby?"* Total blood volume is about 80–90 mL/kg; keep sampling to a minimum (about ≤1–5% of blood volume per 24 h), batch tests and use microsamples.
8. *"What does a K⁺ of 7.5 in a well child mean?"* Probably a haemolysed or squeezed sample: repeat a free-flowing sample and check an ECG before treating.

**OSCE tips**

- In a "request the investigations" station, **group** your tests: bedside now, laboratory today, imaging if indicated, and say **why** each changes management.
- Always mention **glucose** in any convulsing, unconscious or severely ill child.
- Interpret results **with the age** of the child ("Hb 9.8 at 8 weeks is physiological").
- In a CSF interpretation station, comment on **appearance, cells (type), protein, glucose ratio, Gram/ZN/India ink**, then give the diagnosis and treatment.

## Mnemonics & memory aids

- **"SnOut, SpIn"**: Sensitive test Negative rules Out; Specific test Positive rules In.
- **"Bedside four in every sick child: G-H-O-M"**: **G**lucose, **H**b, **O**xygen saturation, **M**alaria RDT.
- **"Will it change what I do?"** (the one question before every test).
- **HIV EID "6-9-6-18"**: PCR at **6** weeks, **9** months, **6** weeks after breastfeeding stops; rapid test at **18** months.
- **CSF "5-0-0.4-0.6"** after 1 month: ≤**5** lymphocytes, **0** neutrophils, protein <**0.4** g/L, glucose ratio ≥**0.6**.
- **LP contraindications "COAGS"**: **C**oma (deep/deteriorating), **O**edema of the disc/raised ICP signs, **A**bnormal clotting or platelets, **G**one into shock, **S**kin infection at the site.
- **Sample errors "HEAD"**: **H**aemolysis (high K), **E**DTA contamination (low Ca, high K), **A**rm with the drip (dilution, high glucose), **D**elay (low glucose).

### Classic exam traps

- **Trap:** "Negative malaria RDT, but give AL anyway because it is Uganda." **Right:** trust a negative RDT in a non-severe child and look for another cause.
- **Trap:** "Repeat the RDT 1 week after treatment to confirm cure." **Right:** HRP2 persists for weeks; use microscopy if you need to check clearance.
- **Trap:** "HIV rapid test negative at 4 months, so the breastfed infant is HIV-negative." **Right:** use DNA PCR <18 months, and the final status is only known 6 weeks after breastfeeding stops.
- **Trap:** "Order a Widal test for prolonged fever." **Right:** blood culture where available; otherwise clinical diagnosis.
- **Trap:** "CT head before LP in every child with suspected meningitis." **Right:** CT only if clinical signs of raised ICP or focal signs; never delay antibiotics.
- **Trap:** "Bag urine culture grew mixed organisms: treat UTI." **Right:** bag samples are contaminated; collect clean-catch, catheter or suprapubic urine.
- **Trap:** "Sickling test negative at 3 months, so no sickle cell disease." **Right:** HbF hides HbS in early infancy; use electrophoresis/HPLC or a validated POC test.
- **Trap:** "CXR for every child with fast breathing." **Right:** not for non-severe pneumonia, bronchiolitis or asthma; reserve for severe, non-resolving or complicated cases and TB.

### Questions seniors ask

1. **"Why did you order this test?"** State the question it answers and how each result would change your plan.
2. **"Which bedside tests does every sick child need?"** Glucose, Hb, malaria RDT, SpO₂ (and HIV test if status unknown).
3. **"How do you know this RDT-positive child has malaria and not something else?"** You don't always: parasitaemia can coexist with pneumonia, meningitis or sepsis; examine fully and treat both if needed.
4. **"When would you do an LP in a child with a febrile convulsion?"** Meningeal signs, prolonged/focal seizure, not back to normal, under 12 months with incomplete immunisation or prior antibiotics, or a petechial rash, once stable and without contraindications.
5. **"How do you diagnose TB in a 2-year-old?"** Contact history, symptoms, growth failure, CXR, Xpert Ultra on gastric aspirate/NPA/stool, TST/IGRA, HIV test; treat by the WHO algorithm if tests are negative but suspicion is high.
6. **"This potassium is 7.2. What will you do?"** Check the child and the ECG; if well and the sample was haemolysed, repeat before treating; if ECG changes, treat as hyperkalaemia now.
7. **"What results are pending at discharge, and who will follow them up?"** Name each (cultures, EID PCR, Xpert, histology) and the person responsible.
8. **"How much would these tests cost the family, and are they worth it?"** Know the main costs locally and prioritise tests that change treatment.
