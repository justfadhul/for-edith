## In a nutshell

Children are not small adults. A 3 kg newborn, a 10 kg toddler and a 40 kg adolescent can all be on the same ward with the same diagnosis, and each needs a **different dose**, often a **different formulation**, sometimes a **different drug**. Most paediatric prescribing is a calculation: **dose (mg/kg) × weight (kg)**, capped at the **adult maximum**. Get the weight right, get the decimal point right, and you have avoided most of the harm.

This handbook is organised by drug group, the way you will meet them on the Mengo and Mulago paediatric wards: antimicrobials, antimalarials, anticonvulsants, respiratory drugs, fluids and nutrition, analgesia, emergency drugs, neonatal drugs, TB and HIV drugs, vaccines and supplements, poisoning antidotes, and finally the drug emergencies you must recognise. Key drugs have a **drug card** (class, mechanism, indications, mg/kg dose, route, frequency, maximum, side effects, contraindications, monitoring, toxicity and antidote).

> [!PEARL]
> For any drug the examiner asks about, answer in this order: **what it is, why we give it, how many mg/kg, by which route, how often, the maximum, what we watch for, when we must not give it, and what reverses it.** Then do the sum out loud for the child in front of you: "He weighs 12 kg, so 50 mg/kg is 600 mg."

> [!UGANDA]
> Doses here follow the **Uganda Clinical Guidelines (UCG) 2023**, the **WHO Pocket Book of Hospital Care for Children (2nd edition, 2013)** and newer WHO guidance: the **WHO 2024 guideline on pneumonia and diarrhoea in children**, the **WHO guidelines for malaria (10 September 2026 edition)**, the **WHO 2023 guideline on wasting and nutritional oedema**, the **WHO 2022 consolidated TB guidelines for children and adolescents**, and the **Uganda consolidated HIV guidelines (2022)**. Where Uganda practice differs from WHO or UK/US guidance, it is flagged. Ugandan wards follow the UCG; when an exam question is set at UCU, the UCG answer is the safe one.

> [!EXAM]
> The commonest fatal paediatric drug errors are **tenfold errors** (a misplaced decimal point: 0.5 mg written as 5 mg, or "5.0" read as "50"), **mL versus mg confusion** (paracetamol 120 mg/5 mL versus 250 mg/5 mL), and **using an adult ampoule without dilution** (50% dextrose, 1:1000 adrenaline IV, undiluted potassium chloride). Examiners ask how you prevent them.

## Definitions & classification

| Term | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Neonate** | Birth to 28 days | Immature liver and kidneys: longer dosing intervals, avoid some drugs completely |
| **Infant** | 1–12 months | Rapid change in drug handling; doses per kg often **higher** than in adults |
| **Child** | 1–12 years | Weight-based doses, capped at the adult dose |
| **Adolescent** | 10–19 years (WHO) | Usually adult doses once ≥ about 40–50 kg; adherence and confidentiality issues |
| **Postnatal age (PNA)** | Days since birth | Sets neonatal dosing intervals (e.g. ampicillin 12-hourly in week 1, 8-hourly in weeks 2–4) |
| **Gestational age / postmenstrual age** | Weeks of pregnancy at birth / gestation + postnatal age | Preterm babies clear drugs more slowly than term babies of the same postnatal age |
| **mg/kg/dose** vs **mg/kg/day** | Dose per administration vs total daily dose | Confusing the two causes three- or four-fold errors |
| **Loading dose** | A bigger first dose to reach a target level quickly | Phenobarbital, phenytoin, quinine, caffeine, aminophylline |
| **Therapeutic index** | Gap between effective and toxic levels | Narrow: gentamicin, aminophylline, phenytoin, digoxin, chloramphenicol |
| **Off-label** | Used outside the licensed age, dose or indication | Common and often unavoidable in paediatrics; follow guideline doses |
| **Weight band** | A dose fixed for a weight range (e.g. AL 1 tablet for 5–14 kg) | Used for ACTs, TB FDCs, ARVs and cotrimoxazole to avoid calculations |

## Epidemiology & Uganda context

- **Medication errors** are about **three times commoner** in children than adults in hospital studies, and **tenfold errors** are almost unique to paediatrics. Most happen at prescribing (wrong dose, wrong weight), then at preparation (wrong dilution) and administration (wrong route, wrong time).
- In Uganda the **big killers** of under-fives are **malaria, pneumonia, diarrhoea, neonatal conditions (prematurity, birth asphyxia, sepsis), malnutrition and HIV**. The drugs for these few conditions make up most of what you will prescribe: **artesunate and ACTs, amoxicillin, ampicillin + gentamicin, ceftriaxone, ORS and zinc, Ringer's lactate, 10% dextrose, F-75/RUTF, cotrimoxazole and ARVs, phenobarbital and diazepam, blood**.
- **Availability by level**:
  - **HC II/III**: ORS, zinc, amoxicillin DT, AL, rectal artesunate (pre-referral), cotrimoxazole, paracetamol, albendazole, vitamin A, iron, diazepam (rectal), IM artesunate or artemether at HC III, first-dose IM gentamicin/ampicillin or ceftriaxone, RUTF at OTC sites.
  - **HC IV / general hospital**: IV fluids, IV antibiotics, IV artesunate, phenobarbital, blood transfusion, oxygen concentrators, nebulised salbutamol, F-75/F-100.
  - **Regional/national referral (Mulago, Mengo)**: IV phenytoin, caffeine, aminophylline, hydrocortisone, ceftazidime/meropenem, acetylcysteine, antivenom (patchy), drug levels (rarely), paediatric ICU drugs.
- **Formulations are a daily problem**: syrups run out, dispersible tablets have to be split, and caregivers often receive a bottle without a measuring syringe. A child "on amoxicillin" may be getting half the dose because a household teaspoon holds 3–7 mL, not 5.
- **Self-medication and herbal medicine** are common. Ask specifically about **local herbs** (some are hepatotoxic or nephrotoxic), **drugs bought at a drug shop**, and **left-over adult tablets**.

## Principles of prescribing for children

### 1. The weight is part of the prescription

- **Weigh every child** at admission, undressed (or subtract the clothes), on a calibrated scale, and **write the weight at the top of the drug chart**. Reweigh SAM, nephrotic and dehydrated children daily: their weight changes fast.
- **Estimating weight** when you cannot weigh (resuscitation):

| Age | APLS formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 0–12 months | (0.5 × age in months) + 4 | 6 months → 7 kg |
| 1–5 years | (2 × age in years) + 8 | 3 years → 14 kg |
| 6–12 years | (3 × age in years) + 7 | 8 years → 31 kg |
| Older formula (1–10 years) | (age + 4) × 2 | 3 years → 14 kg |

  A **length-based tape** (e.g. Broselow) is more accurate than age and gives pre-calculated doses. Rules of thumb: birth weight **doubles by 5 months, triples by 1 year, quadruples by 2 years**.
- **Malnourished children** are lighter than their age suggests: always weigh. **Obese children**: for drugs that stay in water (gentamicin) or have a narrow margin, dose on **ideal body weight** or an adjusted weight, and never exceed adult doses.

### 2. Weight versus body surface area (BSA)

- **Most drugs: dose by weight (mg/kg).** Simple, good enough, and the basis of almost every UCG and WHO table.
- **BSA (mg/m²)** tracks metabolic rate and organ size better. It is used for **chemotherapy**, some ARVs historically, and **hydrocortisone replacement**. BSA (m²) = √(height in cm × weight in kg ÷ 3600) (Mosteller). A 10 kg, 72 cm child is about **0.45 m²**; an adult about **1.7–1.8 m²**.
- **Why mg/kg doses are often higher in small children**: per kg, a toddler has a bigger liver and higher metabolic rate than an adult, so drugs like **theophylline, phenytoin and paracetamol** are cleared faster. Dosing a 10 kg child at one-seventh of the adult dose (by weight) usually *under*-doses.
- **Never exceed the adult dose.** A 50 kg adolescent on "ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg" gets 4 g (the adult maximum), not 5 g.

### 3. How children handle drugs (pharmacokinetics)

| Stage | Neonate (especially preterm) | Infant / child | What it means at the bedside |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Absorption** | Gastric pH higher (less acid), gastric emptying slow and irregular; **thin skin absorbs topical drugs** | Near adult by 2 years | Oral penicillins absorbed well; **avoid** alcohol/iodine skin washes and steroid creams over big areas in neonates; IM absorption unreliable in shock |
| **Distribution** | **Total body water ~75–80%** (adult 60%) → bigger volume for water-soluble drugs; **low albumin** and **bilirubin competing for albumin** | Body water falls to adult values by ~1 year | Gentamicin needs a **higher mg/kg** dose for the same peak but a **longer interval**; **sulphonamides and ceftriaxone displace bilirubin → kernicterus** risk |
| **Metabolism** | **Immature liver enzymes**, especially **glucuronidation** | Liver clearance **higher than adults** per kg from about 1–2 years | **Chloramphenicol → grey baby syndrome**; caffeine half-life ~100 h in preterms (once-daily dosing); toddlers need relatively large paracetamol/phenytoin doses |
| **Excretion** | **GFR low at birth** (about 20–30% of adult per m²), matures over the first year; lower in preterms | Adult by about 1–2 years | Longer intervals for **aminoglycosides, penicillins, vancomycin** in the first weeks; avoid nephrotoxins |
| **Blood–brain barrier** | More permeable | | Bilirubin, opioids and sedatives reach the brain more easily: **naloxone and caution with morphine**; apnoea after benzodiazepines/opioids |

> [!PEARL]
> The two big ideas: **neonates clear drugs slowly** (so the interval gets longer), and **toddlers clear many drugs fast** (so the mg/kg dose is relatively big). Postnatal age matters as much as weight in the first month.

### 4. Formulations and administration

- **Choose the formulation** the family can give: **dispersible tablets** (amoxicillin DT, AL dispersible, TB FDCs, pDTG, ABC/3TC) are cheaper, stable without a fridge and easier to dose than syrups. Dissolve in a little clean water or breast milk.
- **Write the strength of liquids** ("paracetamol 120 mg/5 mL, give 7.5 mL") and **give an oral syringe**. Household spoons are inaccurate.
- **Excipients**: some syrups contain **sugar** (dental caries in chronic use), **alcohol** or **propylene glycol**; **benzyl alcohol** preservative in some injections caused the fatal **gasping syndrome** in preterm babies. Use preservative-free water for neonatal dilutions where possible.
- **IV drugs for small children** must be **diluted to a measurable volume** (e.g. 1 mL of 1:1000 adrenaline + 9 mL saline = 1:10,000) and **flushed** through: a small infant's line dead space can hold a whole dose.
- **IM injections**: anterolateral **thigh** in infants and toddlers (never the buttock: sciatic nerve), maximum about **1 mL per site** in infants. **IM artesunate and artemether go into the anterior thigh.**
- **Rectal route**: diazepam (using the IV solution with a syringe without needle, or a rectal tube), artesunate suppositories, paracetamol suppositories. Hold the buttocks together for a few minutes.
- **Taste and adherence**: bitter drugs (quinine, chloroquine, some ARVs) are often spat out. **Repeat the dose if the child vomits within 30 minutes** (for ACTs and many antibiotics); for rectal artesunate, re-insert if expelled within 30 minutes.

### 5. Writing a safe paediatric prescription

Every prescription must contain:

1. Child's **full name, age, date of birth, weight (kg) and date weighed**, inpatient number, ward; **allergies** ("NKDA" if none).
2. **Generic drug name** in capitals or clear print (no abbreviations like "AZT" on an inpatient chart where "azathioprine" exists; write zidovudine).
3. The **mg/kg dose and the calculated dose**: "Gentamicin 7.5 mg/kg = **90 mg** IV once daily".
4. **Route, frequency, start date, duration or review date**.
5. For liquids: **strength and volume** (mg **and** mL).
6. For infusions: drug, amount, diluent, final volume, rate in mL/h (and drops/min for gravity sets).
7. **Your name, signature, cadre and phone** so the nurse can call.

**Writing rules that prevent tenfold errors**

- **No trailing zero**: write **1 mg**, never "1.0 mg" (read as 10 mg).
- **Always a leading zero**: write **0.5 mg**, never ".5 mg" (read as 5 mg).
- **Write "micrograms" and "units" in full**: "µg" is misread as "mg", "U" as "0".
- **Use whole units where possible**: 500 micrograms rather than 0.5 mg; 125 mg rather than 0.125 g.
- **Check the maximum**: is the calculated dose above the adult dose? If yes, give the adult dose.
- **Double check high-risk calculations** with a colleague: insulin, potassium, opioids, digoxin, IV antimalarials, aminophylline, anything given to a neonate.

### 6. The commonest errors (and how to stop them)

| Error | Example | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| **Tenfold (decimal) error** | Morphine 0.5 mg written ".5 mg" read as 5 mg | Leading zero, no trailing zero, recalculate |
| **mg/kg/day given as mg/kg/dose** | Amoxicillin "80 mg/kg" given twice daily (160 mg/kg/day) | Write per dose and frequency |
| **Wrong weight** | Using last month's weight, or a guessed weight that was never corrected | Weigh, document, reweigh |
| **Wrong strength of liquid** | Paracetamol 250 mg/5 mL given in the mL calculated for 120 mg/5 mL (double dose) | Write mg and mL and the strength |
| **Adult ampoule not diluted** | 50% dextrose IV, 1:1000 adrenaline IV, undiluted KCl | Know the paediatric strength: 10% dextrose, 1:10,000 adrenaline, KCl only diluted in a bag |
| **Exceeding adult maximum** | 40 kg child given 100 mg/kg ceftriaxone = 4 g as a single non-meningitic dose | Cap at the adult dose |
| **Wrong interval in neonates** | Gentamicin 7.5 mg/kg daily in a 1.8 kg day-2 neonate | Check postnatal age and weight |
| **Drug–drug or drug–fluid incompatibility** | Ceftriaxone with Ringer's lactate (calcium) in a neonate; phenytoin in dextrose | Know the compatibility; flush lines |
| **Omitted doses** | First dose of antibiotic delayed 6 h waiting for the pharmacy | "Stat" doses given in the emergency area; check the chart on every round |

## Clinical acumen

### Focused history

Before prescribing anything to a child, ask (each answer changes the drug, the dose or the formulation):

- **Age and weight** (and **gestation and postnatal age** in neonates): sets the dose, interval and allowed drugs.
- **Allergies**: what exactly happened? A rash on amoxicillin during a viral illness is not penicillin anaphylaxis. **Sulphonamide allergy** matters for cotrimoxazole and SP; **previous Stevens–Johnson** with nevirapine, cotrimoxazole or anticonvulsants is a lifelong contraindication.
- **What has already been given**, when, and by whom: antimalarials at the HC III (quinine in the last 12 h changes the quinine loading dose), **rectal artesunate**, **diazepam doses** (two doses already → move to phenobarbital), antibiotics, paracetamol from home (cumulative overdose), **herbs**.
- **HIV status** (child and mother) and **ART regimen**: interactions (rifampicin with DTG, LPV/r and NVP), cotrimoxazole prophylaxis (do not add SP), abacavir hypersensitivity.
- **TB treatment**: rifampicin speeds up the metabolism of DTG (double DTG dose), LPV/r, phenytoin, steroids, and stains urine orange.
- **Nutrition**: SAM changes fluids (no standard boluses), antibiotics (always), iron (not in stabilisation), vitamin A (only if eye signs or measles) and drug handling (low albumin).
- **Kidney and liver problems**: oliguria (gentamicin, potassium), jaundice (ceftriaxone in neonates, paracetamol, TB drugs, NVP).
- **G6PD deficiency or sickle cell disease**: avoid oxidant drugs (primaquine, dapsone, nitrofurantoin, sulphonamides in known deficiency); SCD needs penicillin V, folic acid and hydroxyurea.
- **Can the child swallow and keep things down?** Vomiting everything = a danger sign and a reason for the parenteral route.
- **Who will give the medicine at home**, can they read, do they have a measuring syringe, can they afford it?

### Examination

- **Weight**, **length/height** (for BSA and Z-scores), **MUAC**, oedema.
- **ABCD and vital signs** against age norms: RR and SpO₂ before and after opioids or benzodiazepines; heart rate on salbutamol and aminophylline (stop aminophylline if pulse >180); capillary refill before fluids.
- **Hydration and perfusion**: decides oral versus IV fluids and the bolus.
- **Pallor**: severe anaemia changes fluids (blood, not crystalloid), and AZT, cotrimoxazole and chloramphenicol can cause marrow suppression.
- **Jaundice** in a neonate: avoid ceftriaxone and sulphonamides.
- **Level of consciousness and gag**: unconscious children get nothing by mouth; use IV/IM/rectal or NGT.
- **Skin and mucosae**: rash, target lesions, mucosal ulcers (SJS from cotrimoxazole, NVP, phenobarbital, carbamazepine).
- **Ears and hearing** before and during long aminoglycoside courses in older children.

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **Slow or shallow breathing, SpO₂ falling** after diazepam, midazolam, phenobarbital or morphine: open the airway, oxygen, bag-mask ventilation; naloxone for opioids.
> - **Stridor, wheeze, facial swelling, hypotension or collapse** within minutes of an injection (ceftriaxone, penicillin, blood, vaccines): **anaphylaxis**, adrenaline IM now.
> - **Sweating, jitteriness, coma or a fit during IV quinine** (or any sick child not feeding): **hypoglycaemia**; check glucose.
> - **Puffy face, crackles, rising RR and liver edge after fluids**: **fluid overload** (especially SAM, severe anaemia, severe malaria): stop the fluid, sit up, furosemide 1 mg/kg IV.
> - **Bradycardia or arrhythmia during IV phenytoin, potassium or aminophylline**: stop the infusion.
> - **Blistering rash with mouth/eye involvement** on cotrimoxazole, NVP or anticonvulsants: **Stevens–Johnson syndrome**; stop the drug for life.
> - **Dark urine and new pallor 1–3 weeks after IV artesunate**: post-artesunate delayed haemolysis; check Hb.
> - **Oculogyric crisis or neck twisting after metoclopramide** (or haloperidol): acute dystonia.

### Thinking like a clinician

- **"What does this child weigh, and is the dose per dose or per day?"** Say the sum out loud.
- **"Could the drug be the problem?"** A drowsy child after rectal diazepam, a hypoglycaemic child on quinine, a deaf child after weeks of gentamicin, a jaundiced child on TB drugs, a breathless SAM child after a bolus: think of the drug first.
- **"Is this the right drug for this age?"** Neonates: ampicillin + gentamicin, not chloramphenicol; no ceftriaxone with jaundice; phenobarbital, not diazepam, for neonatal fits. Under 8: no doxycycline. Under 12: no codeine. Under 16: no aspirin.
- **"What is the cheapest effective oral option?"** WHO 2024: chest-indrawing pneumonia without danger signs is treated with **oral amoxicillin at home**, not admission for injections.
- **"Is the child getting the same drug twice?"** Paracetamol syrup from home plus IV paracetamol; cotrimoxazole prophylaxis plus SP; two benzodiazepines from two clinicians.
- **Common student mistakes**: prescribing in mL without the strength; forgetting the maximum; using adult 50% dextrose; giving boluses to a child with SAM or severe anaemia; prescribing quinine "IV stat" as a push; giving gentamicin 8-hourly at the old 2.5 mg/kg dose; forgetting zinc with ORS; giving rectal artesunate and then not referring; stopping cotrimoxazole prophylaxis in an HIV-exposed infant before infection is excluded.

## Clinical workup

For drugs, the "workup" is the set of checks before you prescribe and the monitoring afterwards. Use it every time you write a paediatric chart.

### Step 0: First 5 minutes

In an emergency (convulsion, shock, anaphylaxis, hypoglycaemia, severe respiratory distress), **ETAT first, paperwork second**:

1. **Triage**: emergency signs (**ABCD**: obstructed breathing, severe respiratory distress, central cyanosis, shock, coma, convulsions, severe dehydration) → treat immediately.
2. **Estimate the weight** (formula or tape) if you cannot weigh, and say it out loud: "About 12 kg."
3. Give the **first-line drug from memory**: oxygen, **10% dextrose 5 mL/kg** if glucose low or unknown in a convulsing/unconscious child, **diazepam 0.5 mg/kg rectally**, **20 mL/kg Ringer's lactate** for shock (15 mL/kg over 1 h in SAM), **adrenaline 0.01 mL/kg of 1:1000 IM** for anaphylaxis, **salbutamol** for wheeze.
4. **Closed-loop communication**: "Diazepam 6 mg rectally, that is 1.2 mL of the 5 mg/mL ampoule. Please confirm." A second person checks the ampoule strength.
5. **Record the time and dose** of everything given before the child moves on.

### Step 1: Focused history

- [ ] **Age, weight (measured or estimated?), gestation and postnatal age** (neonates).
- [ ] **Allergies** and previous severe drug reactions (SJS, anaphylaxis, abacavir hypersensitivity).
- [ ] **Drugs already given** today (HC III referral note, rectal artesunate, diazepam, antibiotics, quinine in the last 12 h, home paracetamol, herbs).
- [ ] **HIV status and ART**, TB treatment, cotrimoxazole prophylaxis.
- [ ] **Nutrition** (SAM?), **sickle cell disease**, **kidney or liver disease**, **G6PD** if known.
- [ ] **Ability to swallow and vomiting**: decides the route.
- [ ] **Caregiver**: who gives the medicine, literacy, cost.

### Step 2: Focused examination

- **Weight, length, MUAC, oedema** (dose and nutrition).
- **ABCD and vital signs** against age norms; **SpO₂**.
- **Perfusion** (capillary refill, pulse volume, cold hands) and **hydration**.
- **Pallor and jaundice**.
- **Consciousness** (AVPU), **gag and swallow**.
- **Skin and mucosa** (baseline before cotrimoxazole, NVP, anticonvulsants).

### Step 3: Bedside tests

- **Blood glucose** (glucometer) in every convulsing, unconscious, severely ill, malnourished or quinine-treated child.
- **Malaria RDT** before antimalarials (treat severe malaria immediately if the test is delayed).
- **Hb (HemoCue)** before transfusion decisions, on AZT and in severe malaria.
- **Pulse oximetry** before and after oxygen, opioids and sedatives.
- **Urine output** (weigh nappies: 1 g ≈ 1 mL; normal ≥1 mL/kg/h) on gentamicin, potassium and fluids.
- **Urine dipstick** for glucose and ketones (DKA), blood (haemoglobinuria after artesunate or in G6PD crises).

### Step 4: Laboratory & imaging

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Serum creatinine / urea** | Before and during gentamicin, vancomycin, TDF; oliguria; severe malaria | Rise above age normal (e.g. >35 µmol/L in an infant, >60 in a school child) | Extend the gentamicin interval or switch to ceftriaxone; reduce renally cleared drugs; stop NSAIDs (regional referral; HC IV sometimes) |
| **Serum electrolytes (Na, K)** | IV fluids >24 h, SAM, diarrhoea, DKA, potassium replacement | Na <130 or >150; K <3 or >5.5 mmol/L | Change the fluid; correct K slowly; stop KCl if K high or oliguria |
| **Full blood count** | AZT, cotrimoxazole high dose, chloramphenicol, severe anaemia | Hb <8 g/dL on AZT; neutropenia | Switch AZT; stop chloramphenicol; transfuse by threshold |
| **LFTs (ALT, bilirubin)** | TB drugs, NVP, high-dose paracetamol, valproate, overdose | ALT >3× ULN with symptoms or >5× without | Stop hepatotoxic drugs, reintroduce one by one; acetylcysteine for paracetamol |
| **Paracetamol level** (4 h post-ingestion) | Paracetamol overdose (national referral; rarely available) | Above the treatment line | Acetylcysteine; if unavailable, treat on the history of dose (>150 mg/kg) |
| **Serum calcium, magnesium** | Neonatal fits, hypocalcaemic tetany, rickets, SAM | Ca <2.0 mmol/L (or ionised <1.0) | Calcium gluconate 10% slowly IV |
| **Drug levels** (phenytoin, gentamicin trough, digoxin, aminophylline) | Toxicity or poor response (rarely available outside national referral) | Gentamicin trough >1–2 mg/L | Lengthen interval; clinical monitoring where no levels |
| **Blood culture and CSF** | Before the first antibiotic dose if it does not delay treatment | Organism and sensitivity | Narrow or change the antibiotic (regional/national referral) |
| **Blood group and cross-match** | Severe anaemia (Hb <4 g/dL or <6 with distress) | | Transfuse 20 mL/kg whole blood or 10 mL/kg packed cells (TRACT: 30 mL/kg whole blood in some) |
| **HIV DNA PCR / rapid test** | Every admitted child of unknown status | Positive | Cotrimoxazole, ART, drug interactions |
| **Chest X-ray** | Before TB treatment if available; suspected aspiration (kerosene) | | Supports TB diagnosis; chemical pneumonitis |

### Step 5: Putting it together

**Model summary before prescribing:** "Baby Namata, 2 years, **12 kg** (WFH Z-score −1), HIV-unexposed, fully immunised, no allergies. Severe malaria with prostration and Hb 7.8 g/dL; glucose 4.6 mmol/L; tolerating oral fluids poorly. **Plan**: IV artesunate **3 mg/kg = 36 mg** (child <20 kg) at 0, 12 and 24 h then daily; switch to a full 3-day ACT when she can drink; paracetamol **15 mg/kg = 180 mg** 6-hourly; maintenance fluid **1,100 mL/day** (100 × 10 + 50 × 2) mostly orally; glucose 6-hourly; Hb check day 3 and at 2–3 weeks for delayed haemolysis; discharge on DP and post-discharge malaria chemoprevention."

**Worked example 1: a gentamicin dose**
A 14-day-old baby weighing 2.8 kg with suspected sepsis. Weeks 2–4 of life → gentamicin **7.5 mg/kg once daily** (WHO) = **21 mg**. The vial is 40 mg/mL; 21 mg = 0.525 mL. Dilute 1 mL (40 mg) with 3 mL saline = 10 mg/mL; give **2.1 mL** IV over 3–5 minutes. Ampicillin 50 mg/kg = **140 mg** every **8 hours** (second week of life). Check that the baby passes urine.

**Worked example 2: is this paracetamol dose safe?**
An 8 kg infant has been given "paracetamol syrup 10 mL 4-hourly" at home for 2 days. If the syrup is 120 mg/5 mL, each dose is **240 mg = 30 mg/kg**, six times a day = **180 mg/kg/day** (maximum 60). If it is the 250 mg/5 mL syrup, it is **375 mg/kg/day**. This is a **repeated supratherapeutic ingestion**: check LFTs, glucose and INR if possible, start **acetylcysteine** and seek senior advice.

**Worked example 3: converting a 50% dextrose ampoule**
A 10 kg unconscious child, glucose 1.8 mmol/L. Dose: **10% dextrose 5 mL/kg = 50 mL**. If only 50% dextrose and water are available: **1 mL/kg of 50% (10 mL) + 4 mL/kg water (40 mL)** = 50 mL of 10% dextrose. Give over 5 minutes, recheck glucose in 30 minutes, then start a dextrose-containing maintenance fluid or feeds.

## Antimicrobials

Most antibiotics on a Ugandan paediatric ward are prescribed for **pneumonia, sepsis, meningitis, neonatal infection and SAM**. Learn the "ward six" (ampicillin, gentamicin, ceftriaxone, amoxicillin, cloxacillin, metronidazole) perfectly, then the rest.

### Antibiotics by indication (UCG 2023 / WHO)

| Indication | First line | Duration / notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Pneumonia, fast breathing or chest indrawing, no danger sign (2–59 months)** | **Amoxicillin DT 40 mg/kg orally 12-hourly** | **5 days** (WHO 2024: 3 or 5 days for fast breathing only; 5 days for chest indrawing), outpatient |
| **Severe pneumonia** (danger sign, SpO₂ <90%) | **Ampicillin 50 mg/kg IV 6-hourly** (or benzylpenicillin 50,000 IU/kg 6-hourly) **+ gentamicin 7.5 mg/kg once daily** | ≥5 days; switch to oral amoxicillin when improving. **Not better at 48 h → ceftriaxone 80 mg/kg daily**; staph suspected → **cloxacillin 50 mg/kg 6-hourly + gentamicin** |
| **Young infant (0–59 days) with possible serious bacterial infection** | **Ampicillin 50 mg/kg + gentamicin** (5 mg/kg week 1, 7.5 mg/kg after) | 7–10 days; meningitis 14–21 days. If referral is refused: **gentamicin IM daily + oral amoxicillin 50 mg/kg 12-hourly × 7 days** (WHO 2015 PSBI) |
| **Bacterial meningitis (>1 month)** | **Ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg IV once daily** (or 50 mg/kg 12-hourly), max 4 g/day | 10 days (7–14; longer for Gram-negatives, GBS) |
| **Sepsis (child >2 months)** | Ampicillin + gentamicin, or ceftriaxone 50–80 mg/kg daily | 7–10 days; add cloxacillin for skin/bone source |
| **Bloody diarrhoea (dysentery)** | **Ciprofloxacin 15 mg/kg orally 12-hourly × 3 days** (WHO 2024) | Second line: ceftriaxone 50–80 mg/kg daily × 3 days. **No antibiotics for acute watery diarrhoea** (except cholera) |
| **Cholera** | **Azithromycin 20 mg/kg single dose** (max 1 g) | Rehydration is the treatment; antibiotics shorten shedding |
| **SAM, uncomplicated** | **Amoxicillin 40 mg/kg 12-hourly × 5 days** (UCG) | WHO 2023: may be omitted in carefully monitored outpatients; Uganda still gives it |
| **SAM, complicated (inpatient)** | **Ampicillin 50 mg/kg 6-hourly × 2 days then oral amoxicillin × 5 days + gentamicin 7.5 mg/kg daily × 7 days** | Ceftriaxone if no improvement at 48 h or meningitis |
| **Osteomyelitis / septic arthritis** | **Cloxacillin 50 mg/kg IV 6-hourly** (+ gentamicin in young children) | IV until improving, then oral, total **3–6 weeks** |
| **Acute otitis media** | Amoxicillin 40 mg/kg 12-hourly | 5 days |
| **Streptococcal pharyngitis** | **Benzathine penicillin** single IM dose, or amoxicillin 50 mg/kg once daily × 10 days | Prevents rheumatic fever |
| **Pertussis** | **Azithromycin 10 mg/kg day 1, 5 mg/kg days 2–5** (or erythromycin 12.5 mg/kg 6-hourly × 10 days) | Treat contacts <1 year |
| **Typhoid** | Ciprofloxacin 15 mg/kg 12-hourly, or azithromycin 10–20 mg/kg daily, or ceftriaxone | 7–14 days depending on drug and severity |

### Drug card: Amoxicillin

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Aminopenicillin (beta-lactam) |
| **Mechanism** | Binds penicillin-binding proteins → blocks cell-wall cross-linking → **bactericidal**; **time-dependent** killing (time above MIC matters, so give it regularly) |
| **Indications (paeds)** | Community pneumonia (outpatient and step-down), otitis media, sinusitis, pharyngitis, uncomplicated SAM, PSBI when referral is not possible, step-down after IV ampicillin |
| **Dose, route, frequency** | **Pneumonia: 40 mg/kg orally 12-hourly** (80 mg/kg/day) × 5 days. **UCG weight/age bands (250 mg DT)**: **2–12 months: 250 mg (1 tab) BD; 1–3 years: 500 mg (2 tabs) BD; 3–5 years: 750 mg (3 tabs) BD**. Young infant PSBI: **50 mg/kg 12-hourly × 7 days**. Older/milder infections (WHO 2013): 25 mg/kg 12-hourly |
| **Maximum** | **1 g per dose** (3–4 g/day) |
| **Side effects** | Diarrhoea, nappy candidiasis, rash (a non-allergic **maculopapular rash is common with EBV** infection), rarely anaphylaxis |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | True penicillin allergy (anaphylaxis, angioedema) |
| **Monitoring** | Clinical response at **day 3**: fever, RR, chest indrawing; return immediately if danger signs |
| **Toxicity & antidote** | Very safe; no antidote. Anaphylaxis → adrenaline |

> [!PEARL]
> **Dispersible tablets** (250 mg) are the Ugandan standard: dissolve in a little water or breast milk in a cup. Show the caregiver, and ask her to show you back. The commonest cause of "treatment failure" in outpatient pneumonia is that the child got half the doses.

### Drug card: Ampicillin

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Aminopenicillin (IV/IM) |
| **Mechanism** | Cell-wall synthesis inhibitor, bactericidal, time-dependent. Covers streptococci, *Listeria*, enterococci, many *H. influenzae* and some *E. coli*; **not** penicillinase-producing staph |
| **Indications (paeds)** | **Severe pneumonia, neonatal sepsis and meningitis** (with gentamicin), complicated SAM, sepsis of unknown source |
| **Dose, route, frequency** | **Child >1 month: 50 mg/kg IV/IM 6-hourly.** **Neonates (WHO)**: **50 mg/kg 12-hourly in week 1, 8-hourly in weeks 2–4** (meningitis: 100 mg/kg per dose at the same intervals). UCG neonatal sepsis lists **50 mg/kg 6-hourly** |
| **Maximum** | 2 g per dose (12 g/day in meningitis) |
| **Side effects** | Diarrhoea, rash, phlebitis; seizures with very high doses in renal failure |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Penicillin anaphylaxis; reduce frequency in severe renal impairment |
| **Monitoring** | Temperature, feeding, RR, perfusion; culture results |
| **Toxicity & antidote** | None specific |

> [!UGANDA]
> UCG 2023 gives ampicillin **6-hourly** for neonatal sepsis whatever the age; the WHO Pocket Book uses **12-hourly in the first week** and **8-hourly in weeks 2–4** because neonatal kidneys clear it slowly. Both are safe (ampicillin has a wide margin). Follow your unit chart at Mengo or Mulago, but know why the WHO interval is longer.

### Drug card: Gentamicin

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Aminoglycoside |
| **Mechanism** | Binds the **30S ribosomal subunit** → misreading of mRNA; **concentration-dependent** killing with a **post-antibiotic effect** (hence once-daily dosing). Gram-negative cover; synergy with ampicillin against GBS, *Listeria*, enterococci |
| **Indications (paeds)** | Neonatal sepsis, severe pneumonia, complicated SAM, UTI/pyelonephritis, sepsis (always **with** a beta-lactam) |
| **Dose, route, frequency** | **Child >1 month (and neonates in weeks 2–4): 7.5 mg/kg IV/IM once daily.** **Neonates, week 1: 5 mg/kg once daily if ≥2.5 kg; 3 mg/kg once daily if low birth weight** (some units extend to 36–48-hourly in very preterm babies). Give IV slowly over 3–5 min or IM into the thigh |
| **Maximum** | Dose by mg/kg; in obese children use ideal/adjusted body weight. Course usually **≤7–10 days** |
| **Side effects** | **Nephrotoxicity** (usually reversible), **ototoxicity** (cochlear and vestibular, often permanent), neuromuscular blockade (apnoea with MgSO₄ or in botulism/myasthenia) |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | AKI/oliguria, myasthenia gravis; avoid with furosemide or other nephrotoxins; mitochondrial mutation (m.1555A>G) causes deafness after a single dose (rarely tested in Uganda) |
| **Monitoring** | **Urine output**, creatinine at baseline and every 2–3 days in longer courses, **trough level <1 mg/L** before the next dose where available (rare), hearing screen after long courses |
| **Toxicity & antidote** | No antidote; stop and switch to ceftriaxone. Neuromuscular block → ventilatory support; calcium may help |

> [!DRUG]
> The old regimen of **2.5 mg/kg 8-hourly** is still seen on charts. Once-daily **7.5 mg/kg** gives a higher peak (better killing), **less kidney toxicity** (a drug-free trough lets the tubules clear it) and one injection instead of three. In a child with poor urine output or rising creatinine, **hold the dose** and switch to ceftriaxone rather than "reducing" it blindly.

### Drug card: Ceftriaxone

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Third-generation cephalosporin |
| **Mechanism** | Beta-lactam, bactericidal; broad Gram-negative and streptococcal cover, crosses inflamed meninges well; **long half-life → once-daily dosing** |
| **Indications (paeds)** | **Meningitis**, sepsis, severe pneumonia failing first line, dysentery (second line), typhoid, gonococcal ophthalmia neonatorum, pre-referral single dose |
| **Dose, route, frequency** | **Sepsis/pneumonia: 50–80 mg/kg IV/IM once daily** (UCG pneumonia: 80 mg/kg). **Meningitis: 100 mg/kg once daily** (or 50 mg/kg 12-hourly). **Neonates: 50 mg/kg once daily (100 mg/kg for meningitis)**, only if not jaundiced. **Ophthalmia neonatorum: 50 mg/kg IM single dose (max 125 mg)**. IV over **30 minutes** in neonates (or 2–4 min bolus in older children); IM dissolved in 1% lignocaine (never give the lignocaine solution IV) |
| **Maximum** | **4 g/day** (meningitis); 2 g per dose otherwise |
| **Side effects** | Diarrhoea, rash, biliary sludge ("pseudolithiasis"), phlebitis, *C. difficile*, rarely immune haemolysis |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Neonates with hyperbilirubinaemia or preterm** (displaces bilirubin from albumin → kernicterus): use **cefotaxime** if available. **Never with IV calcium-containing fluids in neonates** (Ringer's lactate, calcium gluconate, TPN): fatal **ceftriaxone–calcium precipitates** in lungs and kidneys. In older children, flush the line or use separate lines. Cephalosporin anaphylaxis; caution with penicillin anaphylaxis |
| **Monitoring** | Fever curve, consciousness, neck stiffness, seizures (meningitis); jaundice in neonates |
| **Toxicity & antidote** | No antidote |

> [!REDFLAG]
> **Ceftriaxone + Ringer's lactate in a neonate is a never event.** Use normal saline or dextrose to flush and dilute, and give calcium (if needed) through a different line after a 48 h gap in neonates.

### Drug card: Cloxacillin (and flucloxacillin)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Penicillinase-resistant (anti-staphylococcal) penicillin |
| **Mechanism** | Beta-lactam stable to staphylococcal penicillinase |
| **Indications (paeds)** | ***Staphylococcus aureus***: pneumonia with pneumatoceles/empyema or after measles, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, pyomyositis, cellulitis, impetigo, neonatal skin/umbilical sepsis |
| **Dose, route, frequency** | **25–50 mg/kg IV/IM 6-hourly** (50 mg/kg for severe infection, bone, empyema). **Neonates: 25–50 mg/kg 12-hourly in week 1, 8-hourly in weeks 2–4.** Oral (flu)cloxacillin **12.5–25 mg/kg 6-hourly** |
| **Maximum** | 2 g per dose |
| **Side effects** | Phlebitis, diarrhoea, rash; cholestatic hepatitis (flucloxacillin, rare in children) |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Penicillin allergy; does **not** cover MRSA (vancomycin or clindamycin) |
| **Monitoring** | Fever, local signs, CRP/ESR where available; X-ray for bone |
| **Toxicity & antidote** | None |

### Drug card: Metronidazole

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Nitroimidazole |
| **Mechanism** | Reduced inside anaerobes and protozoa to radicals that break DNA |
| **Indications (paeds)** | Anaerobic cover (peritonitis, perforated appendix, brain abscess, aspiration), **necrotising enterocolitis** (with ampicillin + gentamicin), **neonatal tetanus**, **amoebic dysentery/liver abscess**, **giardiasis**, *C. difficile* |
| **Dose, route, frequency** | **7.5 mg/kg IV/oral 8-hourly** (anaerobes, tetanus; WHO). **Amoebiasis: 10 mg/kg 8-hourly × 5–10 days.** **Giardiasis: 5 mg/kg 8-hourly × 5–7 days.** Neonates: 7.5 mg/kg 12-hourly (first weeks) |
| **Maximum** | 500 mg per dose |
| **Side effects** | Nausea, metallic taste, dark urine; disulfiram reaction with alcohol (adolescents, alcohol-containing syrups); peripheral neuropathy on long courses |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Severe liver disease (reduce dose) |
| **Monitoring** | Clinical response; LFTs if prolonged |
| **Toxicity & antidote** | None specific |

### Drug card: Co-trimoxazole (sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Sulfonamide + diaminopyrimidine (ratio **5:1**). Strengths: paediatric tablet **120 mg** (100/20), syrup **240 mg/5 mL** (200/40), adult **480 mg** (400/80) and **960 mg** (800/160) |
| **Mechanism** | Sequential block of **bacterial folate synthesis** (dihydropteroate synthase, then dihydrofolate reductase) |
| **Indications (paeds)** | **Prophylaxis (CPT)** for HIV-exposed infants from **6 weeks** and all children living with HIV (prevents PCP, toxoplasma, malaria, bacterial infections); **PCP treatment**; UTI (if sensitive); some shigella (resistance common) |
| **Dose, route, frequency** | **CPT once daily by weight (UCG): <5 kg: 120 mg; 5–14.9 kg: 240 mg; 15–29.9 kg: 480 mg; ≥30 kg: 960 mg.** **Treatment: 4 mg/kg trimethoprim (24 mg/kg combined) 12-hourly.** **PCP: 120 mg/kg/day combined (= 20 mg/kg/day trimethoprim) in 3–4 divided doses × 21 days** (UCG; WHO Pocket Book 8 mg/kg TMP 8-hourly), IV or oral, plus **prednisolone** if hypoxic |
| **Maximum** | 960 mg per dose (prophylaxis); treatment by mg/kg |
| **Side effects** | Rash, **Stevens–Johnson syndrome**, marrow suppression (neutropenia, anaemia), hepatitis, hyperkalaemia (high dose), kernicterus risk in jaundiced neonates |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Sulfa allergy/SJS; **first 6 weeks of life** (bilirubin displacement); **severe anaemia or neutropenia**; G6PD deficiency (caution); **never with SP** (duplicate antifolate) |
| **Monitoring** | Rash at every visit (**stop at once if mucosal involvement**), FBC if pale |
| **Toxicity & antidote** | Marrow suppression: stop; **folinic acid** for severe antifolate toxicity. SJS: stop for life, supportive care, use **dapsone** as alternative prophylaxis |

> [!UGANDA]
> HIV-exposed infants start CPT at **6 weeks** (when NVP prophylaxis dose changes) and continue **until HIV infection is excluded after breastfeeding ends**. Children living with HIV continue it **until at least 15 years** (UCG 2023 stopping rules: >15 years, on ART ≥1 year, suppressed VL, no stage 3/4 event, not pregnant).

### Drug card: Azithromycin

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Macrolide (azalide) |
| **Mechanism** | Binds **50S** ribosomal subunit; long tissue half-life allows short courses |
| **Indications (paeds)** | **Pertussis**, cholera, atypical pneumonia (*Mycoplasma*), typhoid, trachoma, yaws, penicillin allergy |
| **Dose, route, frequency** | **Pertussis: 10 mg/kg once daily day 1 (max 500 mg), then 5 mg/kg (max 250 mg) days 2–5** (infants <6 months: 10 mg/kg daily × 5 days). **Cholera/trachoma: 20 mg/kg single dose (max 1 g).** **Typhoid: 10–20 mg/kg daily × 7 days.** Atypical pneumonia: 10 mg/kg daily × 3 days |
| **Maximum** | 500 mg/day (1 g single dose) |
| **Side effects** | Diarrhoea, vomiting, **QT prolongation**, **infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis** in the first weeks of life (smaller risk than erythromycin) |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | QT-prolonging drugs (quinine, some antimalarials), liver disease |
| **Monitoring** | Projectile vomiting in neonates (pyloric stenosis) |
| **Toxicity & antidote** | None |

### Drug card: Benzylpenicillin (crystalline penicillin, "X-pen")

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Natural penicillin (IV/IM). **1 mega-unit (MU) = 600 mg** |
| **Mechanism** | Cell-wall synthesis inhibitor; excellent for streptococci, pneumococcus (mostly), meningococcus, *Treponema*, clostridia |
| **Indications (paeds)** | Severe pneumonia (with gentamicin), meningococcal disease, **symptomatic congenital syphilis**, tetanus, diphtheria |
| **Dose, route, frequency** | **50,000 IU/kg IV/IM 6-hourly** (pneumonia). **Meningitis: 100,000 IU/kg 6-hourly** (UCG: 100,000–150,000 IU/kg 4–6-hourly). **Neonates: 50,000 IU/kg 12-hourly in week 1, 8-hourly in weeks 2–4** (double for meningitis). **Congenital syphilis (symptomatic): 50,000 IU/kg IV 12-hourly for the first 7 days, then 8-hourly, total 10 days** (or procaine penicillin 50,000 IU/kg IM daily × 10 days) |
| **Maximum** | 2.4 g (4 MU) per dose |
| **Side effects** | Allergy, phlebitis, seizures (huge doses in renal failure), Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction in syphilis |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Penicillin anaphylaxis |
| **Monitoring** | Clinical response |
| **Toxicity & antidote** | None |

### Drug card: Benzathine penicillin G

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Long-acting depot penicillin (IM only) |
| **Mechanism** | Slow release from the muscle gives low penicillin levels for **2–4 weeks** |
| **Indications (paeds)** | **Asymptomatic congenital syphilis** (baby of an untreated/inadequately treated RPR-positive mother, normal examination); **streptococcal pharyngitis**; **secondary prophylaxis of rheumatic fever/RHD** |
| **Dose, route, frequency** | **Congenital syphilis (asymptomatic): 50,000 IU/kg deep IM single dose** (anterolateral thigh). **Pharyngitis: 600,000 IU if <30 kg, 1.2 MU if ≥30 kg, single dose.** **Rheumatic fever prophylaxis: 600,000 IU (<30 kg) or 1.2 MU (≥30 kg) IM every 4 weeks** (every 3 weeks in high-risk patients), for at least 10 years or to age 21 (longer with severe RHD) |
| **Maximum** | 2.4 MU per dose |
| **Side effects** | **Painful injection** (can be reconstituted with lignocaine), allergy |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Penicillin anaphylaxis. **Never IV** (cardiorespiratory arrest, embolic gangrene). Never for neurosyphilis or symptomatic congenital syphilis (poor CSF levels) |
| **Monitoring** | Observe 30 min after injection; RHD clinic attendance |
| **Toxicity & antidote** | Anaphylaxis → adrenaline |

| Drug | Dose | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Chloramphenicol** | **25 mg/kg IV/oral 6-hourly** (max 1 g/dose) | Cheap, crosses into CSF; **contraindicated in preterms and neonates <7 days** (**grey baby syndrome**: vomiting, grey colour, hypotension, collapse from immature glucuronidation); idiosyncratic **aplastic anaemia**. Second-line in Uganda |
| **Cefotaxime** | Neonates **50 mg/kg 12-hourly (week 1), 8-hourly (weeks 2–4)**; children 50 mg/kg 6–8-hourly | Preferred over ceftriaxone in jaundiced neonates; less available |

> [!EXAM]
> The examiner's favourite antibiotic questions in paediatrics: (1) the **two drugs and doses for a sick young infant** (ampicillin 50 mg/kg + gentamicin 5–7.5 mg/kg); (2) **why not ceftriaxone in a jaundiced neonate** (bilirubin displacement, calcium precipitation); (3) **why not chloramphenicol in the first week** (grey baby); (4) **why once-daily gentamicin** (concentration-dependent killing, post-antibiotic effect, less nephrotoxicity); (5) **oral amoxicillin dose for pneumonia** (40 mg/kg BD × 5 days).

## Antimalarials

Malaria is the leading cause of admission on most Ugandan paediatric wards. The drugs are simple; the art is in **recognising severe malaria**, giving **artesunate fast**, **treating hypoglycaemia and anaemia**, and **completing the oral course**.

### Uganda national malaria treatment policy (UCG 2023)

| Situation | First line | Alternatives |
|---|---|---|
| **Uncomplicated malaria** (all ages, including infants) | **Artemether–lumefantrine (AL)** | Artesunate–amodiaquine (AS-AQ); second line **dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine (DP)**; pyronaridine–artesunate; quinine tablets if none available |
| **Severe malaria** | **IV artesunate** | IM artesunate; **IV quinine** or **IM artemether** if artesunate unavailable |
| **Pre-referral** (no IV facilities) | **Rectal artesunate** (children **≤6 years** only) | IM artesunate, IM artemether or IM quinine |
| **After severe malaria** | Complete with a **3-day ACT**; UCG 2023 discharges severe malaria on **DP**, then **monthly DP for 3 months** (post-discharge malaria chemoprevention, PDMC) | |

### Drug card: Artesunate (IV/IM)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Artemisinin derivative (water-soluble) |
| **Mechanism** | Endoperoxide bridge activated by haem iron → free radicals that kill **all blood stages, including young ring forms**, so it clears parasites fastest and prevents sequestration. Converted to **dihydroartemisinin (DHA)**; very short half-life (<1 h), so it must be followed by a partner drug |
| **Indications (paeds)** | **All severe malaria** (any age, any trimester), and uncomplicated malaria in a child who cannot take oral drugs |
| **Dose, route, frequency** | **Child <20 kg: 3 mg/kg; ≥20 kg: 2.4 mg/kg** IV (or IM into the anterior thigh) **at 0, 12 and 24 h, then once daily** until able to take oral medicine (minimum 24 h/3 doses), **then a full 3-day ACT** |
| **Preparation** | Vials of 30, 60 or 120 mg. Add the whole **1 mL of 5% sodium bicarbonate** (shake until clear, use within 1 h). **IV**: add **5 mL** of saline or 5% dextrose → **10 mg/mL**, give as a slow push over 1–2 (UCG: up to 5) minutes. **IM**: add **2 mL** saline → **20 mg/mL**. **Never water for injection** |
| **Maximum** | No fixed cap; dose by weight |
| **Side effects** | Very well tolerated; dizziness, nausea; **post-artesunate delayed haemolysis (PADH)** 1–3 weeks later (especially with high parasitaemia), rare allergy |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Known artemisinin allergy (rare). Safe in neonates, infants and all trimesters |
| **Monitoring** | Consciousness, fits, **glucose** (malaria itself causes hypoglycaemia), Hb, urine output, parasitaemia at 48–72 h if available; **Hb at 2–3 weeks** (PADH) |
| **Toxicity & antidote** | None needed. PADH: transfuse if severe |

> [!DRUG]
> **Worked example**: a 14 kg child with cerebral malaria. Dose **3 mg/kg × 14 = 42 mg**. Use one 60 mg vial: 1 mL bicarbonate + 5 mL saline = 6 mL at **10 mg/mL**; give **4.2 mL** IV. Repeat at 12 h and 24 h, then daily. If the child weighed 22 kg: **2.4 × 22 = 52.8 mg ≈ 53 mg** (5.3 mL).

> [!EXAM]
> Why **3 mg/kg** in children under 20 kg? Pharmacokinetic studies showed that small children have lower exposure to artesunate and DHA at 2.4 mg/kg, so WHO raised the dose in 2015 to give the same exposure as adults.

### Drug card: Rectal artesunate (RAS)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Artemisinin suppository (100 mg; 50 mg in some kits) |
| **Indications** | **Pre-referral treatment** of suspected severe malaria in children **≤6 years** when injectable treatment is not available (VHT, HC II, HC III without IV) |
| **Dose** | **10 mg/kg single dose.** **UCG bands: 5–<14 kg (4 months to <3 years): 1 × 100 mg; 14–19 kg (3 to <6 years): 2 × 100 mg** |
| **Technique** | Insert blunt end first, hold the buttocks together for **10 minutes**; **repeat the dose if expelled within 30 min** |
| **Key rule** | **RAS is not a treatment, it is a bridge.** The child must be **referred immediately** and receive **injectable artesunate and a full ACT**. In the CARAMAL study (2018–2020), children given RAS without completed referral and follow-on treatment did worse; WHO now stresses complete referral and restricts RAS to pre-referral use only |

### Drug card: Artemether–lumefantrine (AL, "Coartem")

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | ACT: artemether (fast, short-acting) + lumefantrine (slow, long-acting partner) |
| **Mechanism** | Artemether clears most parasites in 3 days; lumefantrine (half-life 3–6 days) kills the rest |
| **Indications (paeds)** | **First-line uncomplicated malaria** in Uganda (all ages, including infants); completion after parenteral treatment |
| **Dose, route, frequency** | Tablet 20/120 mg (dispersible for small children). **6 doses over 3 days: 0 h, 8 h, then twice daily on days 2 and 3.** **5–14 kg: 1 tablet; 15–24 kg: 2 tablets; 25–34 kg: 3 tablets; ≥35 kg: 4 tablets** per dose |
| **Maximum** | 4 tablets per dose |
| **Side effects** | Nausea, vomiting, dizziness, sleep disturbance; QT effect minor |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | First trimester (adolescents: follow current pregnancy policy), severe malaria (needs parenteral treatment), QT-prolonging drugs |
| **Monitoring** | Give with **milk or fatty food** (lumefantrine absorption increases many-fold); **repeat the dose if vomited within 30 min**; return if danger signs |
| **Toxicity & antidote** | None |

> [!UGANDA]
> For infants under 5 kg, the **WHO guidelines for malaria (September 2026 edition)** recommend an ACT at the same mg/kg target as for a 5 kg child and, where AL is used, the **new infant formulation of artemether–lumefantrine (1:12 ratio, approved in 2025 for babies <5 kg)**. UCG 2023 predates it and gives 1 standard tablet as the smallest band. Until the infant formulation is stocked, small or sick infants with malaria should be assessed for severe disease and treated in hospital under senior guidance.
>
> **ACTs and ART (WHO)**: do not use **artesunate–amodiaquine** in children taking **efavirenz or zidovudine** (neutropenia, hepatotoxicity), and do not use **artesunate + SP** in children on **cotrimoxazole**.

## Anticonvulsants

Convulsions in Ugandan children are most often due to **malaria (cerebral or febrile seizures), meningitis, hypoglycaemia, hypocalcaemia (neonates), hypoxic-ischaemic injury, epilepsy and electrolyte problems**. The drug is only part of the treatment: **ABC, glucose, oxygen, recovery position, treat the cause**.

### Convulsions: the step-by-step drug ladder (child >1 month)

1. **0 min**: ABC, recovery position, suction, oxygen; **check glucose** (treat if <2.5 mmol/L, or <3 mmol/L in SAM: 10% dextrose 5 mL/kg). Do not put anything in the mouth.
2. **Fitting ≥5 min (or still fitting on arrival)**: **diazepam 0.5 mg/kg rectally** (or **0.2–0.3 mg/kg IV** slowly over 1–2 min), **max 10 mg**. Alternative: **midazolam 0.3–0.5 mg/kg buccal/intranasal** (max 10 mg) or 0.15–0.2 mg/kg IV/IM.
3. **Still fitting 10 min after the first dose**: **second benzodiazepine dose** (same dose). **Never more than two doses** (respiratory depression).
4. **Still fitting 10 min later (status epilepticus)**: **phenobarbital 15–20 mg/kg IV over 15–20 min (or IM)**, or **phenytoin 15–20 mg/kg IV over 20–60 min in normal saline** (cardiac monitoring), or (where available) **levetiracetam 40 mg/kg IV over 5 min** (APLS/NICE 2022: as effective as phenytoin, fewer side effects).
5. **Still fitting**: second agent (e.g. phenytoin after phenobarbital), call anaesthesia/ICU (thiopentone or midazolam infusion with intubation at national referral).
6. **Throughout**: treat **fever** (paracetamol), **malaria** (artesunate), **meningitis** (ceftriaxone after LP if safe), **hypoglycaemia**; check sodium and calcium.

> [!UGANDA]
> UCG 2023 (severe malaria complications) uses **diazepam 0.5 mg/kg rectally or 0.2 mg/kg IV (max 10 mg)**, then **phenobarbital 10–15 mg/kg loading** with **2.5 mg/kg** maintenance once or twice daily, or **phenytoin 15 mg/kg**. The WHO Pocket Book uses phenobarbital **15 mg/kg** in children and **20 mg/kg** in neonates. Buccal midazolam and IV levetiracetam are standard in UK/US algorithms but are not reliably stocked in Ugandan public hospitals.

> [!PEARL]
> A febrile, convulsing Ugandan child has **cerebral malaria, meningitis or hypoglycaemia until proven otherwise**. Simple febrile seizures (6 months–5 years, generalised, <15 minutes, single in 24 h, full recovery within an hour) need **no anticonvulsant prophylaxis**, only reassurance and fever control (antipyretics do not prevent recurrence).

## Respiratory drugs

The two big respiratory emergencies are **acute asthma/wheeze** and **croup**. Pneumonia is treated with antibiotics and **oxygen** (the most important respiratory "drug": give it when **SpO₂ <90%**, by nasal prongs at 0.5–1 L/min in infants and 1–2 L/min in older children; UCG targets SpO₂ >94% in sick young infants).

### Acute asthma: the drug ladder (GINA 2025, UCG 2023)

1. **Oxygen** to keep SpO₂ **94–98%**; sit the child up.
2. **Salbutamol** inhaler **100 micrograms/puff via a spacer** (with a mask under 4 years): **2–6 puffs (≤5 years) or 4–10 puffs (6–11 years) every 20 min for the first hour**; or **nebulised 2.5 mg (<5 years) / 5 mg (≥5 years)** in 3–4 mL saline, driven by oxygen, every 20 min × 3.
3. **Ipratropium bromide** for severe attacks: **250 micrograms nebulised (≤12 years; 500 micrograms ≥12)** every 20 min for the first hour, mixed with salbutamol.
4. **Systemic corticosteroid** within the first hour: **prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg orally once daily** (max **20 mg under 2 years, 30 mg at 2–5 years, 40 mg at 6–11 years**) for **3–5 days** (UCG: 1 mg/kg, 3 days), or **hydrocortisone 4 mg/kg IV 6-hourly** (max 100 mg) if vomiting or too breathless to swallow, or **dexamethasone 0.3–0.6 mg/kg** once (2 days).
5. **Not improving after 1 hour (severe or life-threatening)**: **MgSO₄ 40 mg/kg IV over 20 min (max 2 g)**; consider **aminophylline 5 mg/kg IV over at least 20 min** (omit if already on theophylline) then 5 mg/kg 6-hourly (WHO) or infusion 1 mg/kg/h; **IV salbutamol 15 micrograms/kg over 10 min** in HDU; call for help (intubation is dangerous in asthma).
6. **Discharge**: inhaler technique with spacer, controller treatment (low-dose ICS; GINA 2025 Track 1 for 6–11 years uses ICS-formoterol as maintenance and reliever at steps 3–4), written action plan, review in 1 week.

> [!UGANDA]
> GINA 2025 discourages SABA-only treatment and promotes ICS-containing relievers (ICS-formoterol) for adolescents and adults, and ICS-formoterol maintenance-and-reliever therapy from 6 years. In Uganda, budesonide-formoterol and beclometasone inhalers are expensive and often unavailable in public facilities, so many children still go home on salbutamol only. At minimum, any child with more than occasional symptoms should leave with a **steroid inhaler and a spacer** (a cut plastic water bottle works when commercial spacers are unavailable).

## Fluids, electrolytes & nutrition

Fluids are drugs. They have a dose (mL/kg), a rate, side effects (overload, hyponatraemia, cerebral oedema) and contraindications (boluses in SAM and severe anaemia). Prescribe them as carefully as an antibiotic.

### Maintenance fluids (UCG 2023)

| Weight | Daily maintenance | Per hour ("4-2-1" rule, Holliday–Segar) |
|---|---|---|
| First 10 kg | **100 mL/kg/day** | 4 mL/kg/h |
| Next 10 kg (11–20 kg) | **+50 mL/kg/day** | +2 mL/kg/h |
| Each kg above 20 | **+25 mL/kg/day** (UCG) (Holliday–Segar: +20) | +1 mL/kg/h |

- **Examples**: 8 kg → 800 mL/day; 15 kg → 1,000 + 250 = **1,250 mL/day**; 30 kg → 1,000 + 500 + 250 = **1,750 mL/day** (UCG) or 1,700 mL (Holliday–Segar).
- Add **10% for each °C of fever** (UCG); give more for ongoing losses (diarrhoea, burns).
- **Give it orally or by NGT whenever possible** (breast milk counts). IV maintenance: **isotonic fluid with 5% dextrose** (e.g. Ringer's lactate or 0.9% saline with 5% dextrose; UCG lists half-normal saline with 5% dextrose). NICE (NG29) and AAP 2018 recommend **isotonic** maintenance fluids to prevent **hospital-acquired hyponatraemia**; sick children (meningitis, pneumonia, post-op) secrete ADH and should often get **two-thirds to three-quarters of maintenance**.
- **Meningitis, cerebral malaria, severe pneumonia**: restrict to **~2/3 to 3/4 of maintenance** unless dehydrated or shocked.

### Neonatal fluids (UCG 2023)

| Day | Total fluids (oral + IV) | Fluid if IV |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | **60 mL/kg/day** | **10% dextrose** |
| Day 2 | 90 mL/kg/day | 10% dextrose |
| Day 3 | 120 mL/kg/day | Half-normal saline + 5% dextrose (or 10% dextrose with electrolytes) |
| Day 4 onwards | **150 mL/kg/day** | When fully fed, up to 180 mL/kg/day |

If only IV fluids are given, **do not exceed 100 mL/kg/day** unless dehydrated, under a radiant heater or on phototherapy (more insensible loss). Reduce if facial oedema develops.

### Shock: fluid boluses (child without SAM or severe anaemia)

1. **Ringer's lactate or 0.9% saline 20 mL/kg as rapidly as possible** (UCG 2023), through an IV or **intraosseous** line.
2. Reassess (pulse, capillary refill, consciousness, urine, **liver size and crackles**). If no improvement, **repeat 10–20 mL/kg** (UCG: up to 3 times, max 60 mL/kg); give **blood 20 mL/kg** if bleeding or severe anaemia.
3. Still shocked → septic shock: further fluids with great caution, **adrenaline or dopamine** infusion where available; ICU.
4. Improving: continue rehydration (for diarrhoea, **70 mL/kg over 5 h in infants, 2.5 h in children**).

> [!UGANDA]
> The **FEAST trial** (2011, Uganda, Kenya and Tanzania; children with severe febrile illness and impaired perfusion) found that **fluid boluses increased mortality** compared with maintenance fluid alone. WHO's **2016 ETAT update** therefore recommends **10–20 mL/kg of isotonic crystalloid over 30–60 minutes** for children with shock (all of: cold hands, capillary refill >3 s, weak fast pulse), with careful reassessment, and **no boluses for children with impaired perfusion who are not in shock**. UCG 2023 still says "20 mL/kg as rapidly as possible". On the ward: give the bolus to a child who is truly shocked, but **reassess after every 10 mL/kg**, stop at the first sign of overload, and remember that **severe anaemia needs blood**, not saline.

### Shock in a child with severe acute malnutrition

- **15 mL/kg over 1 hour** of **Ringer's lactate with 5% dextrose**, **half-strength Darrow's with 5% dextrose**, or **0.45% saline with 5% dextrose** (UCG 2023; WHO).
- Monitor **pulse and RR every 5–10 minutes**. If improving, **repeat 15 mL/kg over 1 h**, then switch to **oral/NGT ReSoMal 10 mL/kg/h for up to 10 h** and start F-75.
- If no improvement after the first hour: **septic shock**: maintenance IV fluid (4 mL/kg/h), **transfuse 10 mL/kg fresh whole blood slowly over 3 h**, IV antibiotics, start feeding.
- **Stop at once** if the pulse rises by **≥25/min** or RR by **≥5/min** (heart failure).

### Dehydration: Plans A, B and C (UCG 2023)

| Plan | Who | What |
|---|---|---|
| **A** (no dehydration) | Diarrhoea, no signs | Home: **extra fluids** (ORS **50–100 mL after each loose stool under 2 years, 100–200 mL at 2–5 years**), **continue feeding**, **zinc**, **when to return**. Give 2 packets of ORS |
| **B** (some dehydration: 2 of restless/irritable, sunken eyes, drinks eagerly, skin pinch slow) | | **ORS 75 mL/kg over 4 h** in the clinic (<6 kg: 200–400 mL; 6–9.9 kg: 400–700; 10–11.9 kg: 700–900; 12–19 kg: 900–1,400), then reassess |
| **C** (severe: 2 of lethargic/unconscious, sunken eyes, drinks poorly/unable, skin pinch very slow >2 s) | | **Ringer's lactate 100 mL/kg IV**: **<12 months: 30 mL/kg in 1 h, then 70 mL/kg in 5 h; ≥12 months: 30 mL/kg in 30 min, then 70 mL/kg in 2.5 h.** Repeat the first part if radial pulse still weak. Add ORS **5 mL/kg/h** as soon as the child can drink. No IV possible: **ORS 20 mL/kg/h by NGT for 6 h** (120 mL/kg) |

> [!REDFLAG]
> **Plan C is not for children with SAM.** A malnourished child with diarrhoea gets **ReSoMal slowly** (or the SAM shock protocol), because the standard Plan C volume can tip them into heart failure.

**Other SAM medicines (WHO / UCG)**

- **Antibiotics for all inpatients** (see Antimicrobials). **Hypoglycaemia <3 mmol/L**: 50 mL of 10% glucose/sucrose orally or by NGT, or **10% dextrose 5 mL/kg IV**, then feed 2-hourly.
- **No iron in the stabilisation phase** (free iron feeds bacteria and causes oxidative damage). Start iron in rehabilitation if on F-100; **RUTF already contains iron**.
- **Vitamin A**: only if **eye signs** of deficiency or **recent measles** (F-75, F-100 and RUTF contain vitamin A): days 1, 2 and 15.
- **Folic acid** and **zinc/copper** come in the mineral mix of F-75/F-100.
- **Measles vaccine** if unvaccinated; **albendazole** in rehabilitation (≥1 year); treat malaria and test for HIV and TB.

### Blood transfusion (the most life-saving "fluid" in Uganda)

- **Indications (WHO/UCG)**: **Hb <4 g/dL**, or **Hb 4–6 g/dL with** respiratory distress, impaired consciousness, shock, heart failure, very high parasitaemia or ongoing loss.
- **Dose**: **whole blood 20 mL/kg** or **packed cells 10 mL/kg over 3–4 h**. **SAM: 10 mL/kg over 3 h**. The **TRACT trial (2019; Uganda and Malawi)** found that **30 mL/kg whole blood** improved survival in afebrile children compared with 20 mL/kg (but not in febrile children), and that children with uncomplicated Hb 4–6 g/dL could be safely observed without immediate transfusion.
- **Furosemide 1 mg/kg IV** only if signs of fluid overload (not routinely).
- Check vital signs at the start, after 15 min and every 30 min; stop if fever, rash, breathlessness or dark urine (transfusion reaction).

## Analgesia & antipyretics

Children feel pain as much as adults, including newborns, and are **under-treated** everywhere. Use the WHO **two-step ladder** for children (WHO 2012 persisting pain guideline; UCG 2023): **step 1 paracetamol ± ibuprofen**, **step 2 morphine**. **Codeine and tramadol are not used in children** (unpredictable metabolism: CYP2D6 ultra-rapid metabolisers have died of respiratory depression).

### Aspirin: avoid in children

- **Avoid aspirin under 16 years** because of **Reye's syndrome** (acute encephalopathy + fatty liver after viral illness, especially influenza and varicella).
- Exceptions under specialist care: **Kawasaki disease** (high dose in the acute phase, then **3–5 mg/kg once daily** as an antiplatelet for 6–8 weeks), **rheumatic fever arthritis/carditis** (anti-inflammatory doses, or naproxen), and antiplatelet use after cardiac surgery.
- Many Ugandan homes keep adult aspirin: ask, and counsel against it.

## Emergency & resuscitation drugs

Keep a **weight-based emergency drug chart** at every bedside on the acute ward, and the **paediatric strengths** ready: **1:10,000 adrenaline, 10% dextrose, 10% calcium gluconate, 4.2% bicarbonate for infants**.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Adrenaline 1:1000 (1 mg/mL)** | **Anaphylaxis** | **0.01 mL/kg (10 mcg/kg), max 0.5 mL**: **<6 years 0.15 mL; 6–12 years 0.3 mL; >12 years 0.5 mL** | **IM, anterolateral thigh** | Repeat every **5 min** if no improvement |

| **Adrenaline 1:1000** | **Croup** (severe) | **0.4–0.5 mL/kg, max 5 mL** | Nebulised | Observe ≥2–4 h after |

> [!DRUG]
> **Two adrenaline strengths, two routes, two doses.** Anaphylaxis = **1:1000, IM, 0.01 mL/kg**. Cardiac arrest = **1:10,000, IV/IO, 0.1 mL/kg**. Both deliver **10 micrograms/kg**. Giving 1:1000 IV at 0.1 mL/kg is a **tenfold overdose** that can cause fatal arrhythmia. To make 1:10,000: **1 mL of 1:1000 + 9 mL of saline**.

### Paediatric resuscitation in brief (ETAT; ERC/AHA paediatric life support, 2025 updates)

1. Unresponsive and not breathing normally: **5 rescue breaths**, check for signs of life, then **CPR 15 compressions : 2 breaths** (two rescuers), rate **100–120/min**, depth one-third of chest (≈4 cm infant, 5 cm child).
2. **Attach monitor/defibrillator**. Non-shockable (asystole, PEA: most paediatric arrests, usually after hypoxia or shock): **adrenaline 10 mcg/kg as soon as IV/IO access is obtained**, then every 3–5 min. Shockable (VF/pVT, rare): **4 J/kg**, adrenaline and amiodarone after the 3rd shock.
3. **Reversible causes (4 Hs and 4 Ts)**: **hypoxia**, **hypovolaemia**, **hypo/hyperkalaemia, hypoglycaemia, hypocalcaemia**, **hypothermia**; tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins, thrombosis.
4. **Intraosseous access** (proximal tibia, 1–2 cm below the tibial tuberosity, medial flat surface) if no IV in 90 seconds: every resuscitation drug and fluid can go through it.

> **Drugs to avoid in neonates**: **chloramphenicol** in the first week (grey baby), **ceftriaxone** if jaundiced or with calcium, **sulphonamides/cotrimoxazole** in the first weeks (kernicterus), **diazepam** for seizures (use phenobarbital), **codeine**, **aspirin**, **benzyl alcohol-preserved injections** (gasping syndrome), **alcohol and iodine** over large skin areas, **topical steroids** over big areas.

## TB & HIV drugs

Both TB and HIV drugs are given by **weight band** using **child-friendly dispersible fixed-dose combinations (FDCs)**. Reweigh at every visit and move the child up a band as they grow: **under-dosing as children grow** is a classic cause of treatment failure.

> - **Nevirapine**: avoid with rifampicin.
> - **EFV**: can be used with rifampicin without dose change (children >3 years).

## Poisoning antidotes

**General management of a poisoned child (UCG 2023 / WHO Pocket Book)**: ABC, glucose, oxygen; **identify the poison** (bring the container); **do not induce vomiting**; **activated charcoal 0.5–1 g/kg (max 50 g)** orally or by NGT **within 1 hour** for adsorbable poisons (not for kerosene, caustics, iron, alcohols, lithium); gastric lavage only for life-threatening ingestions within 1 hour with a protected airway (rarely); remove contaminated clothes and wash skin (organophosphates); observe for at least 6 h; **assess for neglect or deliberate poisoning** in every case.

|---|---|---|---|
| **Paracetamol** | **Acetylcysteine (NAC)** IV | **150 mg/kg over 15–60 min, then 50 mg/kg over 4 h, then 100 mg/kg over 16 h** (WHO Pocket Book for <20 kg: in **3, 7 and 14 mL/kg** of 5% dextrose) | Toxic dose **>150 mg/kg** (UCG: >200 mg/kg in children <6 years); best **within 8 h**, still useful later. ## Management of drug emergencies & toxicity

These are the drug emergencies you must recognise and start treating on the paediatric ward before the senior arrives.

### 1. Anaphylaxis (ceftriaxone, penicillins, blood, vaccines, peanuts in RUTF, insect stings)

1. **Call for help**; stop the trigger (stop the infusion, keep the line).
2. **Adrenaline 1:1000 IM into the anterolateral thigh**: **0.01 mL/kg** (**<6 years 0.15 mL; 6–12 years 0.3 mL; >12 years 0.5 mL**). **Repeat every 5 min** if no improvement.
3. **Lie the child flat** (legs raised) or sitting if breathless; **high-flow oxygen**; airway support.
4. **IV/IO access: 0.9% saline 20 mL/kg** for hypotension or poor perfusion (repeat).
5. Wheeze: salbutamol nebuliser. Stridor: nebulised adrenaline.
6. Adjuncts after stabilisation: chlorphenamine for itch, hydrocortisone (doses in the emergency table). Observe **at least 6–12 h** (biphasic reactions). Record the allergy and tell the family.

### 2. Respiratory depression after benzodiazepines, phenobarbital or opioids

1. Airway (head tilt, jaw thrust, suction), **oxygen**, **bag-valve-mask ventilation** if RR is low or SpO₂ falling.
2. **Opioid**: **naloxone** (0.01 then 0.1 mg/kg). **Benzodiazepine**: support ventilation; flumazenil only in specific cases (not in a child whose seizures may return).
3. Review the chart: **how many doses** of diazepam (max two)? Phenobarbital after two diazepam doses? Did the child also get morphine?
4. Do not give the next sedative dose until the child is reassessed.

### 3. Hypoglycaemia during treatment (quinine, sepsis, SAM, fasting, insulin)

1. Check glucose; **10% dextrose 5 mL/kg IV** (2 mL/kg in neonates) or sugar water by NGT if no IV.
2. Recheck in 30 min; continue a dextrose-containing fluid and **feeds**.
3. On quinine: check glucose **4-hourly**; dilute quinine in **dextrose**.

### 4. Fluid overload (after boluses, transfusion or excessive maintenance)

**Signs**: rising RR and HR, new **crackles**, **enlarging liver**, gallop rhythm, raised JVP (older children), periorbital/facial oedema, falling SpO₂. **Children with SAM, severe anaemia and severe malaria are most at risk.**
1. **Stop the fluid** (or slow the blood), sit the child up, oxygen.
2. **Furosemide 1 mg/kg IV** (repeat if needed).
3. Recalculate the fluid plan; weigh daily.

### 5. Gentamicin toxicity

**Rising creatinine, oliguria, or a child who becomes unsteady or deaf.** Stop gentamicin, switch to a non-nephrotoxic antibiotic (ceftriaxone), give appropriate fluids, check potassium; arrange a hearing assessment later. Prevent it: once-daily dosing, correct weight, **no gentamicin in an anuric child**, short courses.

### 6. Extravasation injury (dextrose, calcium, phenytoin, potassium, bicarbonate)

Stop the infusion, aspirate what you can through the cannula, remove it, elevate the limb, mark the area and review; plastic surgery referral for blistering or necrosis. Check cannula sites **hourly** during irritant infusions.

### 7. Stevens–Johnson syndrome / toxic epidermal necrolysis (cotrimoxazole, NVP, phenobarbital, carbamazepine, phenytoin, sulphonamides)

Fever, sore mouth and eyes, then target lesions, blisters and skin peeling with **two or more mucosal surfaces**. **Stop the drug immediately and permanently**, admit, fluids (burns-type care), analgesia, eye care (ophthalmology), mouth care, nutrition, infection surveillance. Record and counsel: the drug (and its class) must never be given again.

### 8. Acute dystonic reaction (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, haloperidol)

Oculogyric crisis, torticollis, trismus, tongue protrusion. Stop the drug; **diphenhydramine 1 mg/kg IV** or **benztropine/procyclidine** (specialist doses); **diazepam** as an alternative. Avoid metoclopramide as a routine antiemetic in children; use **ondansetron 0.15 mg/kg** (max 4–8 mg) where an antiemetic is genuinely needed.

### 9. Post-artesunate delayed haemolysis (PADH)

New pallor, dark urine, jaundice **7–21 days after IV artesunate** (especially with high parasitaemia). Check Hb; transfuse if severe; it is not a reason to avoid artesunate. Ask children to return for an **Hb check 2–3 weeks** after severe malaria (they also come back for PDMC).

### 10. Paracetamol overdose (accidental or repeated supratherapeutic)

1. Calculate **mg/kg ingested** (all doses over 24 h).
2. Single ingestion ≥150 mg/kg (≥200 mg/kg in <6 years per UCG), or **repeated doses >75–90 mg/kg/day** with symptoms or abnormal LFTs, or unknown amount: **start acetylcysteine** (do not wait for levels you cannot get).
3. Check **glucose, LFTs, INR, creatinine** at baseline and at the end of the infusion.
4. NAC anaphylactoid reactions (flushing, wheeze) are rate-related: pause, give chlorphenamine, restart more slowly.

### 11. Organophosphate poisoning (a Ugandan classic: agricultural sprays, "dudu" killers)

**SLUDGE/DUMBELS**: salivation, lacrimation, urination, diarrhoea, GI cramps, emesis; bradycardia, **bronchorrhoea and bronchospasm**, miosis, then muscle fasciculations, weakness and respiratory failure.
1. Protect staff (gloves), **remove clothes and wash the skin**.
2. ABC, suction, oxygen.
3. **Atropine 0.02–0.05 mg/kg IV**, **double the dose every 5 min until the chest is clear**, then infusion. Expect to need large total doses.
4. **Pralidoxime 25–50 mg/kg IV over 30 min** within 24 h, then infusion.
5. Diazepam for fits; ventilation if weak.

### 12. Kerosene ingestion

**Do not make the child vomit**, no lavage, no charcoal. Oxygen if breathless; observe at least **6 hours**; CXR if symptomatic; antibiotics only for secondary bacterial pneumonia. Teach: store paraffin in labelled containers out of reach, never in soda bottles.

## Ward-round & exam pearls

**Model presentation (drug-focused):** "This is Baby Okello, a **2-year-old boy weighing 10.5 kg** (weight-for-height Z-score −1.5, MUAC 13.2 cm, no oedema), fully immunised for age by card, HIV-negative mother, still breastfeeding plus family foods. He was admitted yesterday with **severe malaria (prostration, two convulsions) and Hb 6.9 g/dL**. He received **rectal diazepam 5 mg** at the HC III and **one dose of IV artesunate 32 mg (3 mg/kg)** here at 22:00; the 12-hour dose is due at 10:00. **Glucose 5.1 mmol/L** this morning. No further fits. He is on **maintenance fluids at 1,025 mL/day** (100 × 10 + 50 × 0.5), mostly breast milk and oral fluids, and **paracetamol 160 mg 6-hourly**. **Plan**: complete artesunate to 24 h then **AL 1 tablet BD × 3 days** once drinking (or DP per UCG at discharge), Hb on day 3 and at 2–3 weeks, PDMC, mosquito net counselling."

**Viva questions with answers**

1. *"How do you estimate the weight of a 4-year-old in resuscitation?"* (2 × 4) + 8 = **16 kg** (APLS), or (4 + 4) × 2 = 16 kg; then weigh as soon as possible.
2. *"Why are neonates at risk of kernicterus with ceftriaxone and cotrimoxazole?"* Both displace unconjugated bilirubin from albumin; free bilirubin crosses the immature blood–brain barrier.
3. *"What is grey baby syndrome?"* Chloramphenicol toxicity in neonates (immature glucuronidation): vomiting, abdominal distension, grey cyanosis, hypothermia, cardiovascular collapse.
4. *"Why does a 2-year-old need a relatively high mg/kg dose of many drugs?"* Higher hepatic clearance per kg (bigger liver relative to body size and higher metabolic rate).
5. *"How do you give IV artesunate to a 12 kg child?"* 3 mg/kg = 36 mg; 60 mg vial + 1 mL bicarbonate + 5 mL saline = 10 mg/mL; give 3.6 mL IV slowly at 0, 12, 24 h then daily, then a full ACT.
6. *"Why not boluses in SAM?"* Their hearts are small and weak (cardiac muscle atrophy), sodium is retained and potassium depleted: standard boluses cause heart failure. Use 15 mL/kg over 1 h of a dextrose-containing fluid and monitor every 5–10 min.
7. *"Why no codeine in children?"* Variable CYP2D6 metabolism to morphine: ultra-rapid metabolisers can stop breathing; poor metabolisers get no analgesia.
8. *"What drug do you give a neonate who is fitting?"* Phenobarbital 20 mg/kg IV/IM (after checking glucose and correcting hypoglycaemia), further 10 mg/kg doses to 40 mg/kg.
9. *"How do you make 1:10,000 adrenaline?"* 1 mL of 1:1000 + 9 mL normal saline.
10. *"What is the first-line treatment of uncomplicated malaria in a 9 kg child?"* AL 1 tablet (20/120 mg) at 0 and 8 h, then twice daily on days 2 and 3 (6 doses), with milk or food.

**OSCE tips**

- In a **prescribing station**: write the **weight**, the **mg/kg**, the **calculated dose in mg**, the **volume in mL** with the **strength**, route, frequency, duration, maximum and your signature. Say your calculation out loud.
- In an **ORS/counselling station**: demonstrate mixing **1 sachet in 1 litre**, frequent small sips, the 4 rules (extra fluid, zinc, continue feeding, when to return) and the danger signs.
- In a **"drug error" station**: identify the error (decimal point, wrong strength, adult ampoule), stop the harm, treat the child, tell the senior and the family honestly, and report it (incident form).

## Mnemonics & memory aids

- **Weight**: "**Double at five, triple at one, quadruple at two**" (months, year, years) for birth weight; resuscitation weight "**(age + 4) × 2**".
- **Gentamicin**: "**3-5-7.5**": 3 mg/kg LBW neonates in week 1, 5 mg/kg term neonates in week 1, 7.5 mg/kg for everyone older, all **once daily**.
- **Ampicillin**: "**50 for the fifty**": 50 mg/kg every 6 h (child), 12 h (week 1 neonate), 8 h (weeks 2–4).
- **Artesunate**: "**3 under 20**": 3 mg/kg if under 20 kg, 2.4 mg/kg if 20 kg or more; "**0-12-24 then daily**".
- **Seizure ladder "D-D-P-P"**: **D**iazepam, **D**iazepam (10 min later), **P**henobarbital, **P**henytoin; glucose at every step.
- **Fluids "100-50-25"**: maintenance per day (UCG).
- **Plan C "30-70"**: 30 mL/kg then 70 mL/kg: "**1 then 5**" hours for infants, "**half then 2½**" for children.
- **Adrenaline "1000 IM, 10,000 IV"**: the higher number (more dilute) is for the vein.
- **Never in children "CCC-AT"**: **C**hloramphenicol (neonates), **C**eftriaxone + calcium (neonates), **C**odeine; **A**spirin; **T**etracyclines (<8 years).
- **Kerosene: "Don't Empty, Don't Wash, Don't Charcoal."**
- **Organophosphate: "DUMBELS"** and treat with "**A-P**": **A**tropine (double until dry) + **P**ralidoxime.

### Classic exam traps

- **Trap:** "Give 50% dextrose 1 mL/kg IV to a hypoglycaemic child." **Right:** 10% dextrose 5 mL/kg (dilute 50% one part to four parts water).
- **Trap:** "Use 1:1000 adrenaline 0.1 mL/kg IV in cardiac arrest." **Right:** 1:10,000 at 0.1 mL/kg (1:1000 at that volume is a tenfold overdose).
- **Trap:** "Ceftriaxone is the best antibiotic for a jaundiced 3-day-old with sepsis, run with Ringer's lactate." **Right:** ampicillin + gentamicin (or cefotaxime); never ceftriaxone with calcium-containing fluids in neonates.
- **Trap:** "Give 20 mL/kg saline boluses to a child with SAM in shock" or "to a child with Hb 3 g/dL." **Right:** SAM: 15 mL/kg over 1 h of a dextrose-containing fluid; severe anaemia: transfuse.
- **Trap:** "Give diazepam to a fitting neonate." **Right:** phenobarbital 20 mg/kg (and check glucose).
- **Trap:** "A third dose of diazepam because the child is still fitting." **Right:** maximum two benzodiazepine doses, then phenobarbital or phenytoin.
- **Trap:** "Rectal artesunate given, now send the child home with AL." **Right:** rectal artesunate is pre-referral only: refer urgently for injectable artesunate, then a full ACT.
- **Trap:** "Codeine syrup for post-tonsillectomy pain in a 6-year-old." **Right:** paracetamol ± ibuprofen; morphine if severe.

### Questions seniors ask

1. **"What does this child weigh, and what dose is that?"** Always know the weight and do the sum aloud: "13 kg, gentamicin 7.5 mg/kg = 97.5 mg, so about 98 mg IV once daily."
2. **"Why once-daily gentamicin?"** Concentration-dependent killing and post-antibiotic effect; a drug-free trough reduces nephrotoxicity; fewer missed doses.
3. **"Why 3 mg/kg artesunate in small children?"** Under-20 kg children had lower drug exposure at 2.4 mg/kg; WHO raised the dose to match adult exposure.
4. **"The child had two doses of diazepam and is still fitting. What next?"** Phenobarbital 15–20 mg/kg IV over 15–20 min (or phenytoin 15–20 mg/kg in saline over ≥20 min), airway support, glucose, treat the cause.
5. **"Why no aspirin?"** Reye's syndrome (encephalopathy and liver failure after viral infections); use paracetamol or ibuprofen.
6. **"How much maintenance fluid for a 25 kg child?"** UCG: 1,000 + 500 + (5 × 25) = **1,625 mL/day** (Holliday–Segar 1,600 mL/day), about 68 mL/h; give two-thirds if meningitis or severe pneumonia.
7. **"What would you check before giving potassium?"** That the child is passing urine and the serum potassium is not high; never as a bolus; ≤40 mmol/L peripherally.
8. **"What is the antidote to organophosphates and how do you know you've given enough?"** Atropine, doubling every 5 min until the chest is clear of secretions, heart rate >80, BP normal and axillae dry (not by pupil size); plus pralidoxime.
