## In a nutshell

- **Obstructed labour** happens when the presenting part cannot advance through the birth canal **despite adequate uterine contractions**, because the passage is too small for the passenger, or the passenger is presenting the wrong diameter.
- It is one of the "big five" killers in Uganda. It leads directly to **uterine rupture, PPH, puerperal sepsis, obstetric fistula** and **stillbirth or birth asphyxia**. It is also almost entirely **preventable** with good labour monitoring (the partograph or WHO Labour Care Guide) and timely caesarean section.
- The mechanical causes are the "3 Ps": the **Passage** (a contracted pelvis from childhood malnutrition, adolescence or rickets, or tumours), the **Passenger** (macrosomia, hydrocephalus, **malposition**, **malpresentation**, locked twins) and the **Powers**, which are usually *strong* in obstruction. That last point is what separates obstruction from simple slow labour caused by poor contractions.
- **Malposition** means the vertex presents, but the occiput is not anterior (OP or transverse). **Malpresentation** means anything other than the vertex presents (breech, face, brow, shoulder, compound).
- **Never forget**: a woman who has laboured long with a **hot, dry, tachycardic body, a tender lower segment, a rising Bandl's ring and bloody urine** is about to rupture her uterus. She needs **fluids, a catheter, antibiotics and theatre now**. She must **never** get oxytocin.

## Definitions & classification

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Prolonged labour** | Active first stage lasting longer than expected. On the WHO Labour Care Guide (LCG) an alert is triggered when the cervix does not progress within the time limits for each centimetre from 5 cm. The classic partograph uses the **alert line** (1 cm/hour) and the **action line** 4 hours to its right. Total labour > 24 hours is a traditional cut-off. |
| **Obstructed labour** | Failure of descent of the presenting part **despite good contractions**, because of mechanical disproportion between the fetus and the birth canal. |
| **Cephalopelvic disproportion (CPD)** | The fetal head is too big for that pelvis (absolute), or presents a diameter that is too big (relative, e.g. deflexed OP or brow). |
| **Malposition** | The vertex presents but the denominator (the occiput) is **not anterior**: occipito-posterior (OP) or occipito-transverse (OT). |
| **Deep transverse arrest (DTA)** | The head is in OT position **at or below the ischial spines** with a fully dilated cervix, and it fails to rotate or descend despite good contractions. |
| **Malpresentation** | A presenting part other than the vertex: **breech, face, brow, shoulder (transverse or oblique lie) or compound**. |
| **Cord presentation / prolapse** | The cord lies below the presenting part with **intact** membranes (presentation) or **ruptured** membranes (prolapse, which can be occult or overt). |
| **Shoulder dystocia** | A vaginal cephalic birth that needs **additional manoeuvres** to deliver the shoulders after routine gentle traction has failed (RCOG GTG 42). |

### Grading moulding and caput (used on the partograph and LCG)

| Grade | Moulding (skull bones at the sutures) | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Bones separated, sutures felt easily | Normal |
| 1+ | Bones **just touching** | Normal adaptation |
| 2+ | Bones **overlapping but reducible** with gentle pressure | Watch closely |
| 3+ | Bones **overlapping and NOT reducible** | **Disproportion or obstruction** |

Caput (scalp oedema) is graded 1+ to 3+ in the same way. Big caput **plus** 3+ moulding **plus** no descent means CPD until proven otherwise. Remember that caput can make the head *feel* lower than it is, so **always pair the vaginal station with the abdominal fifths palpable**.

## Epidemiology & Uganda context

- Obstructed labour complicates an estimated **~4–5% of births worldwide** and causes about **8% of maternal deaths**. Ugandan hospital studies report a much higher proportion of admissions (around **10%**) because referral hospitals receive the neglected cases.
- At Mulago, obstructed labour and its complications (rupture, sepsis, PPH) accounted for about **a quarter of maternal deaths** in an audit. Obstructed labour caused about **73% of uterine ruptures** in Ugandan series, and uterine rupture alone caused roughly **1 in 10 maternal deaths** at the national referral hospital.
- **Risk factors in Uganda** include adolescent pregnancy (a small, immature pelvis), short maternal stature (less than about 150 cm), childhood malnutrition, primigravidity, macrosomia (diabetes, post-dates), malpresentation, **delay in reaching care** (the three delays: deciding to seek care, reaching a facility, receiving care there), long labour at home or with traditional birth attendants, and HC IIIs without theatre capacity.
- The Mulago criteria-based audit set **six standards** for every woman with obstructed labour: **IV fluids, IV antibiotics, monitoring of maternal vital signs, bladder catheterisation, blood grouping and cross-matching, and delivery within 2 hours of diagnosis**.

> [!UGANDA]
> The Ministry of Health is replacing the classic partograph with the **WHO Labour Care Guide**. Whichever tool your ward uses, the principle is the same: **plot, recognise deviation early, act**. At an **HC III** (no theatre), recognising prolonged labour means **referring early**, not "trying a little oxytocin". At an **HC IV or hospital** it means a decision for CS. Most obstructed labours seen at Mengo or Mulago are late referrals, and the delay happened before the woman arrived.

- **Breech** occurs in about **20% of pregnancies at 28 weeks** but only **3–4% at term**, because most babies turn spontaneously.
- **Occipito-posterior** position occurs in 10–20% of labours at onset, but most rotate anteriorly. Only about **5% persist as OP** at delivery.
- **Face** presentation occurs in about 1 in 500 births and **brow** in about 1 in 1,000–1,500. **Transverse lie** at term occurs in about 1 in 300, and is much commoner in grand multiparas.
- **Cord prolapse** occurs in about 0.1–0.6% of births. **Shoulder dystocia** occurs in about 0.6–0.7% of vaginal cephalic births (RCOG).

## Pathophysiology

### Why obstruction destroys the uterus

1. Contractions continue against an immovable block. The **upper segment** keeps contracting and **retracting** (its fibres get shorter and thicker with each contraction), while the passive **lower segment** is stretched thinner and thinner.
2. The junction between the thick upper segment and the thin lower segment rises and becomes visible or palpable as a transverse groove: the **pathological retraction ring (Bandl's ring)**. It can rise towards the umbilicus. **Bandl's ring means imminent rupture.**
3. Eventually the thinned lower segment tears. This is **uterine rupture**: fetal death, massive haemorrhage into the peritoneum, and maternal shock.
4. In multiparas, the uterus may instead go into **exhaustion (secondary inertia)**, so contractions stop. This is not "improvement". In primigravidas the uterus rarely ruptures and tends to become **tonically contracted**.

### Why obstruction causes fistula

The impacted head **compresses the bladder base, urethra, vagina and rectum against the pubic symphysis and sacrum** for hours. This causes **ischaemic pressure necrosis**. The dead tissue sloughs 3–10 days later and leaves a **vesicovaginal (VVF) or rectovaginal (RVF) fistula**. Haematuria and an oedematous anterior lip of the cervix during labour are early warnings. The same ischaemia damages the pelvic nerves (**foot drop**, from lumbosacral plexus or common peroneal nerve injury), the pubic bones and the vaginal walls (which later scar and cause **vaginal stenosis**). Together these make up the "obstructed labour injury complex".

### Why obstruction kills the baby

Strong contractions without progress cause repeated reductions in placental blood flow, which leads to **hypoxia, acidosis and fetal distress**, and then **intrapartum stillbirth**. Prolonged rupture of membranes adds **ascending infection** (chorioamnionitis, neonatal sepsis).

### Why the mother becomes septic, dehydrated and ketotic

Long labour without food or fluids leads to **dehydration and ketoacidosis** (a dry tongue, tachycardia, ketonuria, oliguria). Repeated vaginal examinations and long-ruptured membranes lead to **ascending infection** (fever, foul liquor). A tachycardic, febrile, dehydrated woman is a poor surgical candidate. That is why you **resuscitate before and during** the move to theatre.

### Why malposition and malpresentation obstruct: the diameters

```
Presenting diameter by attitude of the head (term fetus)
  Well-flexed vertex (OA) .... suboccipitobregmatic   9.5 cm   -> fits easily
  Deflexed vertex (OP) ........ occipitofrontal       11.5 cm  -> slow, may obstruct
  Brow ........................ mentovertical         13.5 cm  -> will NOT fit
  Face (fully extended) ....... submentobregmatic     9.5 cm   -> fits IF mentoanterior
Average gynaecoid pelvic inlet (transverse) ~13 cm, AP ~11 cm
```

- **OP position**: the head tends to be **deflexed**, so it presents the larger occipitofrontal diameter. The occiput has to rotate through 135 degrees (the "long arc") instead of 45. Labour is long, backache is severe, and the woman gets an urge to push early.
- **Deep transverse arrest**: the head descends in OT but fails to rotate at the level of the spines, often because of an android (heart-shaped) pelvis with prominent spines or poor contractions.
- **Face**: the neck is fully extended. **Mentoanterior** face can deliver because the chin rotates under the symphysis and the head is then born by **flexion**. In **mentoposterior** face the neck is already maximally extended and cannot extend further to negotiate the sacrum, so it is **undeliverable vaginally at term**.
- **Brow**: halfway between flexion and extension, it presents the **largest diameter (13.5 cm)**. At term it obstructs unless it flexes to a vertex or extends to a face.
- **Shoulder presentation**: no fetal part can engage, so it is **always obstructed** if left alone. This is **"neglected shoulder presentation"**, often with a **prolapsed arm**, and it causes rupture, especially in grand multiparas.
- **Breech**: the body (the smaller part) is born first. The **after-coming head** has **no time to mould** and can become trapped, especially in a preterm baby whose body can pass through an incompletely dilated cervix.

### Why malpresentations happen

Anything that lets the baby move freely, or stops the head settling into the pelvis, can cause a malpresentation:

- **Lax uterus**: grand multiparity.
- **Excess room**: polyhydramnios, or a preterm baby.
- **Something blocking the pelvis**: placenta praevia, fibroids, a contracted pelvis, ovarian tumours.
- **Abnormal shape**: uterine anomalies (bicornuate, septate) or twins.
- **Fetal factors**: anencephaly, hydrocephalus, neck masses (these cause face presentation), neuromuscular problems and fetal death.

> [!PEARL]
> When you find a malpresentation, ask **"why?"** Scan for **placenta praevia** before any vaginal examination or ECV, and look for fetal anomalies, polyhydramnios, fibroids or a uterine anomaly.

## Clinical acumen

### Focused history

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Parity, age, height | Primigravida or adolescent: small pelvis, uterus tends to be tonic. Grand multipara: uterus prone to **rupture** and to malpresentation. |
| **When did labour start? When did the membranes rupture?** | The duration shows how long the bladder has been compressed (fistula risk) and the infection risk (prolonged rupture > 18 hours). |
| Where has she been labouring (home, TBA, HC II/III)? Was she given any "medicine to push" (herbal oxytocics) or oxytocin? | **Herbal or unsupervised uterotonics** plus obstruction is a common route to rupture in Uganda. |
| Has she passed urine? Is there blood in the urine? | **Haematuria** means bladder compression and is a fistula warning. **Anuria** points to dehydration or rupture. |
| Is she still feeling fetal movements? | Loss suggests fetal death, which changes the delivery plan. |
| Previous CS or uterine surgery | A **scarred uterus** plus obstruction means very high rupture risk. |
| Previous births: weights, prolonged labour, instrumental delivery, stillbirths | These predict CPD. The **biggest previous baby** tests the pelvis. |
| Sudden severe pain, then contractions stopped | **Uterine rupture**, a classic history. |
| Fever, foul-smelling liquor | Chorioamnionitis. |
| Antenatal care: scan (lie, placenta), height, HIV status, blood group, Hb | Placenta praevia, known breech, anaemia (poor tolerance of haemorrhage), PMTCT plans for the baby. |

### Examination

**General examination** (look for exhaustion and sepsis):
- Is she anxious, restless and exhausted?
- **Dehydration**: dry tongue, sunken eyes.
- **Tachycardia** (over 100/min), **fever**, hypotension, pallor.
- Ketotic breath.

**Abdomen**:
- **Lie and presentation.**
- **Fifths of the head palpable** above the brim: 3/5 or more palpable in the second stage means the head is not engaged.
- **Bandl's ring**: a groove running obliquely across the abdomen.
- **Tender, stretched lower segment.**
- **Distended bladder**, which may be displaced upwards.
- Contractions that are strong, frequent, or **tonic** (the uterus never relaxes).
- **Fetal heart**: absent, bradycardic or tachycardic.
- Signs of **rupture**: fetal parts easily felt under the skin, the fetus felt outside the uterus with the uterus as a separate mass, an absent fetal heart, peritonism, shock.

**Vulva and vagina**:
- An **oedematous vulva**.
- Meconium or foul-smelling liquor.
- **Hot, dry vagina.**
- An **oedematous cervix**, often a swollen anterior lip "hanging like a curtain", **loosely applied** to the head.
- **Big caput and moulding 3+.**
- **Station**: note it, but beware caput making the head feel lower than it is.
- **Position**:
  - OP: the anterior fontanelle is felt anteriorly.
  - Face: mouth, gums and malar bones. The mouth plus malar bones form a **triangle**. In breech, the anus and ischial tuberosities lie in a **straight line**.
  - Brow: the anterior fontanelle, supraorbital ridges and root of the nose.
  - Shoulder: ribs, scapula, axilla, or a prolapsed hand.
- A **prolapsed cord or limb**.
- **Blood-stained urine** in the catheter bag.

> [!EXAM]
> **Hand versus foot on VE**: a hand has fingers of unequal length and a thumb that can be abducted, and there is no heel. A foot has a heel, toes in a straight line and a big toe that does not abduct. **Mouth versus anus**: the mouth has hard gums and sucks your finger. The anus grips, and meconium stains the glove.

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **Bandl's ring**, a tender lower segment, or haematuria: rupture is imminent. Go to theatre now.
> - **Sudden cessation of contractions**, shock, fetal parts under the skin, or loss of the presenting part on VE: **uterine rupture**. Resuscitate and do an emergency laparotomy.
> - **Maternal fever over 38 °C**, tachycardia, or foul liquor: sepsis. Start IV antibiotics immediately.
> - **Prolapsed cord**: category 1 emergency.
> - **Prolapsed arm with transverse lie**: neglected shoulder presentation. **Never pull the arm.**
> - **A scarred uterus** plus prolonged labour.
> - **Fetal bradycardia**, or loss of the fetal heart.

### Thinking like a clinician

1. **Slow labour: is it the powers or an obstruction?** Look at the contractions. **Weak or infrequent contractions and no signs of obstruction** mean **inefficient uterine action**, which (in a primigravida with a cephalic presentation and no CPD) may be augmented with oxytocin. **Strong contractions and no descent**, with moulding 3+, caput and an oedematous cervix, mean **obstruction**, and oxytocin would be lethal.
2. **Never trust station alone.** A head at "0 station" with 3/5 palpable abdominally is **not engaged**. That is caput and moulding lying to you.
3. **In a multipara, "obstructed labour" is a malpresentation or a big baby until proven otherwise.** A multipara has already proven her pelvis with previous babies.
4. **Contractions stopping in a long labour is ominous**: think exhaustion or rupture, not "labour settling".
5. **The dead baby does not remove the emergency.** The mother still has an obstructed uterus that can rupture and become infected. A **CS for a dead baby** is sometimes the safest route when destructive delivery is not possible or safe. The decision depends on cervical dilatation, the station of the head, operator skill and signs of rupture.
6. **Think of fistula from the moment of diagnosis.** Catheterise at once, and document haematuria and the amount of urine. Keep the catheter in afterwards.
7. **Breech in labour at an HC III or IV**: check the gestation, the type of breech, the estimated fetal weight, how far labour has progressed and whether theatre is available. A multipara at 8 cm with a frank breech will often deliver before transfer, so **be ready to conduct an assisted breech**.
8. **Common student mistakes**:
   - reporting only the cervical dilatation without the **fifths palpable, moulding, caput and the state of the bladder**
   - suggesting oxytocin for "poor progress" without checking for obstruction
   - forgetting the catheter
   - pulling on a breech
   - calling a face a breech

## Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing features | Key investigation or clue |
|---|---|---|
| **Obstructed labour** | Strong contractions, no descent, moulding 3+, caput, Bandl's ring, oedematous cervix, haematuria | Clinical: partograph or LCG crosses the action line; head 3/5 or more palpable in the second stage |
| **Inefficient uterine action (hypotonic)** | Weak or infrequent contractions (fewer than 3 in 10 min, each under 40 s), no moulding 3+, the mother is not distressed | Tocography by hand: count contractions over 10 min |
| **Uterine rupture** | Sudden pain, then contractions stop, shock, fetal parts palpable, loss of station, vaginal bleeding, haematuria | Clinical; point-of-care ultrasound shows a fetus or free fluid outside the uterus |
| **Placental abruption** | Pain, a "woody" hard uterus, dark bleeding, shock out of proportion to visible loss, fetal distress or death | Clinical; ultrasound (retroplacental clot, not always visible) |
| **False labour** | Irregular contractions, no cervical change | Serial VE 2–4 hours apart |
| **Full bladder impeding descent** | Palpable suprapubic mass; descent improves after voiding | Catheterise |
| **Malposition (OP) with normal progress** | Backache, slower but steady progress | Serial VE; plot on the LCG |

## Investigations

Do not delay delivery waiting for investigations in obstructed labour. Send them as you prepare for theatre.

| Test | What you are looking for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Hb / FBC** | Anaemia; raised WBC | Tolerance of blood loss; sepsis |
| **Blood group and cross-match (at least 2 units)** | | Risk of PPH and rupture |
| **Urinalysis or dipstick** | Ketones, **blood**, protein | Dehydration, bladder injury, pre-eclampsia |
| **Urea, creatinine, electrolytes** | AKI, hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia | Electrolyte derangements are common in obstructed labour in Uganda |
| **Random blood sugar** | Hypoglycaemia (starvation), or diabetes if the baby is macrosomic | |
| **Blood culture** (if febrile) and a **high vaginal swab** | Organisms | Guide antibiotics for sepsis |
| **HIV test** (if unknown), syphilis | | PMTCT for the baby |
| **Ultrasound** (if available and it will not delay care) | Presentation, fetal heart, placenta site, fetal anomaly, free fluid | Confirms malpresentation, fetal death, praevia |
| **Pelvimetry (clinical)** | Sacral promontory reached, prominent spines, narrow subpubic arch (fewer than 2 fingers), intertuberous diameter (fewer than 4 knuckles) | Contracted pelvis |

## Management

### A. Obstructed labour: the emergency algorithm

1. **Recognise and call for help**: the senior midwife, the doctor on call, the anaesthetist and the theatre. **Stop any oxytocin infusion.**
2. **Position**: left lateral, to relieve aortocaval compression. Give **oxygen** if in distress.
3. **IV access**: two large-bore cannulae (16–18 G). Take blood for **Hb, grouping and cross-matching**, urea and electrolytes, and blood culture if febrile.
4. **Rehydrate**: **Ringer's lactate or 0.9% saline, 1 L fast**, then continue to give **at least 1.5 L** before and during surgery. Titrate to pulse, BP and urine output (target over 30 mL/hour). If she is shocked, give boluses and treat as haemorrhage or sepsis.
5. **Catheterise**: **Foley 14–16 F**. Empty the bladder and **note the colour** (haematuria) and volume. Leave it on free drainage. An empty bladder is safer at CS (less bladder injury) and it protects the bladder base.
6. **Antibiotics**: IV broad-spectrum antibiotics now (see the drug table).
7. **Assess**:
   - Is the fetus alive?
   - What is the cervical dilatation?
   - How many fifths are palpable?
   - Are there signs of rupture?
   - What is the presentation?
8. **Decide the mode of delivery and aim to deliver within 2 hours.**
   - **Live fetus**: **caesarean section**. Get the head out with care. A **deeply impacted head** may need an assistant to **push it up from below** (a "push method" with the hand in the vagina), or the surgeon can deliver the breech first (the reverse breech or "pull" method). Extend the incision to a J or inverted T if needed. Watch for lateral extension of the incision into the uterine vessels.
   - **Dead fetus, cervix fully dilated, head 3/5 or less above the brim, no rupture, experienced operator**: consider **craniotomy** or another destructive delivery to avoid a scar in a woman who may labour again far from a theatre. Otherwise do a **CS**.
   - **Signs of rupture**: **laparotomy** now, with repair or hysterectomy.
9. **Neonatal team at delivery**: expect asphyxia and sepsis. Follow HBB/NRP.
10. **After delivery**: active management of the third stage with **oxytocin 10 IU IM/IV**. Be vigilant for PPH, because the uterus is exhausted and atonic.

> [!DRUG]
> **Oxytocin is contraindicated in obstructed labour.** Augmenting contractions against a mechanical block ruptures the uterus, especially in multiparas and in scarred uteri.

Once infection is established (chorioamnionitis, endometritis or rupture), continue IV antibiotics until **48 hours afebrile**. Many Ugandan units then switch to oral agents (for example amoxicillin plus metronidazole, or cefixime plus metronidazole) to complete **5–7 days**.

> [!UGANDA]
> In the Mulago audit, and in other Ugandan hospital protocols, a woman with obstructed labour gets **IV fluids, IV antibiotics, observations, a catheter and a cross-match, and is delivered within 2 hours**. In practice most Ugandan units use **ceftriaxone plus metronidazole** or **ampicillin plus gentamicin plus metronidazole**. Learn the regimen on your ward and follow the current **Uganda Clinical Guidelines 2023** and the **Essential Maternal and Newborn Clinical Care Guidelines**. **HC III**: rehydrate, catheterise, give the first dose of antibiotics, and **refer immediately** with a midwife escort and the partograph or LCG. **HC IV / general hospital**: resuscitate and do the CS on site.

### B. Destructive procedures and symphysiotomy (in context)

- **Craniotomy** (perforating the fetal skull to collapse it): for a **dead fetus** in cephalic presentation, with the **cervix fully dilated** (or at least 7 cm in expert hands), the **head 3/5 or less above the brim**, and **no signs of rupture**. **Decapitation** is for a neglected shoulder presentation with a dead fetus. Both need training. After a destructive delivery, **explore the uterus and genital tract** for rupture and tears, and **catheterise for at least 7–14 days**.
- **Symphysiotomy** (dividing the symphysis pubis fibres under local anaesthesia to enlarge the pelvis by 2–3 cm): historically used for a **live fetus, vertex, cervix fully dilated, head 3/5 or less above the brim, mild CPD, with no CS available**. It is **rarely done in Uganda today** because of long-term pelvic pain, walking difficulty, and urethral or bladder injury. It is examined as a concept, so know the indications and why it has fallen out of favour.

> [!NOTE]
> The WHO and MSF texts still describe these procedures for settings with no safe CS. In a Ugandan hospital with theatre, the answer to "live baby, obstructed labour" is almost always **caesarean section**.

### C. After delivery: preventing fistula and complications

1. **Leave the catheter in for 7–14 days** after prolonged or obstructed labour, or if there was haematuria. Many fistula units prefer 14 days. Encourage oral fluids of at least 3–4 L a day so the urine stays dilute and flushes the bladder.
2. If urine leaks per vaginam despite the catheter, **examine with a speculum and do a dye test**. A small fresh fistula may close with **continuous catheter drainage for 4–6 weeks**. Refer to the fistula team (see the VVF/RVF topic).
3. Monitor for **PPH, sepsis and ileus**, and for **foot drop** (common peroneal or lumbosacral nerve injury): refer to physiotherapy.
4. **Counselling**: explain what happened. If the pelvis is contracted, the next delivery should be an **elective CS in hospital** at 39 weeks. **Birth spacing and family planning** matter.
5. **Mourning** if the baby died. Offer psychosocial support.

### D. Malpositions

#### Occipito-posterior (OP)
1. **Diagnosis**:
   - Flattened lower abdomen, with fetal limbs felt anteriorly.
   - FHR heard in the flank.
   - Backache.
   - Early urge to push.
   - On VE, the **anterior fontanelle is easily felt anteriorly** and the posterior fontanelle posteriorly.
2. **First stage**:
   - support, mobility, hydration, analgesia, an empty bladder
   - most (about 90%) rotate to OA
   - if contractions are weak **and there are no signs of obstruction**, augmentation may be considered (in a primigravida, per protocol)
3. **Second stage**:
   - Allow time. Many deliver spontaneously as **"face to pubes"** (a direct OP), which needs a larger episiotomy.
   - If the second stage is delayed with the head at or below the spines and no obstruction: **ventouse** (with rotation, e.g. a Kiwi OmniCup posterior cup) or **manual rotation** by a skilled operator.
   - If the head is **high (more than 1/5 palpable) or there are signs of obstruction: CS.**

#### Deep transverse arrest
- The head is in OT at or below the spines and fails to rotate.
- **Check for obstruction first**: moulding 3+, caput, fifths palpable.
- **If there is no obstruction**:
  - **rotational vacuum delivery**, or **manual rotation to OA** followed by vacuum
  - **Kielland's forceps** only in expert hands
- **If there is obstruction or the attempt fails**: CS. Do not persist with a failed instrumental delivery.

### E. Breech presentation

**Types**:
- **Frank (extended)**: hips flexed, knees extended. This is the commonest type, especially in primigravidas.
- **Complete (flexed)**: hips and knees flexed.
- **Footling or incomplete**: one or both hips extended, with a foot below the buttocks. This type carries the highest risk of cord prolapse.

**Antenatal plan (from 36 weeks)**:
1. **Confirm by ultrasound**. Look for the causes: placenta praevia, anomalies, fibroids, uterine anomaly, liquor volume.
2. **Offer ECV (external cephalic version)**:
   - from **36+0 weeks in nulliparous** and **37+0 weeks in multiparous** women (RCOG GTG 20a)
   - success is about **50%** overall (higher in multiparas)
   - tocolysis with **terbutaline 0.25 mg SC** improves success
   - do a **CTG before and after**
   - give **anti-D** if she is Rh-negative
   - do it where emergency CS is available
3. **Contraindications to ECV**:
   - CS is indicated anyway (e.g. placenta praevia)
   - APH within the last 7 days
   - abnormal CTG
   - a major uterine anomaly
   - ruptured membranes
   - multiple pregnancy (except the second twin)
   
   **Relative contraindications**: previous CS (discuss), FGR, pre-eclampsia, oligohydramnios, an unstable lie.
4. If ECV fails or is declined, counsel about **planned CS versus planned vaginal breech birth**. The **Term Breech Trial** (2000) found planned CS reduced perinatal mortality and serious neonatal morbidity, with no difference in maternal serious morbidity in the short term. However, a CS scar is dangerous in a future pregnancy in a low-resource setting (rupture, praevia, accreta).

**Favourable criteria for vaginal breech birth**:
- a frank or complete breech with a **flexed head**
- an estimated fetal weight of about **2.0–3.8 kg**
- an adequate pelvis
- no other indication for CS
- no footling presentation
- a **skilled attendant** present
- **spontaneous** labour that progresses well

**Assisted vaginal breech delivery: step by step**
1. Full dilatation confirmed. Empty the bladder. Have the neonatal resuscitation set, a paediatrician or skilled midwife, and a warm towel ready. The mother is in the lithotomy position at the edge of the bed.
2. The mother pushes. **"Hands off the breech"** until the **umbilicus** is born. Traction causes **extension of the arms and head**. Consider an **episiotomy** as the buttocks distend the perineum.
3. Legs: if they are extended, flex the knee (**Pinard's manoeuvre**: press the popliteal fossa to flex the knee, then sweep the leg out).
4. Pull down a loop of the cord gently. Keep the **sacrum anterior** and hold the baby by the **bony pelvis** (thumbs on the sacrum, fingers on the iliac crests), never the abdomen (liver and kidney injury).
5. **Arms**: if they are not delivered spontaneously, use **Lovset's manoeuvre**. Rotate the baby 180 degrees, keeping the back anterior, to bring the posterior shoulder under the symphysis, and sweep that arm down across the face. Then rotate 180 degrees back the other way to deliver the other arm.
6. **After-coming head**: let the baby hang for a few seconds (**Burns–Marshall**) until the nape of the neck (hairline) is seen. Then either:
   - **Mauriceau–Smellie–Veit (MSV)**: the baby lies on your forearm, with your middle finger in the mouth or (preferably) index and ring fingers on the **malar bones** to **flex** the head. The other hand's middle finger pushes the occiput up and the other fingers pull on the shoulders. An assistant applies **suprapubic pressure** to keep the head flexed. Deliver slowly by flexion.
   - **Burns–Marshall**: grasp the feet and swing the body up in an arc over the mother's abdomen while the head delivers slowly. Guard the perineum.
   - **Forceps to the after-coming head** (Piper's, or ordinary obstetric forceps) if skilled.
7. **Delivering the head slowly** prevents a sudden decompression, which causes intracranial haemorrhage.
8. **Trapped head in a preterm baby** (cervix not fully dilated): **incise the cervix (Dührssen incisions at 2, 10 and 6 o'clock)**. Symphysiotomy is a last resort.

> [!EXAM]
> Viva: "Why hands off?" Answer: traction before the umbilicus is born makes the arms extend over the head (nuchal arms) and deflexes the head. The fetus is "delivered by the mother, not pulled by the doctor".

### F. Face, brow, shoulder and compound presentations

| Presentation | Diagnosis | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Face** | A deep groove between the head and back abdominally. On VE: mouth, gums, nose and malar bones (a triangle); the mouth sucks your finger | **Mentoanterior**: allow labour. It usually delivers spontaneously by flexion. **No ventouse on the face.** Low forceps only if expert. **Mentoposterior** (persistent) or any obstruction: **CS**. Warn the parents that the face will be swollen and bruised. |
| **Brow** | Head high, not engaged. On VE: anterior fontanelle, supraorbital ridges, root of the nose | **Persistent brow at term: CS.** Early in labour it may convert to face or vertex; watch briefly with low threshold. No oxytocin, no instruments. |
| **Shoulder / transverse lie** | Uterus wider than long, no pole in the pelvis, head in one flank. On VE: ribs, axilla, scapula, or a **prolapsed arm** | **Antenatal**: look for the cause (praevia, fibroids, polyhydramnios). ECV from 37 weeks. Admit at about 37–38 weeks if the lie is unstable (cord-prolapse risk). **In labour with a live fetus: CS**. It may need a **vertical (classical or DeLee) uterine incision** if the lower segment is poorly formed or the back is down. **Neglected with a dead fetus**: CS is usually safest. Decapitation only by an expert. **Internal podalic version only for the second twin.** |
| **Compound** | A hand or arm beside the head | Usually resolves. Do not push the arm up routinely; pinching the fingers may make the baby withdraw it. If obstructed or the cord prolapses: CS. |

### G. Cord prolapse: algorithm (RCOG GTG 50)

1. **Call for help**, note the time, and **stop any oxytocin**.
2. **Do not handle the cord** (it causes vasospasm). If it is outside the vagina, replace it gently or cover it with warm, saline-soaked gauze.
3. **Relieve compression**:
   - Push the presenting part up with fingers in the vagina, or
   - **Knee–chest position**, or the **exaggerated Sims (left lateral, head down, pillow under the hip)** position, especially during transfer.
4. **Bladder filling** if a delay is expected (e.g. transfer from an HC III or IV): insert a Foley, **instil 500–750 mL of 0.9% saline**, and clamp it. **Empty the bladder before the uterus is opened.**
5. **Tocolysis**: **terbutaline 0.25 mg SC** if there is a delay and the CTG is abnormal.
6. **Deliver**:
   - **Category 1 CS** (aim for delivery **within 30 minutes**) if the fetus is alive and delivery is not imminent.
   - If the cervix is **fully dilated** and delivery is quick and safe: an **assisted vaginal birth** (ventouse or forceps) or a breech extraction by a skilled operator.
   - **If the fetus is dead**: vaginal delivery.
7. Have a neonatal resuscitation team ready. Take **paired cord gases** if possible. Debrief the family.

**Prevention**: do not do an ARM when the head is high or the lie is unstable. Admit women with a transverse or unstable lie from 37 weeks.

### H. Shoulder dystocia: HELPERR

Suspect it when the head delivers and then **retracts against the perineum ("turtle sign")**. Do not panic, **do not pull**, and **do not use fundal pressure**. Note the time the head was delivered.

| Step | Action | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **H**elp | Call the midwife, obstetrician, paediatrician and anaesthetist. Stop the mother pushing. | You need hands, and pushing further impacts the shoulder. |
| **E**valuate for episiotomy | Makes room for internal manoeuvres. It does **not** free the shoulder on its own. | The obstruction is bony, not soft tissue. |
| **L**egs: **McRoberts** | Lay her flat and **hyperflex both thighs onto the abdomen**. | Flattens the lumbosacral angle, rotates the symphysis upwards and increases the AP diameter. It works alone in up to about 90% of cases. |
| **P**ressure: **suprapubic** | An assistant pushes on the **posterior aspect of the anterior shoulder**, from the side of the fetal back, towards the fetal chest (continuous, then rocking). | Adducts and rotates the anterior shoulder into the wider oblique diameter. |
| **E**nter (internal rotation) | **Rubin II** (push behind the anterior shoulder), **Woods' screw** (push on the front of the posterior shoulder), **reverse Woods**. | Rotates the shoulders into the oblique diameter. |
| **R**emove the posterior arm | Follow the posterior arm to the elbow, flex it, and sweep the hand across the chest and out. | Reduces the shoulder width by 2–3 cm. |
| **R**oll | **All-fours (Gaskin)** position, then retry the manoeuvres. | Gravity and changes in pelvic diameters. |

**Last resorts** (rare): posterior axillary sling traction, deliberate clavicle fracture, **Zavanelli** (replacing the head followed by CS), symphysiotomy.

**Afterwards**:
- Check the baby for **brachial plexus (Erb's) palsy**, fractured clavicle or humerus, and HIE.
- Check the mother for **PPH** and **third- or fourth-degree tears**.
- Document the **time of each manoeuvre, who did what, and which shoulder was anterior**.

## Complications

| Maternal | Fetal / neonatal |
|---|---|
| **Uterine rupture**, haemorrhagic shock, death | Intrapartum stillbirth |
| **PPH** (atony after a prolonged labour, tears, extension of the CS incision) | Birth asphyxia, HIE, cerebral palsy |
| **Puerperal sepsis**, peritonitis, septic pelvic thrombophlebitis | Neonatal sepsis (prolonged rupture of membranes) |
| **Obstetric fistula** (VVF, RVF), vaginal stenosis | Intracranial haemorrhage (breech, instruments), cephalhaematoma |
| **Foot drop** (lumbosacral or common peroneal nerve injury) | Brachial plexus injury (shoulder dystocia, breech), clavicle or humerus fracture |
| Bladder injury at CS, ileus, AKI, VTE | Cord prolapse causing hypoxia |
| Secondary infertility, psychological trauma, stigma | Facial oedema and bruising (face presentation) |

## Ward-round & exam pearls

### Model presentation

> "Mrs N is a 19-year-old primigravida at 40 weeks, referred from an HC III after 30 hours of labour, with membranes ruptured 20 hours ago. She is exhausted, febrile at 38.2 °C, with a pulse of 118 and a dry tongue. The abdomen shows a cephalic presentation with 3/5 palpable, a Bandl's ring at 2 fingerbreadths below the umbilicus, and a tender lower segment. The FHR is 170 bpm. On VE the cervix is 8 cm and oedematous, the position is ROT, and there is caput 3+ and moulding 3+. The catheter drained 100 mL of blood-stained urine. **Summary: obstructed labour with impending uterine rupture, chorioamnionitis and fetal distress.** I have started Ringer's lactate, sent a cross-match, given IV ceftriaxone and metronidazole, and informed theatre for an emergency CS. The catheter will remain in for 14 days because of the fistula risk."

### Viva questions

1. **What are the signs of obstructed labour?**
   - **Maternal**: dehydration, tachycardia, fever, exhaustion, Bandl's ring, a tender lower segment, a distended bladder, haematuria, an oedematous vulva and cervix.
   - **Fetal**: moulding 3+, caput, a high head, distress or death.
2. **Why is oxytocin contraindicated?** Contracting against a fixed obstruction thins the lower segment until it ruptures.
3. **Why does a mentoposterior face not deliver?** The neck is already maximally extended and cannot extend further around the sacrum. A mentoanterior face delivers by flexion.
4. **Which presenting diameter is largest?** Mentovertical (brow), 13.5 cm.
5. **Name three manoeuvres for the breech arms and head.** Lovset (arms), Mauriceau–Smellie–Veit and Burns–Marshall (head). Also Pinard (legs).
6. **When is ECV offered?** From 36 weeks in nulliparous women and 37 weeks in multiparous women. It is about 50% successful.
7. **Bladder volume for cord prolapse?** 500–750 mL of saline.
8. **First manoeuvres in shoulder dystocia?** McRoberts plus suprapubic pressure.

### OSCE tips

- In a breech-mannequin station, **verbalise "hands off until the umbilicus"**, keep the **back anterior**, and **hold the pelvis, not the abdomen**.
- In a shoulder dystocia station, **say the time**, call for help, and avoid fundal pressure. Document everything.
- When you counsel for ECV, give the success rate (about 50%), the risks (small: transient FHR changes, abruption, need for emergency CS in about 1 in 200), the alternatives, and anti-D if Rh-negative.

## Mnemonics & memory aids

- **Causes of obstruction: 3 Ps.** Passage (contracted pelvis, tumours), Passenger (big baby, malposition, malpresentation, anomaly, locked twins), Powers (usually strong in obstruction).
- **Signs of obstruction: "MOULD BH".** Moulding 3+, Oedematous cervix/vulva, Unengaged head (3/5+), Long labour, Distressed or dead fetus, Bandl's ring, Haematuria.
- **Shoulder dystocia: HELPERR.** Help, Evaluate for episiotomy, Legs (McRoberts), Pressure (suprapubic), Enter (rotational manoeuvres), Remove the posterior arm, Roll onto all fours.
- **Breech: "Hands off, back up, arms by Lovset, head by MSV."**
- **Face vs breech on VE:** the face makes a **triangle** (mouth and malar bones) and the breech makes a **line** (anus and ischial tuberosities).
- **"Chin to the back = CS."** A mentoposterior face will not deliver.
