## In a nutshell

**Sickle cell disease (SCD)** is an inherited haemoglobin disorder in which **haemoglobin S polymerises when deoxygenated**, turning red cells into rigid "sickles" that **block small vessels (vaso-occlusion)** and **break down early (haemolysis)**.

Pregnancy stacks four extra stresses on top of SCD: **more anaemia, a hypercoagulable state, higher oxygen demand, and more infection**, and the **placenta** itself is a low-oxygen vascular bed where sickling happens easily. The result is more **painful crises, acute chest syndrome, thrombosis, pre-eclampsia, growth restriction, preterm birth and stillbirth**, and a maternal mortality several times higher than that of women without SCD.

Why it matters in Uganda: about **1 in 7 Ugandans carries the sickle gene** and an estimated **15,000–20,000 babies are born with SCD each year**. With better childhood care (penicillin, hydroxyurea, malaria prevention), more girls with SCD now reach adulthood and pregnancy. You **will** meet them on the ward.

> [!PEARL]
> The one thing never to forget: **cold, dehydration, hypoxia, infection and acidosis make red cells sickle.** Almost every intervention in SCD pregnancy (warmth, fluids, oxygen, antibiotics, malaria prevention, early analgesia) is about removing one of these triggers.

## Definitions & classification

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sickle cell disease** | Umbrella term for genotypes causing clinical sickling: **HbSS** (sickle cell anaemia, most common and most severe in Uganda), **HbSC**, **HbS/β⁰-thalassaemia** (severe), **HbS/β⁺-thalassaemia** (milder) |
| **Sickle cell trait (HbAS)** | Carrier state. **Not a disease**: normal Hb, no crises. Mild increase in **UTI** and haematuria; important for **partner counselling** |
| **Vaso-occlusive (painful) crisis** | Acute pain from microvascular occlusion and ischaemia in bone marrow, chest, abdomen |
| **Acute chest syndrome (ACS)** | New pulmonary infiltrate on CXR plus fever and/or respiratory symptoms (cough, chest pain, tachypnoea, hypoxia) |
| **Sequestration crisis** | Sudden pooling of blood in the spleen (or liver) with acute fall in Hb and enlarging organ |
| **Aplastic crisis** | Temporary marrow shutdown (usually **parvovirus B19**) with falling Hb and **very low reticulocytes** |
| **Hyperhaemolytic crisis** | Accelerated haemolysis: falling Hb, **high reticulocytes**, rising bilirubin and LDH |
| **Baseline (steady-state) Hb** | Her usual Hb when well (often 6–9 g/dL in HbSS; higher in HbSC). Always compare to this |

## Epidemiology & Uganda context

- The **Uganda Sickle Surveillance Study** (2014–2015, dried blood spots from infants nationwide) found **sickle cell trait in about 13%** and **SCD in about 0.7% of infants**, with the highest rates in the **east-central (Busoga), mid-northern and north-eastern** regions and lower rates in the south-west.
- High trait prevalence is the footprint of **malaria**: HbAS protects against severe falciparum malaria, so the gene was selected for.
- Without care, many children with SCD in Africa die before age 5. Uganda now has **SCD clinics** at Mulago (the national sickle cell clinic), regional referral hospitals and some general hospitals, with **hydroxyurea** increasingly available. This means more women conceive **while taking hydroxyurea**.
- Real-world obstacles: **blood shortages** (and alloantibodies making cross-match slow), no HbS-negative/phenotyped blood, limited access to LMWH, no exchange transfusion outside a few centres, limited echo, and women who present late or book at HC IIIs without specialist input.
- Pregnancy outcomes in African cohorts are poorer than in high-income countries: higher rates of maternal death (mainly from ACS, sepsis, severe anaemia and thromboembolism), stillbirth and low birth weight.

> [!UGANDA]
> Standard Ugandan ANC gives **ferrous sulphate + folic acid (FeFo)** and **IPTp-SP** to everyone. For a woman with SCD, **modify both**: give **folic acid 5 mg daily** (not the low-dose combination), **withhold routine iron** unless iron deficiency is proven, and choose malaria chemoprevention carefully (see Management), because **folic acid at 5 mg or more reduces the effectiveness of SP**.

## Pathophysiology

### 1. The basic mechanism

```
Point mutation: beta-globin gene codon 6, glutamic acid -> valine  (HbS)
        |
Deoxygenated HbS polymerises into long rigid fibres
        |   (triggers: hypoxia, acidosis, dehydration, cold, infection, stress)
Sickled, stiff, sticky red cells
        |
   +----+---------------------------+
   |                                |
VASO-OCCLUSION                   HAEMOLYSIS (RBC life 10-20 days, not 120)
 - bone/marrow infarction (pain)   - chronic anaemia, jaundice, gallstones
 - acute chest syndrome            - free Hb scavenges nitric oxide
 - stroke, priapism, AVN hip         -> vasoconstriction, endothelial damage,
 - splenic infarction                   pulmonary hypertension
   (auto-splenectomy -> infection   - high folate demand (marrow working hard)
    with encapsulated bacteria)
```

### 2. Why pregnancy makes SCD worse

| Physiological change | Consequence in SCD |
|---|---|
| **Plasma volume rises more than red cell mass** (dilutional anaemia) plus increased folate demand | Hb falls from an already low baseline; **folate deficiency** causes megaloblastic crisis |
| **Hypercoagulability** (rise in fibrinogen and factors VII, VIII, X; fall in protein S; venous stasis) | Adds to the prothrombotic state of SCD, so **VTE risk rises markedly** |
| **Increased oxygen consumption** and the **placental bed** (low-oxygen intervillous space) | Sickling in the placenta: **placental infarcts, fibrosis, abruption** |
| **Relative immunosuppression**, urinary stasis | More **UTI, pyelonephritis, pneumonia, malaria**, puerperal sepsis, all of which trigger crises |
| **Nausea and vomiting**, reduced intake | Dehydration triggers crises |
| **Labour**: pain, exertion, dehydration, acidosis, hypothermia, blood loss | **Crises in labour and the puerperium** |
| **Diaphragmatic splinting** late in pregnancy and after CS; pain limiting breathing | Basal atelectasis, which predisposes to **acute chest syndrome** |

### 3. Why the fetus suffers

Sickling in the **uteroplacental circulation** reduces placental blood flow, causing **placental insufficiency**. The fetus gets less oxygen and nutrients, leading to **growth restriction, oligohydramnios, fetal distress and stillbirth**. Maternal severe anaemia and hypoxia during crises add acute insults. Placental vascular disease also underlies the increased **pre-eclampsia** risk. The fetus does **not** sickle in utero, because fetal haemoglobin (HbF) predominates; SCD in the baby shows only after about 6 months as HbF falls.

### 4. The "why" behind the management

| Intervention | Mechanism it targets |
|---|---|
| **Folic acid 5 mg** | Chronic haemolysis consumes folate for new red cells |
| **No routine iron** | Iron is recycled from haemolysed cells, and transfusions add iron: most women are **iron-overloaded**, not deficient |
| **Penicillin + vaccines** | **Functional asplenia** leaves her vulnerable to pneumococcus, meningococcus and *H. influenzae* |
| **Malaria prevention** | Malaria causes haemolysis, fever, dehydration and hypoxia: a major crisis trigger and killer in Africa |
| **Aspirin from 12 weeks** | Reduces placental vascular disease and pre-eclampsia |
| **LMWH** | Counteracts the combined hypercoagulability |
| **Warmth, fluids, oxygen** | Prevent deoxygenation, cold-induced vasoconstriction and increased blood viscosity |
| **Incentive spirometry** | Prevents atelectasis, the precursor of acute chest syndrome |
| **Stop hydroxyurea before conception** | Hydroxyurea is teratogenic in animal studies (it inhibits DNA synthesis) |

## Clinical acumen

### Focused history

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Which genotype (SS, SC, S/β-thal)? How was it diagnosed (electrophoresis)? | Severity and specific risks (SC: sequestration, retinopathy, often under-recognised) |
| **Baseline Hb** and usual symptoms | You need a reference point to recognise acute anaemia |
| Crises in the last year: number, admissions, ICU, **previous acute chest syndrome, stroke** | Predicts crises in pregnancy; previous ACS/stroke may indicate transfusion programmes |
| **Transfusion history** and any problems (reactions, "difficult cross-match") | **Alloantibodies** make blood hard to find and increase haemolytic disease of the newborn risk |
| Medicines: **hydroxyurea**, penicillin, folic acid, iron chelators, ACE inhibitors, analgesics (including opioid use) | Hydroxyurea/chelators/ACEi must stop; penicillin and folic acid must continue |
| Vaccination history (pneumococcal, meningococcal, HiB, hepatitis B, influenza/COVID) | Asplenic protection |
| End-organ damage: breathlessness/exercise tolerance (pulmonary hypertension, cardiomyopathy), kidney disease/proteinuria, eye problems, leg ulcers, avascular necrosis of hip, gallstones | Baseline complications change pregnancy risk and delivery planning |
| **Partner's Hb status** | If he is AS, there is a 50% chance of an affected baby (AS × SS); if AA, the baby will be AS |
| Previous pregnancies: miscarriage, IUGR, pre-eclampsia, stillbirth, CS, postpartum crises | Recurrence risks |
| Malaria prevention: ITN use, chemoprophylaxis | Crisis trigger |
| Current symptoms: pain, fever, cough, breathlessness, chest pain, dysuria, headache, weakness/numbness, abdominal pain, fetal movements | Crises, infection, ACS, stroke, pre-eclampsia, fetal wellbeing |

### Examination

- **Vitals**: temperature (infection), RR and **SpO₂** (ACS), HR, **BP** (pre-eclampsia; note that SCD patients often have a low baseline BP, so a "normal" 130/85 may be relatively high).
- **General**: pallor, **jaundice** (haemolysis), dehydration, leg ulcers, frontal bossing/maxillary hyperplasia, dental hygiene.
- **Chest**: crackles, bronchial breathing, reduced air entry (ACS, pneumonia), signs of pulmonary hypertension (loud P2, right heart failure).
- **Abdomen**: **spleen** (usually impalpable in adults with HbSS because of auto-infarction; **a palpable, enlarging spleen with falling Hb means sequestration**, more likely in HbSC or S/β-thal), liver (hepatic sequestration), RUQ tenderness (cholecystitis, hepatic crisis), renal angle tenderness (pyelonephritis).
- **Obstetric**: SFH (IUGR), lie, fetal heart, uterine tenderness (abruption), contractions (preterm labour).
- **Neurological** if any headache, weakness, speech change (stroke).
- **Bones and joints**: tender, swollen areas in a painful crisis; hip (avascular necrosis).

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **Chest pain, cough, fever, breathlessness or SpO₂ below 95%**: acute chest syndrome until proven otherwise (CXR, oxygen, antibiotics, senior review, early transfusion).
> - **Sudden drop in Hb** (2 g/dL or more below baseline, or below 6 g/dL): sequestration, aplastic crisis (check reticulocytes), hyperhaemolysis or bleeding (abruption, PPH).
> - **Enlarging spleen or liver with falling Hb**: sequestration: urgent transfusion.
> - **Weakness, facial droop, speech disturbance, severe headache, seizures**: stroke (exchange transfusion) or eclampsia.
> - **Fever 38 °C or more**: overwhelming sepsis risk (asplenia) and malaria: cultures, mRDT, IV antibiotics now.
> - **Pain not controlled by opioids, or atypical pain**: think abruption, labour, ectopic, pyelonephritis, appendicitis, cholecystitis, pancreatitis, VTE, or HELLP.
> - **Leg swelling or pleuritic pain**: DVT/PE (an ACS mimic).
> - **Reduced fetal movements** or falling SFH: IUGR/fetal compromise.

### Thinking like a clinician

- **"It's just another crisis" is the most dangerous phrase on the ward.** A woman with SCD in pregnancy who has abdominal pain may have **abruption, labour, pyelonephritis, ectopic pregnancy, appendicitis, cholecystitis or HELLP**. Examine the abdomen properly, check the fetal heart, urine and BP every time.
- **Every painful crisis in pregnancy is a potential acute chest syndrome**: ACS often develops 1–3 days into an admission for pain, especially after opioids (hypoventilation) and with rib/back pain. Check SpO₂ and RR 4-hourly; encourage incentive spirometry.
- **Compare every Hb to her baseline.** A Hb of 7 g/dL is reassuring in a woman whose baseline is 7, and alarming in a woman with HbSC whose baseline is 11.
- **Reticulocytes help separate acute anaemias**: low reticulocytes = aplastic (parvovirus); high = haemolysis or sequestration.
- **Fever is an emergency** because of asplenia. Do not wait for the malaria result before giving antibiotics if she is septic.
- **HbSC is not "mild" in pregnancy**: these women may have near-normal Hb and few previous crises, so they are overlooked, yet they are at risk of sequestration, ACS, VTE and retinopathy.
- **Sickle trait (AS) is not disease**: treat as normal pregnancy (with urine screening and partner counselling). Do not give her the SCD protocol.
- **Common student mistakes**:
  1. Prescribing routine FeFo (iron) to a woman with SCD.
  2. Forgetting that **5 mg folic acid interferes with SP**; failing to plan malaria chemoprevention.
  3. Giving **pethidine** for crisis pain (risk of seizures from its metabolite norpethidine).
  4. Giving NSAIDs in the first trimester or after 28 weeks.
  5. Over-hydrating (pulmonary oedema, especially with pre-eclampsia or cardiac disease); monitor fluid balance.
  6. Forgetting thromboprophylaxis after delivery.

## Differential diagnosis

### Pain in a pregnant woman with SCD

| Condition | Distinguishing features | Key investigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Vaso-occlusive crisis** | Typical of her previous crises (limbs, back, chest, abdomen), triggers (cold, infection, dehydration), normal uterine tone | Diagnosis of exclusion: FBC, retics, infection screen |
| **Placental abruption** | Constant abdominal pain, **woody-hard tender uterus**, vaginal bleeding (may be concealed), fetal distress/absent FHR, shock out of proportion | Clinical, CTG, ultrasound (limited sensitivity), Hb, clotting |
| **Preterm or term labour** | Regular painful contractions, cervical change | Speculum/VE, CTG |
| **Pyelonephritis** | Fever, rigors, loin pain, dysuria | Urinalysis, urine culture |
| **Acute chest syndrome** | Chest pain, cough, fever, tachypnoea, hypoxia, new infiltrate | CXR, SpO₂, blood gas |
| **Pulmonary embolism** | Sudden pleuritic pain, breathlessness, tachycardia, clear chest on auscultation, leg swelling | CTPA/V/Q where available, Doppler legs, ECG |
| **Pre-eclampsia/HELLP** | Epigastric/RUQ pain, rising BP (relative to her low baseline), proteinuria, low platelets, raised liver enzymes | BP, urine protein, FBC, LFTs |
| **Cholecystitis / biliary colic** (pigment gallstones) | RUQ pain after meals, Murphy's sign, fever | Ultrasound, LFTs |
| **Hepatic sequestration / hepatic crisis** | Painful enlarging liver, rising bilirubin, falling Hb | LFTs, FBC, ultrasound |
| **Appendicitis** | Migratory pain, right-sided (higher in pregnancy), fever | Clinical, ultrasound |
| **Malaria** | Fever, rigors, headache, haemolysis | mRDT, blood smear |

### Acute anaemia in SCD

| Cause | Reticulocytes | Other features |
|---|---|---|
| **Aplastic crisis** (parvovirus B19) | **Very low** | Recent viral illness; fetal risk of anaemia/hydrops |
| **Splenic/hepatic sequestration** | High | Enlarging tender organ, shock |
| **Hyperhaemolysis** (often post-transfusion) | High (may fall in severe forms) | Jaundice, high LDH/bilirubin, Hb below pre-transfusion level |
| **Malaria haemolysis** | High | Positive mRDT/smear |
| **Folate deficiency** (megaloblastic) | Low | Macrocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils; poor folate adherence |
| **Bleeding** (abruption, PPH, GI) | Rise after days | Visible or concealed blood loss |

| Test | What you are looking for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Hb electrophoresis / HPLC** (woman and **partner**) | Genotype; baseline HbS and HbF % | Confirms diagnosis, guides counselling |
| **FBC with reticulocytes** at booking and each trimester (and in any crisis) | Baseline Hb, acute falls, reticulocyte response | Detect acute anaemia; plan transfusion |
| **Blood film** | Sickle cells, target cells, Howell–Jolly bodies, malaria parasites | |
| **Serum ferritin** | True iron deficiency versus overload | Decides whether to give iron |
| **Blood group and red cell antibody screen**; extended phenotype if possible | Alloantibodies | Safe cross-match; HDFN risk |
| **Renal function, urine protein:creatinine ratio** | Sickle nephropathy | Baseline for pre-eclampsia |
| **LFTs, bilirubin, LDH** | Haemolysis, hepatic involvement | |
| **Echocardiography** (TR jet velocity) where available | **Pulmonary hypertension** (TR velocity above 2.5 m/s) | PHT carries very high maternal mortality |
| **Retinal examination** | Proliferative retinopathy (especially HbSC) | |

## Management

### 1. Pre-conception care (SCD clinic + obstetrician)

1. **Counsel about risks** to her and the baby, and plan pregnancy when she is well.
2. **Test the partner** (Hb electrophoresis). If he is AS or has SCD, offer genetic counselling (prenatal diagnosis is available in few centres).
3. **Start folic acid 5 mg orally once daily** (continue throughout pregnancy).
4. **Stop hydroxyurea at least 3 months before conception**. If she becomes pregnant on it, stop it, reassure (the absolute risk appears low) and arrange a detailed anomaly scan; do not advise termination on this basis alone.
5. **Stop ACE inhibitors/ARBs** (used for proteinuria) and **iron chelators** (deferasirox, desferrioxamine).
6. **Continue penicillin V prophylaxis** (see table). Update **vaccines**: pneumococcal, meningococcal, *H. influenzae* type b, hepatitis B, and seasonal influenza/COVID where available.
7. **Screen for end-organ damage**: echo (PHT), renal function and proteinuria, retinal screening, red cell antibodies, iron status.
8. **Optimise**: treat infections, malaria prevention, dental care, blood pressure record.

### 2. Antenatal care

Book early (first trimester) at a **hospital with obstetric and haematology/physician support**. **Visits every 2–4 weeks**, more frequently in the third trimester. At every visit: BP, urinalysis, SFH, fetal heart, Hb (as needed), crisis/infection screening questions.

#### Routine drugs

| Drug | Dose | Route | Frequency | Duration / notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Folic acid** | **5 mg** | Oral | Once daily | Throughout pregnancy and after (lifelong in SCD) |
| **Penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin)** | **250 mg** (some protocols 500 mg) | Oral | Twice daily | Continue throughout pregnancy; **erythromycin 250 mg twice daily** if allergic |
| **Aspirin (low-dose)** | **75 mg** | Oral | Once daily at night | **From 12 weeks until 36 weeks** (pre-eclampsia prophylaxis) |
| **Malaria chemoprevention** | See box below | Oral | | Throughout pregnancy, plus **ITN** |
| **Iron** | Only if **ferritin-proven deficiency** | Oral | | Avoid routine FeFo |
| **LMWH** (e.g. enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily; adjust for weight) | Prophylactic dose | SC | Once daily | **During any antenatal admission**; consider antenatal prophylaxis if extra VTE risk factors |
| **Tetanus-diphtheria, other routine ANC** | As standard | | | Mebendazole after first trimester |

> [!DRUG]
> **Malaria chemoprevention in SCD pregnancy: the SP–folic acid problem.** WHO advises that **folic acid 5 mg daily or more should not be given with IPTp-SP**, because high-dose folate antagonises SP's antifolate action. Units manage this differently:
> - **Daily proguanil 200 mg** (safe in pregnancy; give with the folic acid) plus ITN; or
> - **Monthly IPTp-SP from 13 weeks**, with folic acid 5 mg **withheld for about 1–2 weeks after each SP dose**; or
> - Other regimens per the SCD/haematology team.
>
> Do not simply omit malaria prevention: malaria is a leading trigger of crises and death. If she has HIV and takes co-trimoxazole, **no SP**. **Follow your unit protocol** and document the choice.

#### Fetal surveillance

- **Dating/viability scan** in the first trimester.
- **Anomaly scan at 18–22 weeks** (detailed if hydroxyurea exposure).
- **Growth scans every 4 weeks from 24 weeks** (biometry, liquor, umbilical artery Doppler if growth falls off).
- **Fetal movement awareness**; CTG when indicated.

#### Transfusion in pregnancy

- **Routine prophylactic transfusion is not recommended** for all pregnant women with SCD.
- **Indications for top-up transfusion**:
  - **Acute anaemia**: Hb **below 6 g/dL** or a **fall of 2 g/dL or more from baseline** (sequestration, aplastic crisis, hyperhaemolysis);
  - **Symptomatic anaemia** or preparation for **caesarean section** when Hb is low;
  - **Acute chest syndrome** with hypoxia, **stroke** or **multi-organ failure** (these usually need **exchange transfusion** where available);
  - Consider a **regular transfusion programme** for women with **previous severe complications** (e.g. previous ACS, stroke on transfusion), **worsening crises in this pregnancy**, or **twin pregnancy**, on specialist advice.
- **Blood requirements**: **HbS-negative**, matched at least for **ABO, Rh (C, c, E, e) and Kell** where possible, CMV-negative. Check for **alloantibodies** (and warn the lab early). Do not over-transfuse: aim for a **Hb no higher than about 10–11 g/dL** (hyperviscosity triggers crises).

### 3. Managing crises in pregnancy

#### A. Vaso-occlusive (painful) crisis: step by step

1. **Assess rapidly** (ABCDE, pain score, SpO₂, temperature, BP, fetal heart). **Exclude obstetric and surgical causes** of pain.
2. **Analgesia within 30 minutes of arrival**, then reassess every 30 minutes until controlled:

| Drug | Dose | Route | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Paracetamol** | **1 g** | Oral or IV | 6-hourly (max 4 g/day) | For all, unless contraindicated |
| **Morphine** (first-line opioid) | **2.5–5 mg IV** (or 5–10 mg SC / about 0.1 mg/kg), titrated | IV or SC | Repeat every 20–30 minutes until pain controlled, then 2–4-hourly or oral morphine | Monitor RR, sedation and SpO₂; naloxone available; laxative and antiemetic with it |
| **Ibuprofen / diclofenac** (NSAIDs) | Ibuprofen 400 mg; diclofenac 50 mg | Oral | 8-hourly | **Only between 12 and 28 weeks** (avoid in the first trimester and after 28 weeks: premature closure of the ductus arteriosus, oligohydramnios) |
| **Codeine / tramadol** | Codeine 30–60 mg; tramadol 50–100 mg | Oral | 6-hourly | For moderate pain or step-down |
| **Pethidine** | **Avoid** | | | Norpethidine accumulation causes **seizures** |

3. **Fluids**: oral **about 3 L/day** if she can drink; otherwise **IV 0.9% saline or Ringer's lactate** (e.g. 1 L over 4–6 hours then reassess). Keep a **fluid balance chart**; avoid overload.
4. **Oxygen** if **SpO₂ below 95%** (RCOG uses 94%); nasal prongs or mask.
5. **Keep her warm**; avoid cold IV fluids and cold wards.
6. **Look for and treat triggers**: FBC, retics, **mRDT/blood smear**, urinalysis and culture, blood cultures if febrile, CXR if chest symptoms. **Fever: start IV ceftriaxone 2 g once daily** (after cultures) and **treat malaria** with IV artesunate (2.4 mg/kg at 0, 12, 24 hours then daily) if positive and severe, or oral artemether-lumefantrine for uncomplicated malaria (check the UCG advice for the first trimester).
7. **Thromboprophylaxis** (LMWH) and mobilise when possible.
8. **Incentive spirometry** (10 breaths every 2 hours while awake) to prevent ACS.
9. **Monitor** 4-hourly: pain, sedation, RR, SpO₂, temperature, BP, fetal heart. **Daily FBC** in severe crises.
10. **Fetal assessment**: CTG after 28 weeks (a fetal heart abnormality may reflect maternal hypoxia or abruption).
11. Continue folic acid, penicillin, aspirin; step down to oral analgesia as pain settles.

#### B. Acute chest syndrome (emergency)

**Features**: fever, cough, chest pain, tachypnoea, **SpO₂ falling**, crackles, **new infiltrate on CXR**. Can follow a painful crisis, infection or delivery. Differential: pneumonia, PE, pulmonary oedema.

1. **Call senior help** (obstetrician, physician/haematologist, anaesthetist); HDU/ICU.
2. **Oxygen** to keep SpO₂ **94–98%**; sit up; CPAP/ventilation if failing.
3. **CXR** (with abdominal shielding), blood gas, FBC, retics, cultures, cross-match.
4. **Antibiotics**: **IV ceftriaxone 2 g once daily + oral azithromycin 500 mg daily or erythromycin 500 mg 6-hourly** (covers atypical organisms).
5. **Analgesia** (enough to allow deep breathing, not so much that she hypoventilates) and **incentive spirometry**.
6. **Transfusion**: **top-up** if Hb has fallen; **exchange transfusion** if severe hypoxia, worsening despite treatment, multilobar disease, or Hb high enough that top-up would cause hyperviscosity.
7. **Careful fluids** (avoid overload), LMWH, consider **PE** as the alternative diagnosis.
8. Fetal monitoring; **delivery does not treat ACS** and should only be considered for obstetric reasons or as part of senior-led resuscitation.

#### C. Acute anaemia (sequestration, aplastic, hyperhaemolytic)

1. **FBC and reticulocytes**, bilirubin, LDH, parvovirus serology, malaria test, examine spleen and liver.
2. **Transfuse** (top-up) for Hb below 6 g/dL or a fall of 2 g/dL or more, or symptomatic anaemia. In sequestration, transfuse cautiously (blood returns from the spleen as it resolves; aim to restore, not exceed, baseline).
3. **Parvovirus B19** (aplastic crisis): also risk of **fetal anaemia and hydrops**: arrange fetal ultrasound (and MCA Doppler where available).
4. **Hyperhaemolysis** after a transfusion: stop further transfusion unless life-threatening; seek haematology advice (steroids, IVIG).

#### D. Stroke

Sudden weakness, speech disturbance or seizures: stabilise, exclude eclampsia (BP, proteinuria, MgSO₄ if in doubt), urgent imaging, and **exchange transfusion** (or top-up if exchange not available) under specialist care.

### 4. Timing and mode of delivery

1. **Deliver in a hospital** with blood bank, theatre, anaesthesia, HDU and neonatal care.
2. **Timing**: with normal fetal growth, offer **induction of labour (or CS if indicated) between 38+0 and 40+0 weeks** (RCOG). Earlier for complications (IUGR, pre-eclampsia, recurrent crises).
3. **SCD is not an indication for caesarean section**; vaginal birth is preferred. CS only for obstetric indications.
4. Inform the **anaesthetist, haematologist/physician, neonatologist and blood bank** on admission; **cross-match** early (antibodies may delay).

### 5. Intrapartum care: "warm, wet, well-oxygenated, watched"

1. **Warm**: warm labour room, warm IV fluids, blankets; avoid hypothermia in theatre.
2. **Hydrated**: oral fluids; **IV fluids** if not drinking well or vomiting; **fluid balance chart** (risk of overload if pre-eclampsia or cardiac disease).
3. **Oxygenated**: **continuous pulse oximetry**; oxygen if **SpO₂ 94% or below**.
4. **Pain relief**: **epidural/regional analgesia is encouraged** (reduces stress, and is ready for CS); avoid pethidine; **avoid general anaesthesia** where possible (hypoxia, ACS risk).
5. **Continuous CTG** (EFM): higher risk of fetal compromise and stillbirth.
6. **Avoid prolonged labour** (dehydration, exhaustion, acidosis). Partograph action line: act early. Consider operative delivery for delay.
7. **Infection**: prompt treatment of any fever (blood cultures, mRDT, antibiotics).
8. **Third stage**: active management; be ready for **PPH** (she has low reserve: a normal blood loss can make her severely anaemic).
9. **Caesarean section**: regional anaesthesia, warmth, oxygen, thromboprophylaxis, early mobilisation, good analgesia for deep breathing; **transfuse beforehand if Hb is low**.

### 6. Postpartum care

1. **Crisis risk remains high for 1–2 weeks** (about a quarter of women): continue **warmth, hydration, SpO₂ monitoring**, and early mobilisation.
2. **Thromboprophylaxis**: **LMWH for 7 days after vaginal birth, or 6 weeks after caesarean section** (RCOG), plus mobilisation. Where LMWH is unaffordable, at least while in hospital and with early mobilisation, and discuss with seniors.
3. **Incentive spirometry** especially after CS; watch for ACS in the first 72 hours.
4. Continue **folic acid** and **penicillin**; check **Hb** before discharge.
5. **Restart hydroxyurea** after delivery only if **not breastfeeding** (or per haematology advice); breastfeeding is encouraged otherwise.
6. **Contraception** (WHO MEC): **progestogen-only methods (implant, DMPA, POP) and the LNG-IUD are category 1**. DMPA may even **reduce painful crises**. Combined hormonal contraception and copper IUD are category 2 (usable). Pregnancy spacing matters.
7. **Newborn**: if the partner is a carrier or unknown, arrange **newborn screening** (DBS for Hb electrophoresis/HPLC at the immunisation visit where available). SCD in the baby presents after about 6 months (dactylitis, anaemia, infection).
8. Link back to the **SCD clinic**.

### 7. Setting-based summary

| Level | What should happen |
|---|---|
| **HC III** | Identify SCD at booking, give folic acid 5 mg, withhold routine iron, ITN, malaria prevention per protocol, **refer for hospital-based ANC**; treat fever promptly and refer crises |
| **HC IV** | Initial crisis management: analgesia within 30 minutes (paracetamol, morphine or diclofenac 12–28 weeks), IV fluids, oxygen, mRDT and antimalarials, first dose IV ceftriaxone for fever, then **refer** if ACS, severe anaemia, uncontrolled pain, obstetric complication or no blood |
| **Regional / national referral hospital** | Joint obstetric-haematology care, transfusion (top-up; exchange at Mulago/major centres), HDU/ICU, growth scans and CTG, planned delivery, epidural, postnatal LMWH |

## Ward-round & exam pearls

### Model presentation

> "Ms A is a 23-year-old primigravida with **HbSS** (baseline Hb 7.5 g/dL, two admissions for painful crises last year, no previous ACS or stroke, never transfused), at **30 weeks**, admitted yesterday with a **vaso-occlusive crisis** affecting her lower back and thighs, triggered by **malaria** (mRDT positive). She received **morphine within 30 minutes**, now on **oral morphine 10 mg 4-hourly** and paracetamol, IV fluids 3 L/day, and **IV artesunate**. Today her pain score is 4/10, temperature 37.4 °C, **SpO₂ 97% on air**, RR 18, BP 110/70. Hb is **6.9 g/dL** (baseline 7.5), reticulocytes raised. Fetal heart 142 with a reactive CTG; the last growth scan at 28 weeks was on the 30th centile. She is on **folic acid 5 mg, penicillin V 250 mg twice daily, aspirin 75 mg** and **enoxaparin 40 mg daily**. **Plan**: complete antimalarials, step down analgesia, incentive spirometry, watch for ACS (4-hourly SpO₂/RR), repeat Hb tomorrow, growth scan at 32 weeks, and discuss malaria chemoprevention on discharge."

### Likely viva questions

1. **Why is folic acid 5 mg given, not 0.4 mg?** Chronic haemolysis increases red cell turnover and folate demand; deficiency leads to megaloblastic (aplastic-like) crises.
2. **Why no routine iron?** Most women with SCD are iron-replete or overloaded (recycled haem iron, transfusions). Give iron only if ferritin shows deficiency.
3. **What is acute chest syndrome and how do you treat it?** New CXR infiltrate plus fever/respiratory symptoms; oxygen, antibiotics (ceftriaxone + macrolide), analgesia, incentive spirometry, transfusion (top-up or exchange), HDU/ICU.
4. **Which analgesics are avoided?** Pethidine (seizures); NSAIDs before 12 and after 28 weeks.
5. **When do you transfuse?** Acute anaemia (Hb below 6 g/dL or a fall of 2 g/dL or more from baseline), ACS, stroke, multi-organ failure, pre-CS if low; not routinely.
6. **What do you stop before conception?** Hydroxyurea (3 months before), ACE inhibitors/ARBs, iron chelators.
7. **How do you monitor the fetus?** Growth scans every 4 weeks from 24 weeks; Dopplers if growth falls; CTG in labour.
8. **When and how should she deliver?** 38+0 to 40+0 weeks; vaginal unless obstetric indication; warm, hydrated, oxygenated, continuous CTG, regional analgesia.
9. **Postnatal thromboprophylaxis?** LMWH 7 days after vaginal birth, 6 weeks after CS.
10. **Best contraception?** Progestogen-only methods (DMPA may reduce crises), LNG-IUD, implant.
11. **Chance of an affected baby if the partner is AS?** 50% SS, 50% AS.

### OSCE tips

- **Pre-conception counselling station**: cover risks, partner testing, folic acid 5 mg, stopping hydroxyurea, vaccines/penicillin, malaria prevention, avoiding triggers, early booking at a hospital, and the plan for aspirin and growth scans. Be honest but hopeful: most women with SCD who receive good care have a live baby.
- **Genetics**: draw the Punnett square: SS × AA = 100% AS; SS × AS = 50% SS, 50% AS; AS × AS = 25% SS, 50% AS, 25% AA.

## Mnemonics & memory aids

**Crisis triggers: "Cold, Dry, Low O₂, Bugs, Acid"**: cold, dehydration, hypoxia, infection (including malaria), acidosis (plus stress and exertion).

**Antenatal package: "FAMPAS"**

- **F**olic acid 5 mg
- **A**spirin 75 mg from 12 weeks
- **M**alaria prevention + ITN
- **P**enicillin V + vaccines
- **A**void iron, hydroxyurea, ACE inhibitors, pethidine
- **S**cans every 4 weeks from 24 weeks

**Intrapartum: "4 W's"**: **W**arm, **W**et (hydrated), **W**ell-oxygenated, **W**atched (continuous CTG and SpO₂).

**Painful crisis: "PHOTO"**: **P**ain relief within 30 minutes, **H**ydration, **O**xygen if SpO₂ below 95%, **T**riggers (infection, malaria) treated, **O**bstetric causes excluded (and **O**bserve for ACS).
