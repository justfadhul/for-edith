## In a nutshell

- **Pre-eclampsia** is new hypertension after 20 weeks with evidence that the disease has spread beyond the blood vessels: protein in the urine, damage to kidney, liver, brain, blood or lungs, or a struggling placenta (fetal growth restriction).
- It starts in the **placenta** (poor remodelling of the spiral arteries in early pregnancy) and ends in the **mother's endothelium** (the lining of every blood vessel). That is why it affects every organ, and why **delivering the placenta is the only cure**.
- **Eclampsia** is a generalised tonic-clonic seizure in a woman with pre-eclampsia (sometimes the first sign; up to a third of fits occur postpartum).
- In Uganda, hypertensive disorders are the **second commonest direct cause of maternal death after haemorrhage**, and most of those deaths are from **uncontrolled severe hypertension (stroke), eclampsia and pulmonary oedema**, which are largely preventable.
- Three drugs and one decision save lives: **MgSO₄** (prevents and treats fits), a **rapid-acting antihypertensive** (prevents stroke), and **timely delivery**.
- The one thing never to forget: **a BP of ≥160/110 is an emergency. Treat it now, give MgSO₄, and plan delivery.** Do not wait for proteinuria, and do not be reassured by "no symptoms".

## Definitions & classification

**Hypertension in pregnancy** = systolic BP ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥90 mmHg, on two readings (ideally at least 4 hours apart for non-severe values; within 15 minutes for severe values). **Severe hypertension** = ≥160 systolic and/or ≥110 diastolic.

| Category | Definition | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronic (pre-existing) hypertension** | BP ≥140/90 before pregnancy or before 20 weeks, or persisting beyond 12 weeks postpartum | Look for a cause (renal, etc.). Risk of superimposed pre-eclampsia is about 25% |
| **Gestational hypertension** | New BP ≥140/90 at or after 20 weeks, **without** proteinuria or other features of pre-eclampsia | About a quarter progress to pre-eclampsia, so keep checking urine and bloods |
| **Pre-eclampsia** | Gestational hypertension **plus** ≥1 new feature: proteinuria, maternal organ dysfunction or uteroplacental dysfunction | ISSHP 2021 definition; proteinuria is **not** required |
| **Pre-eclampsia superimposed on chronic hypertension** | Woman with chronic hypertension who develops new proteinuria, organ dysfunction or FGR | Sudden worsening of BP control is a clue |
| **Eclampsia** | New generalised tonic-clonic seizures in a woman with pre-eclampsia, with no other cause | Can occur antepartum, intrapartum or postpartum |
| **HELLP syndrome** | Haemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets | A severe variant of pre-eclampsia. BP may be only mildly raised and proteinuria may be absent |
| **White-coat hypertension** | High BP in clinic, normal at home/ambulatory | Still carries some increased risk |

### What counts as proteinuria?

| Test | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Urine dipstick | ≥2+ is significant; 1+ needs confirming (PCR or 24-h urine) |
| Spot urine protein:creatinine ratio (PCR) | ≥30 mg/mmol (≥0.3 mg/mg) |
| 24-hour urine protein | ≥300 mg/24 h |

### Features of severe disease ("pre-eclampsia with severe features")

Any **one** of the following makes it severe:

| System | Severe feature |
|---|---|
| Blood pressure | ≥160 systolic or ≥110 diastolic (confirmed within 15 minutes) |
| Neurological | Severe persistent headache, visual disturbance (blurring, flashing lights, scotomata, cortical blindness), altered consciousness, hyperreflexia with clonus, eclampsia, stroke |
| Respiratory | Pulmonary oedema (breathlessness, crackles, SpO₂ <95%) |
| Hepatic | Epigastric or right upper quadrant pain; transaminases ≥2× upper limit of normal (AST or ALT >70 IU/L) |
| Haematological | Platelets <100 × 10⁹/L; haemolysis; DIC |
| Renal | Creatinine >90–97 µmol/L (>1.1 mg/dL) or doubling; oliguria <30 mL/h (<500 mL/24 h) |
| Fetoplacental | Fetal growth restriction, abnormal umbilical artery Doppler, abruption, stillbirth |

> [!NOTE]
> Older Ugandan teaching (and some UCG tables) grade pre-eclampsia as "mild/moderate" versus "severe" using diastolic BP and the number of pluses of proteinuria. Modern guidance (ISSHP, ACOG, NICE) has dropped **amount of proteinuria** as a marker of severity. Heavy proteinuria still matters (it predicts earlier delivery), but a woman with 1+ protein and a platelet count of 60 has **severe** disease.

## Epidemiology & Uganda context

- Pre-eclampsia complicates roughly **3–8% of pregnancies** worldwide; hypertensive disorders together complicate about 10%.
- Worldwide, hypertensive disorders cause about **14% of maternal deaths**. In low-income settings, eclampsia is far commoner (up to 10 times) than in high-income countries, because women present late, ANC BP checks are missed, and MgSO₄ and antihypertensives are not always on the shelf.
- In **Uganda** (maternal mortality ratio about 189 per 100,000 live births, UDHS 2022), hypertensive disorders are the **second leading direct cause of maternal death after PPH** in MPDSR reports. Perinatal losses come from abruption, stillbirth, FGR and iatrogenic preterm birth.
- A national evaluation of emergency care for severe pre-eclampsia in Uganda found wide variation between facilities. Nifedipine was on the shelf in most HC III+ facilities (about 93%), but **IV/oral labetalol was essentially unavailable**, and the facilities that had protocols on the wall and regular drills delivered safer, faster care.
- A cohort at **Kawempe National Referral Hospital** confirmed that the **Pritchard (IM) regimen** is the standard of care in Uganda, and that giving IM maintenance doses on time is a real challenge on congested wards (missed doses are common).

> [!UGANDA]
> Facility realities you will meet: BP machines that are broken or have the wrong cuff size; urine dipsticks out of stock; MgSO₄ available but calcium gluconate missing; only one midwife for 30 mothers, so 4-hourly IM doses are missed; no ICU bed; and referral delays because of transport. Your job as a clerk is to **measure the BP properly, dip the urine, check the MgSO₄ chart and reflexes, keep the fluid balance chart** and escalate early.

## Pathophysiology

Understanding the mechanism makes every management step logical.

### Stage 1: abnormal placentation (first half of pregnancy, silent)

1. In normal pregnancy, **extravillous trophoblast** invades the decidua and the inner third of the myometrium and **remodels the maternal spiral arteries**. It destroys their muscular, elastic walls, turning them into wide, floppy, low-resistance channels that deliver a high volume of blood at low pressure to the intervillous space.
2. In pre-eclampsia this remodelling is **shallow and incomplete**. The myometrial segments stay narrow and muscular, so they can still constrict.
3. Result: **placental hypoperfusion**, intermittent ischaemia-reperfusion, and **oxidative stress** in the syncytiotrophoblast. (Immune maladaptation to paternal antigens is part of why **first pregnancies and new partners** carry higher risk.)

### Stage 2: maternal syndrome (after 20 weeks)

4. The stressed placenta releases **anti-angiogenic factors**, chiefly **soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1)** and **soluble endoglin**. These mop up the pro-angiogenic factors **VEGF** and **PlGF** (placental growth factor) that keep the endothelium healthy.
5. **Widespread endothelial dysfunction** follows:

| Endothelial change | Clinical consequence | Why it matters for management |
|---|---|---|
| ↓ Nitric oxide and prostacyclin, ↑ thromboxane, ↑ sensitivity to angiotensin II | **Vasoconstriction → hypertension** | Antihypertensives treat the number, not the disease |
| Leaky capillaries, low plasma oncotic pressure | **Oedema, pulmonary oedema**, yet the circulation is **volume-depleted** (haemoconcentration) | Restrict IV fluids (about 80 mL/h). Do not "fill up" for oliguria |
| Glomerular endotheliosis (swollen endothelial cells block the capillary lumen) | **Proteinuria**, ↓ GFR, rising uric acid and creatinine | Monitor urine output; adjust MgSO₄ (renally excreted) |
| Periportal haemorrhage and necrosis, liver capsule stretch | **Epigastric/RUQ pain**, ↑ AST/ALT, rarely **subcapsular haematoma/rupture** | Epigastric pain is a severe feature, not "gastritis" |
| Microangiopathy (fibrin on damaged endothelium) | **Haemolysis** (schistocytes, ↑ LDH) and **platelet consumption** → HELLP, DIC | Check platelets before spinal anaesthesia; watch for bleeding |
| Loss of cerebral autoregulation, vasogenic oedema (PRES) | **Headache, visual disturbance, hyperreflexia, eclampsia**, haemorrhagic stroke when systolic ≥160 | Control systolic BP; MgSO₄ stabilises neurons |
| Placental infarcts, spiral artery atherosis | **FGR, oligohydramnios, abruption, stillbirth** | Fetal surveillance; the fetus may dictate delivery |

6. **Why delivery cures it:** the source of sFlt-1 is the placenta. After delivery, levels fall over days, which is why the disease can **worsen in the first 48–72 hours postpartum** before it resolves.

### Why MgSO₄ works

MgSO₄ is **not** mainly an antihypertensive. It is a **calcium antagonist** that causes cerebral vasodilatation (relieving vasospasm), an **NMDA-receptor antagonist** that raises the seizure threshold, and it reduces blood-brain-barrier permeability (less cerebral oedema). The Magpie Trial showed it **halves the risk of eclampsia**, and the Collaborative Eclampsia Trial showed it is **better than diazepam or phenytoin** at preventing recurrent fits and maternal death.

### Why MgSO₄ can be toxic

Magnesium is excreted **only by the kidneys**. In pre-eclampsia the kidneys are often failing (oliguria), so magnesium accumulates. Toxicity follows a predictable ladder: **reflexes go first, then breathing, then the heart**. That is why we check **reflexes, respiratory rate and urine output** before every dose.

| Serum Mg²⁺ | Effect |
|---|---|
| 0.7–1.0 mmol/L | Normal |
| **2–3.5 mmol/L (4.8–8.4 mg/dL)** | **Therapeutic range** |
| 4–5 mmol/L (about 10–12 mg/dL) | **Loss of patellar reflexes** |
| 6–7.5 mmol/L (about 15–17 mg/dL) | **Respiratory depression / paralysis** |
| >12 mmol/L (>30 mg/dL) | **Cardiac arrest** |

### Risk factors (and why)

| High risk (any ONE → aspirin) | Moderate risk (≥2 → aspirin) |
|---|---|
| Pre-eclampsia in a previous pregnancy | First pregnancy |
| Chronic hypertension | Age ≥40 years |
| Chronic kidney disease | Pregnancy interval >10 years |
| Autoimmune disease (SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome) | BMI ≥35 kg/m² at booking |
| Type 1 or type 2 diabetes | Family history of pre-eclampsia (mother or sister) |
| | **Multiple pregnancy** (large placental mass) |

Other associations: molar pregnancy (pre-eclampsia before 20 weeks), IVF, new partner, sickle cell disease.

## Clinical acumen

### Focused history

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Gestational age (LNMP, early scan)? | Before 20 weeks think **molar pregnancy** or chronic hypertension. GA decides steroids and timing of delivery |
| **Headache?** Where, how severe, relieved by paracetamol? | Severe frontal/occipital headache not relieved by simple analgesia = **cerebral irritation → imminent eclampsia** |
| **Visual symptoms?** Blurring, flashing lights, spots, loss of vision | Occipital cortex oedema (PRES) or retinal vasospasm/detachment; a severe feature |
| **Epigastric or RUQ pain, nausea, vomiting?** | Liver capsule stretch → HELLP or subcapsular haematoma. Often misdiagnosed as "PUD/gastritis" |
| **Breathlessness, orthopnoea, cough with frothy sputum?** | Pulmonary oedema (especially after IV fluids or postpartum) |
| **Fetal movements?** | Reduced movements suggest FGR/hypoxia or IUFD |
| **Vaginal bleeding, abdominal pain, hard uterus?** | **Placental abruption** (common with pre-eclampsia) |
| Urine output? Swelling of face and hands? | Oliguria = renal involvement. Sudden facial oedema is a warning sign (though leg oedema alone is common and non-specific) |
| **Any fits or loss of consciousness?** Fever? | Eclampsia vs. **cerebral malaria**, meningitis, epilepsy |
| Past obstetric history: previous pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, abruption, stillbirth, early delivery? | Recurrence risk; shows how severe it was before |
| Chronic hypertension, kidney disease, diabetes, SLE, sickle cell, HIV? Medications? | Superimposed disease; drug interactions; methyldopa already started? |
| Did she attend ANC? Was BP ever checked? Aspirin or calcium given? | Explains late presentation; audit point |
| Asthma? | Avoid **labetalol** |
| Where does she live, how far from a theatre? | Decides whether she can go home (almost never with severe disease in Uganda) |

### Examination

1. **General**: conscious level (GCS), pallor, jaundice (haemolysis), **oedema of face, hands and sacrum**, bruising or bleeding from puncture sites (DIC).
2. **Blood pressure, measured properly**: seated or left lateral at 45°, correct cuff size (a large cuff for an arm >33 cm, otherwise the reading is falsely high), arm at heart level, **Korotkoff V** (disappearance) for diastolic. Repeat after 15 minutes if severe.
3. Pulse, respiratory rate, **SpO₂**, temperature.
4. **Chest**: basal crackles (pulmonary oedema).
5. **Abdomen**: fundal height (small-for-dates → FGR, oligohydramnios), **uterine tenderness/hardness** (abruption), **RUQ/epigastric tenderness** (liver), fetal lie and presentation, fetal heart rate.
6. **Neurological**: **patellar reflexes and clonus** (≥3 beats of ankle clonus = cerebral irritability), fundoscopy if possible (papilloedema, haemorrhages), focal deficits (stroke).
7. **Vaginal examination** only when planning delivery: Bishop score decides induction versus caesarean.
8. **Urine dipstick** for protein (every admission, every visit).

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - BP ≥160/110 (even once, if confirmed within 15 minutes)
> - Severe headache, visual disturbance, clonus → **imminent eclampsia**
> - Epigastric/RUQ pain, vomiting → HELLP or liver haematoma
> - Breathlessness, SpO₂ <95%, crackles → pulmonary oedema
> - Urine output <30 mL/h (or <100 mL in 4 h)
> - Platelets <100 × 10⁹/L, rising creatinine, rising AST/ALT
> - Vaginal bleeding with a tender, hard uterus → abruption
> - Reduced or absent fetal movements, abnormal FHR
> - Any seizure, drowsiness or confusion
> - Loss of patellar reflexes or RR <16/min on MgSO₄ → **toxicity**

### Thinking like a clinician

- **"A fit in pregnancy after 20 weeks is eclampsia until proven otherwise."** Give MgSO₄ first, then look for malaria, hypoglycaemia and meningitis in parallel. Do not waste the golden hour on a lumbar puncture.
- **BP can be "only" 140/90 in eclampsia and HELLP.** About 1 in 5 eclamptic women have BP below 140/90 at the time of the fit. Judge severity by **symptoms and bloods**, not the BP alone.
- **Oedema is not diagnostic** (most normal pregnant women have ankle oedema), but **sudden facial/hand oedema or rapid weight gain** is a warning.
- **Pre-eclampsia before 20 weeks → think molar pregnancy** (scan, β-hCG) or triploidy.
- **Epigastric pain in a hypertensive pregnant woman is liver involvement until proven otherwise.** Check LFTs and platelets. Antacids delay the diagnosis.
- **Oliguria in pre-eclampsia is usually due to renal vasospasm, not dehydration.** Aggressive fluid challenges drown the patient (pulmonary oedema). Give at most a small bolus (e.g. 250 mL once), then wait; the kidneys usually recover after delivery.
- **The treatment of pre-eclampsia is delivery; the question is always "when and how", balancing maternal risk against fetal prematurity.** At ≥37 weeks the balance always favours delivery.
- **Postpartum does not mean safe.** Many eclamptic fits and strokes occur in the first 48–72 hours after birth. Keep measuring BP, continue MgSO₄ for 24 hours and do not stop antihypertensives abruptly.
- **Common student mistakes:** forgetting to check the MgSO₄ monitoring chart; recording BP without checking the cuff size; calling 1+ protein "severe"; giving **ergometrine** for the third stage (it causes severe hypertension and stroke); forgetting fetal assessment; forgetting to plan **postnatal BP follow-up and aspirin in the next pregnancy**.

## Differential diagnosis

### Of hypertension in pregnancy

| Condition | Distinguishing features | Key investigation |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-eclampsia | New BP ≥140/90 after 20 weeks + proteinuria/organ/fetal involvement | Urine PCR/dipstick, FBC, LFT, creatinine, US |
| Gestational hypertension | New BP after 20 weeks, no proteinuria, normal bloods, normal growth | Same tests are normal; repeat weekly |
| Chronic hypertension | BP high before 20 weeks or at booking; may have target organ damage | Booking BP records, renal function, ECG |
| Molar pregnancy | Hypertension/pre-eclampsia before 20 weeks, uterus large for dates, hyperemesis, bleeding | Ultrasound ("snowstorm"), very high β-hCG |
| Renal disease (glomerulonephritis, lupus nephritis) | Proteinuria/haematuria before 20 weeks, active urinary sediment | Urine microscopy, creatinine, renal US |
| Phaeochromocytoma (rare) | Paroxysmal hypertension, palpitations, sweating, headaches | Urinary/plasma metanephrines |
| Thyrotoxicosis | Tachycardia, tremor, weight loss, goitre | TSH, free T4 |
| Pain or anxiety, wrong cuff size | BP normalises with rest and a correct cuff | Repeat BP properly |

### Of seizures in pregnancy (Uganda)

| Condition | Distinguishing features | Key investigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Eclampsia** | Hypertension, proteinuria, after 20 weeks or within 6 weeks postpartum | BP, urine protein |
| **Cerebral malaria** | Fever, rigors, anaemia, hypoglycaemia; BP usually normal | **mRDT / blood smear**, glucose |
| Hypoglycaemia | Sweating, known diabetes, malaria on quinine, sepsis | **Bedside glucose** |
| Epilepsy | Known history, missed antiepileptic drugs, normal BP | History, drug levels |
| Meningitis / encephalitis | Fever, neck stiffness, photophobia, HIV | LP after stabilisation, CSF CrAg if HIV+ |
| Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis | Postpartum, severe headache, focal signs, papilloedema | CT/MR venography |
| Stroke (haemorrhagic) | Sudden severe headache, focal deficit, very high systolic BP | CT head |
| HIV-related CNS disease (toxoplasma, cryptococcus) | HIV+, low CD4, subacute headache | CrAg, CT |
| Local anaesthetic toxicity, hyponatraemia | After spinal/lignocaine; after large oxytocin infusions in dextrose | Drug history, sodium |

### Of RUQ pain with abnormal liver tests (the HELLP differential)

| Condition | Distinguishing features | Key investigation |
|---|---|---|
| **HELLP** | Hypertension (may be mild), haemolysis, low platelets, AST ↑ | FBC + film, LDH, LFT |
| **Acute fatty liver of pregnancy** | Third trimester, vomiting, jaundice, **hypoglycaemia**, coagulopathy, renal failure; less hypertension | Glucose, PT/INR, bilirubin, ammonia |
| Viral hepatitis (A, B, E) | Very high transaminases (often >1000), jaundice, exposure | Hepatitis serology |
| Severe malaria with haemolysis | Fever, jaundice, anaemia, thrombocytopenia | mRDT/smear |
| Cholecystitis / cholangitis | Fever, Murphy's sign, gallstones | Ultrasound |
| TTP / HUS | Severe thrombocytopenia, haemolysis, neurological signs, renal failure out of proportion; LFTs mild | ADAMTS-13 (rarely available), film |

## Investigations

| Test | What you are looking for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Urine dipstick** (every visit) | Protein ≥2+ (1+ needs confirming) | Diagnosis |
| Urine PCR or 24-h protein | ≥30 mg/mmol or ≥300 mg/24 h | Confirms proteinuria |
| **FBC** | **Platelets <100**, Hb (haemoconcentration or haemolysis) | Severity, HELLP, safety of spinal anaesthesia |
| Blood film | Schistocytes (fragmented red cells) | Microangiopathic haemolysis |
| **LFTs** (AST, ALT, bilirubin) and **LDH** | AST/ALT >70 IU/L, LDH ≥600 IU/L | HELLP, liver involvement |
| **Creatinine, urea, electrolytes** | Creatinine >90 µmol/L | Renal involvement; MgSO₄ dosing |
| Uric acid | Raised | Supports diagnosis; weak predictor |
| Clotting (PT, APTT, fibrinogen) or bedside clotting time | Prolonged; fibrinogen low | If platelets low, abruption, or before surgery |
| **Blood group & cross-match** | | Ready for delivery/PPH |
| Random blood sugar, **mRDT/blood smear** | Hypoglycaemia, malaria | Fits differential; AFLP |
| **Obstetric ultrasound** | Growth (EFW), liquor volume, placenta (abruption), presentation | FGR and timing of delivery |
| Umbilical artery Doppler (where available) | Raised resistance, absent/reversed end-diastolic flow | Fetal compromise → timing |
| CTG / intermittent auscultation | Reduced variability, decelerations | Fetal well-being |
| sFlt-1/PlGF ratio (not routinely available in Uganda) | Ratio <38 rules out pre-eclampsia for 1 week | Triage of suspected cases |
| Chest X-ray / SpO₂ | Pulmonary oedema | If breathless |
| CT head | Haemorrhage, CVT | Focal signs, atypical fits, fit after 48 h postpartum |

## Management

Management rests on **five pillars**: (1) control severe hypertension, (2) prevent/treat seizures with MgSO₄, (3) careful fluid balance, (4) assess mother and fetus, and (5) decide the timing and mode of delivery. Then (6) care for her postpartum and plan for the future.

### 1. Prevention (at ANC)

| Intervention | Who | Dose and timing |
|---|---|---|
| **Low-dose aspirin** | One high-risk factor, or two or more moderate risk factors | **75–150 mg orally once daily at night**, start at **12 weeks** (ideally before 16, and before 20 weeks at the latest), continue to **36 weeks** |
| **Calcium supplementation** | All pregnant women in populations with low dietary calcium (most of Uganda) | **1.5–2.0 g elemental calcium daily** in 3 divided doses from 20 weeks to delivery (WHO). Take it apart from iron tablets (calcium blocks iron absorption) |
| BP and urine at every ANC visit | Everyone | Early detection |
| Health education on danger signs | Everyone | Headache, blurred vision, epigastric pain, fits, swelling of the face → come immediately |

> [!UGANDA]
> In Uganda, aspirin is available mainly as 75 mg tablets (many specialists give 2 × 75 mg = 150 mg at night for high-risk women, following FIGO/ASPRE). Calcium is not yet reliably supplied through the public ANC package, so counsel on dietary calcium (milk, *mukene*/silverfish, dark green leaves) and prescribe it where the family can buy it.

### 2. Gestational hypertension and pre-eclampsia WITHOUT severe features

1. **Admit** at least for initial assessment (in Uganda most units admit all women with pre-eclampsia, because follow-up and transport are unreliable).
2. BP 4-hourly, urine protein daily, fetal movements chart, FHR every shift.
3. Bloods (FBC, LFT, creatinine) at diagnosis and then **twice weekly**; ultrasound for growth and liquor every 2 weeks.
4. Start an **oral antihypertensive if BP is persistently ≥140/90** (CHAP trial and NICE: target about **135/85**; do not drop diastolic below 80).
5. No MgSO₄ routinely (unless severe features appear or during labour/after delivery per local protocol).
6. **Plan delivery at 37+0 weeks** (HYPITAT trial: induction at ≥37 weeks reduces maternal complications without increasing CS). Deliver earlier if severe features, fetal compromise or maternal request after counselling.

#### Oral (maintenance) antihypertensives

| Drug | Dose | Route | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Methyldopa** | 250 mg, increase to 500–750 mg | Oral | 8-hourly (max 3 g/day) | Centrally acting; safest long-term data. **Slow onset (hours to days): never for acute control**. Side effects: sedation, **depression**, postural hypotension, positive Coombs, hepatitis. Switch to another agent after delivery |
| **Nifedipine modified-release (retard)** | 20 mg, increase to 40 mg | Oral | 12-hourly (max 120 mg/day) | Calcium-channel blocker. Headache, flushing, ankle oedema. Widely available in Uganda |
| **Labetalol** | 100–200 mg, up to 400 mg | Oral | 8–12-hourly (max 2.4 g/day) | α + β blocker. First line in the UK. **Avoid in asthma**. Scarce in Uganda |
| Amlodipine | 5–10 mg | Oral | Once daily | Alternative calcium-channel blocker |

> [!DRUG]
> **Contraindicated in pregnancy**: ACE inhibitors (enalapril, captopril) and ARBs (losartan) cause fetal renal failure, oligohydramnios and skull defects; stop them and switch before or as soon as pregnancy is confirmed. **Atenolol** is linked to FGR. **Diuretics** reduce plasma volume and are used only for pulmonary oedema. ACE inhibitors become **first choice again after delivery** (enalapril is compatible with breastfeeding).

### 3. Pre-eclampsia WITH severe features: step by step

1. **Call for help**: senior midwife, medical officer or obstetrician, and the anaesthetist if delivery is likely.
2. **Admit to a high-dependency bed** close to the nursing station, in a quiet room. Left lateral position.
3. **IV access** with a large-bore cannula (16–18 G). Draw FBC, LFT, LDH, creatinine, clotting, group & cross-match, glucose and mRDT.
4. **Control BP within 30–60 minutes** if ≥160/110 (see the table below). Aim for **140–150 / 90–100**. Avoid a precipitous fall, which reduces placental perfusion and causes fetal distress.
5. **Give MgSO₄** (Pritchard loading dose, or Zuspan where infusion pumps are available).
6. **Insert a Foley catheter** and start a strict **input/output chart**; urine output hourly.
7. **Restrict fluids**: total IV plus oral intake about **80 mL/h (1 mL/kg/h)**. No routine fluid boluses.
8. **Assess the fetus**: FHR, ultrasound for growth, liquor and presentation; Doppler if available.
9. If **24 (Uganda: 28) to 34 weeks**, give **antenatal corticosteroids** (dexamethasone 6 mg IM 12-hourly × 4), but **do not delay delivery for steroids** when the mother is unstable.
10. **Plan delivery** (see timing below). Inform the neonatal team.
11. Monitoring every 15–30 minutes until stable, then hourly: BP, pulse, RR, SpO₂, reflexes, urine output, symptoms, FHR.

#### Acute antihypertensives for severe hypertension (≥160/110)

| Drug | Dose | Route | Repeat | Maximum and notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nifedipine immediate-release** | **10 mg** | **Oral (swallow; do not bite or give sublingually)** | Recheck BP after 20–30 min; repeat 10–20 mg if BP still ≥160/110 | Up to about 30–40 mg in the first hour; then continue MR 20 mg 12-hourly. First choice where there is no IV access or pump; most available in Uganda. Headache and tachycardia |
| **Hydralazine** | **5 mg** (10 mg if very high) | **IV slowly over 2–5 min** | Check BP every 5 min for 20 min; repeat 5–10 mg every 20–30 min | Max **20 mg IV** per episode (WHO alternative: 12.5 mg IM every 2 h). Dilute 20 mg ampoule in 10–20 mL water → 1 mg per 1 mL. Side effects: tachycardia, headache (mimics imminent eclampsia), sudden hypotension → fetal distress |
| **Labetalol** | **20 mg** | **IV over 2 min** | Then 40 mg, 80 mg, 80 mg every 10 min if needed | Max **300 mg**; or infusion 20–160 mg/h. Avoid in asthma and heart failure. Rarely available in Ugandan public facilities |

> [!DRUG]
> Methyldopa has **no role** in acute severe hypertension (onset 4–6 hours). Give nifedipine IR or hydralazine, then add methyldopa or nifedipine MR for maintenance. Nifedipine and MgSO₄ can be given together; the feared interaction (profound hypotension/neuromuscular block) is rare in practice. Just monitor.

> [!UGANDA]
> The common Ugandan combination on the labour ward is **hydralazine IV (or nifedipine oral) for acute control + MgSO₄ Pritchard + methyldopa ± nifedipine retard for maintenance**. Where hydralazine is out of stock, oral nifedipine is as effective (RCT evidence shows nifedipine, labetalol and hydralazine are all acceptable). Follow your unit protocol.

### 4. Magnesium sulphate: indications, regimens, monitoring

**Indications:** eclampsia (treatment); pre-eclampsia with severe features (prevention); and many units also give it intrapartum and for 24 h postpartum to all women with pre-eclampsia. It is also used for **fetal neuroprotection before 32 weeks** (see the preterm labour topic).

#### Preparations (know these by heart)

- MgSO₄ comes as **50% solution** (5 g in 10 mL, i.e. 0.5 g per mL). Check the ampoule strength: 10 mL of 50% = 5 g.
- **20% solution for IV use** = 4 g (8 mL of 50%) + 12 mL of water for injection or normal saline = **20 mL of 20%**.

#### Regimens

| Regimen | Loading dose | Maintenance | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pritchard (IM), the standard in Uganda** | **4 g of 20% IV slowly over 5–10 min** (20 mL) **PLUS** **10 g of 50% IM**: 5 g (10 mL) deep IM into **each upper outer buttock**, each with **1 mL of 2% lignocaine** in the same syringe. Total loading = **14 g** | **5 g of 50% IM** (+ 1 mL 2% lignocaine) **every 4 hours in alternate buttocks** | **24 hours after delivery or after the last fit, whichever is later** |
| **Zuspan (IV)**, where infusion pumps and staff allow | **4 g IV over 5–20 min** (some protocols 20–30 min) | **1 g/hour by continuous IV infusion** (e.g. 5 g in 250 mL normal saline at 50 mL/h via pump) | Same: 24 h after delivery or last fit |
| **Recurrent fit on MgSO₄** | **2 g of 20% IV over 5 min** (4 g if weight >70 kg per some protocols), in addition to the regimen | Continue | Look for another cause if fits persist (CT) |
| **At HC II/III before referral** | Give the **loading dose** (IM 10 g ± IV 4 g if trained) | Refer immediately with an escort, the treatment sheet and the next dose (5 g IM) | Next dose due 4 h after loading, even on the road |

> [!DRUG]
> **Checks before EVERY maintenance dose (and hourly on an infusion):**
> 1. **Respiratory rate ≥16/min**
> 2. **Patellar reflexes present** (use biceps reflex if she has had spinal/epidural anaesthesia)
> 3. **Urine output ≥30 mL/h** (≥100 mL in the previous 4 hours)
>
> - **RR <16 or reflexes absent** → **withhold** the dose and call for help.
> - **Urine <30 mL/h** → withhold further IM doses (or halve the infusion to 0.5 g/h), check creatinine, and resume when urine output improves.
> - **Respiratory arrest** → ventilate with bag and mask, give **calcium gluconate 1 g (10 mL of 10%) IV slowly over 10 minutes**, stop MgSO₄.
> - Keep **calcium gluconate drawn up/at the bedside** throughout.

**Side effects to warn about:** warmth and flushing during the IV loading (normal), nausea, and pain at the IM site (hence lignocaine). The neonate may be floppy (hypotonia), so tell the resuscitation team.

#### If MgSO₄ is not available

**Diazepam** (inferior; use only if no MgSO₄): 10 mg IV slowly over 2 minutes, repeat once if fits recur; maintenance 40 mg in 500 mL normal saline titrated to keep her sedated but rousable (max 100 mg in 24 h). Risk of respiratory depression in mother and neonate. Rectal diazepam 20 mg if no IV access.

### 5. Eclampsia: emergency algorithm

1. **Call for help. Do not leave her alone.** Note the time.
2. **Protect from injury** (sides of bed up, remove sharp objects). **Do not restrain** her. **Do not put anything in her mouth.**
3. After the fit: **left lateral (recovery) position**, clear the airway, **suction**, give **oxygen 4–6 L/min** by mask.
4. Check **breathing and pulse**. Bag-mask ventilation if not breathing.
5. **IV access** (16–18 G); take bloods (FBC, LFT, creatinine, clotting, glucose, mRDT, group & cross-match).
6. **MgSO₄ loading dose** immediately (Pritchard 4 g IV + 10 g IM), then maintenance.
7. **Check blood glucose** (hypoglycaemia) and **mRDT** (malaria). Treat what you find.
8. **Treat BP** if ≥160/110 (nifedipine IR 10 mg oral once she can swallow safely, otherwise hydralazine 5 mg IV).
9. **Catheterise**, strict input/output, **restrict fluids** to 80 mL/h.
10. **Assess the fetus.** Expect fetal bradycardia during and just after a fit. It usually recovers within 3–5 minutes once the mother is oxygenated. **Do not rush to theatre during the bradycardia; resuscitate the mother first.**
11. **Deliver within 12 hours** of the fit once she is stable (see below), regardless of gestation.
12. Monitor: BP, pulse, RR, SpO₂, reflexes, urine output, GCS every 15–30 min; and document everything on an eclampsia chart.
13. Postpartum: HDU/ICU if unconscious; continue MgSO₄ for 24 h after delivery or last fit.

### 6. HELLP syndrome

1. Treat as **severe pre-eclampsia**: MgSO₄, BP control, fluid restriction.
2. **Deliver**: immediately if ≥34 weeks, DIC, liver haematoma, abruption or fetal compromise. Between 24/28 and 34 weeks, deliver after steroids (within 24–48 h) if the mother is stable.
3. **Platelets**:
   - Transfuse platelets if <20 × 10⁹/L, or **<50 × 10⁹/L before caesarean** or if actively bleeding.
   - Spinal anaesthesia is generally safe with platelets **≥75–80 × 10⁹/L** and a stable count. Below that, general anaesthesia (with careful blunting of the intubation pressor response).
4. Correct coagulopathy with **fresh frozen plasma**; fibrinogen with cryoprecipitate if available.
5. Watch for **liver haematoma/rupture** (shoulder-tip pain, shock): ultrasound, surgical team.
6. Dexamethasone to improve the platelet count is **not** recommended routinely (no outcome benefit), only for fetal lung maturity.
7. Labs 6–12-hourly until improving; the nadir is usually 24–48 h postpartum.

### 7. Timing and mode of delivery

| Situation | Timing |
|---|---|
| **Eclampsia** | Deliver **within 12 hours** of the fit, after stabilisation |
| Severe pre-eclampsia **≥34 weeks** | Deliver once stable (within 24 h) |
| Severe pre-eclampsia **<34 weeks** (viable) | Stabilise, give steroids, and deliver after 48 h if stable, **or immediately** if: uncontrollable BP despite 3 drugs, eclampsia, pulmonary oedema, HELLP/DIC, rising creatinine, abruption, non-reassuring fetal status, IUFD. Expectant management only in a referral centre with NICU and daily review |
| Severe pre-eclampsia **before viability** (Uganda <28 weeks) | Counsel; maternal life comes first. Termination of pregnancy is usually advised |
| Pre-eclampsia without severe features **<37 weeks** | Expectant management with monitoring; deliver at 37+0 or earlier if it becomes severe |
| Pre-eclampsia without severe features **≥37 weeks** | **Deliver** (induction) |
| Gestational hypertension, well controlled | Deliver at 37–39 weeks |
| Chronic hypertension, well controlled | Deliver at 37–39 weeks |

**Mode of delivery:**
- **Vaginal delivery is preferred** if there are no obstetric contraindications: less blood loss and no anaesthetic risk.
- Cervix favourable (Bishop ≥6–8): **ARM + oxytocin**.
- Cervix unfavourable: **misoprostol 25 mcg vaginally every 6 h** or **25 mcg orally every 2 h**, or a Foley balloon catheter.
- **Caesarean section** if: usual obstetric indications; fetal distress; the cervix is very unfavourable and delivery is not expected within 12 h (eclampsia) or 24 h (severe pre-eclampsia); severe disease far from term with a compromised fetus.
- **Second stage**: consider **shortening it with vacuum** if BP is uncontrolled or she is exhausted (bearing down raises BP and intracranial pressure).
- **Third stage**: **oxytocin 10 IU IM** is the uterotonic. **NEVER ergometrine or Syntometrine**: it causes vasoconstriction, a hypertensive crisis and stroke.
- **Anaesthesia**: spinal is preferred (platelets ≥75–80). General anaesthesia risks a hypertensive surge at intubation (stroke) and a difficult airway (laryngeal oedema).

### 8. Postpartum care

1. **Continue MgSO₄ for 24 hours** after delivery (or after the last fit).
2. **BP 4-hourly** (more often if severe) for at least 3 days. BP typically peaks on **days 3–6**.
3. Continue antihypertensives and **wean slowly** as BP falls. After delivery:
   - Switch **methyldopa** to another agent within 2 days (risk of **postnatal depression**).
   - First choices postnatally: **nifedipine MR 20 mg 12-hourly**, **amlodipine 5–10 mg daily**, or **enalapril 5–20 mg daily** (safe in breastfeeding; check creatinine and potassium).
   - Target <140/90.
4. Continue **fluid balance charting** until urine output normalises (≥0.5 mL/kg/h). Watch for **pulmonary oedema** (mobilisation of oedema fluid). If it develops: sit up, oxygen, **furosemide 20–40 mg IV**, stop IV fluids.
5. Repeat **FBC, LFT and creatinine** 24–48 h after delivery (HELLP can appear or worsen postpartum).
6. **Analgesia**: paracetamol is fine. NSAIDs are best avoided if there is renal impairment, oliguria or low platelets (otherwise ACOG considers them acceptable).
7. **Thromboprophylaxis** (enoxaparin) if CS, prolonged immobility or other risk factors, once platelets are safe.
8. **Discharge** when BP <150/100 on oral drugs, symptoms have resolved and bloods are improving. **Review BP within 1 week**, then at 6 weeks.
9. **Future counselling**:
   - **Recurrence** about 15% (up to 25–65% after early severe disease or HELLP).
   - **Aspirin 75–150 mg from 12 weeks** in every future pregnancy.
   - **Lifelong cardiovascular risk** (double the risk of hypertension, heart disease and stroke): annual BP checks.
   - Refer if hypertension/proteinuria persists beyond 6–12 weeks (chronic hypertension or renal disease).
10. **Contraception**: progestogen-only methods, IUD and implants are safe. **Avoid combined oestrogen methods** while hypertensive (UKMEC/WHO MEC category 3–4).

### 9. What to do where (Uganda levels of care)

| Level | What to do |
|---|---|
| **HC II/III** | Detect (BP + urine at every ANC visit). For severe features or eclampsia: MgSO₄ **loading dose**, first dose of antihypertensive (nifedipine IR 10 mg), catheterise if possible, **refer immediately** to HC IV/hospital with a health worker, notes and the next MgSO₄ dose |
| **HC IV / general hospital** | Full MgSO₄ regimen, IV hydralazine or oral nifedipine, steroids, basic labs (FBC, dipstick, clotting time), induction or **caesarean section**, HDU-type monitoring. Refer if HELLP with bleeding, unconscious after eclampsia, pulmonary oedema needing ventilation, or <34 weeks needing NICU |
| **Regional / national referral** (Mengo, Mulago, Kawempe) | ICU/HDU, blood products (platelets, FFP), CT, NICU for preterm babies, expectant management of early-onset disease, anaesthetic expertise |

## Complications

| Maternal | Fetal / neonatal |
|---|---|
| Eclampsia; **intracerebral haemorrhage** (the leading cause of death); PRES | **Fetal growth restriction**, oligohydramnios |
| **Pulmonary oedema**, ARDS | **Placental abruption** → hypoxia, IUFD |
| HELLP, DIC, liver haematoma/rupture | **Iatrogenic prematurity** (RDS, IVH, NEC, sepsis) |
| Acute kidney injury | Stillbirth |
| Placental abruption, PPH | Neonatal hypotonia/respiratory depression from MgSO₄ |
| Cortical blindness, retinal detachment (usually reversible) | Birth asphyxia |
| Aspiration pneumonia (after fits) | |
| Long-term: chronic hypertension, ischaemic heart disease, stroke, CKD | Long-term: cardiometabolic risk of FGR |

## Ward-round & exam pearls

### Model case presentation

> "Mrs N.A., a 19-year-old primigravida at 35 weeks by a 12-week scan, admitted 6 hours ago from Kasangati HC IV with **pre-eclampsia with severe features**: BP 170/115, severe frontal headache and blurred vision, and 3+ proteinuria. She received a Pritchard loading dose of MgSO₄ at 10:00 and hydralazine 5 mg IV twice. Currently BP 150/100, RR 18, reflexes present, urine output 40 mL/h, SpO₂ 97%. No clonus. Platelets 140, AST 45, creatinine 70. The fetus is cephalic, FHR 142, EFW 2.1 kg (10th centile). Bishop score 4. **Plan**: continue MgSO₄ with 4-hourly monitoring, nifedipine MR 20 mg 12-hourly, cervical ripening with misoprostol 25 mcg for delivery within 24 h, oxytocin (not ergometrine) for the third stage, and continue MgSO₄ for 24 h postpartum."

### Viva questions (with answers)

1. **What are the monitoring parameters for MgSO₄?** RR ≥16, patellar reflexes present, urine ≥30 mL/h; serum Mg if available (2–3.5 mmol/L). Antidote: calcium gluconate 1 g IV over 10 min.
2. **Why lignocaine with the IM MgSO₄?** The 50% solution is very painful. 1 mL of 2% lignocaine in the same syringe reduces the pain.
3. **Why no ergometrine?** It causes smooth-muscle vasoconstriction → a sudden rise in BP → stroke or eclampsia.
4. **Why restrict fluids when she is oliguric?** Capillary leak and low oncotic pressure mean fluids go into the lungs, not the circulation. Oliguria reflects renal vasospasm; it resolves after delivery.
5. **When is proteinuria not needed to diagnose pre-eclampsia?** When there is new hypertension plus organ dysfunction (low platelets, raised creatinine/transaminases, neurological symptoms, pulmonary oedema) or FGR.
6. **What is the pathophysiological basis?** Failed trophoblast invasion/spiral artery remodelling → placental ischaemia → sFlt-1/soluble endoglin → systemic endothelial dysfunction.
7. **How long is MgSO₄ continued?** 24 hours after delivery or the last fit, whichever is later.
8. **Differential of a fit at 34 weeks in Uganda?** Eclampsia, cerebral malaria, hypoglycaemia, epilepsy, meningitis, CVT/stroke, HIV-related CNS infection.
9. **What does HELLP stand for, and what are the thresholds?** Haemolysis (LDH ≥600), Elevated Liver enzymes (AST ≥70), Low Platelets (<100).
10. **What is the treatment target for BP?** 140–150/90–100 acutely (NICE target 135/85 for maintenance); avoid a precipitous fall.

### OSCE tips

- **BP measurement station**: explain, seat her or position left lateral at 45°, choose the correct cuff, palpate the systolic first, use **Korotkoff V**, repeat, interpret, and **always dip the urine**.
- **MgSO₄ preparation station**: state the strengths aloud (50% = 0.5 g/mL); make up 20% (8 mL of 50% + 12 mL diluent); give 5 g IM + 1 mL 2% lignocaine per buttock; state the monitoring and the antidote.
- **Counselling station**: explain pre-eclampsia in plain words ("the placenta is not getting enough blood and is releasing substances that affect your blood vessels"), why delivery is needed, danger signs, aspirin next time, and the long-term heart risk.

## Mnemonics & memory aids

> [!EXAM]
> **Severe features: the "7 P's"**: **P**ressure ≥160/110, **P**ain in the head (headache), **P**hotopsia (visual disturbance), **P**ain in the epigastrium/RUQ, **P**ulmonary oedema, **P**latelets <100, **P**ee (oliguria or rising creatinine).

- **MgSO₄ monitoring: "R-R-U"**: **R**espiratory rate ≥16, **R**eflexes present, **U**rine ≥30 mL/h. And **"C for Calcium, C for Crisis"**.
- **Toxicity ladder: "Knees, then Breathing, then Heart"**: reflexes lost (4–5 mmol/L) → respiratory depression (6–7.5) → cardiac arrest (>12).
- **Pritchard = "4 + 5 + 5 then 5 every 4"**: 4 g IV, 5 g IM each buttock, then 5 g IM every 4 h.
- **Acute antihypertensives: "No Hurry? Labetalol"** - **N**ifedipine, **H**ydralazine, **L**abetalol. **Methyldopa is "Maintenance only"**.
- **Drugs to avoid: "ACE, ARB, Atenolol, Ergometrine, Excess fluids"**.
- **Eclampsia delivery clock**: "**12 hours** for a fit, **24 hours** for severe, **37 weeks** for mild".
