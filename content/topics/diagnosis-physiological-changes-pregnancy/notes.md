title: Diagnosis of Pregnancy & Physiological Changes
summary: Pregnancy is diagnosed from symptoms (amenorrhoea, nausea, breast changes), signs (uterine enlargement, Hegar's, Chadwick's, fetal heart) and tests (hCG, ultrasound). The mother's body then adapts in every system, including a 40–50% rise in plasma volume, a 30–50% rise in cardiac output, respiratory alkalosis, a 50% rise in GFR, a hypercoagulable state and insulin resistance. Knowing these normal changes lets you tell physiology from disease and explains why pregnant women bleed, clot, desaturate and decompensate differently.
highYield:
  - Urine hCG is positive from about the time of the missed period. Serum β-hCG is detectable 8–11 days after conception, doubles about every 48 hours early on and peaks at 8–10 weeks.
  - On transvaginal ultrasound a gestational sac is seen at about 4.5–5 weeks, a yolk sac at about 5.5 weeks and cardiac activity at about 6 weeks. The discriminatory hCG zone is about 1,500–3,500 IU/L.
  - Positive (certain) signs are fetal heart sounds, fetal parts or movements felt by the examiner, and the fetus seen on ultrasound. Everything else is presumptive or probable.
  - Plasma volume rises 40–50% but red cell mass only 20–30%, so there is physiological haemodilution. Hb below 11 g/dL is still anaemia.
  - Cardiac output rises 30–50% (stroke volume, then heart rate up by 10–20 bpm). SVR falls, so BP dips in the second trimester. The supine gravid uterus compresses the IVC from about 20 weeks, so tilt her left.
  - Minute ventilation rises about 40–50% through tidal volume (progesterone), PaCO₂ falls to about 4 kPa (30 mmHg) and FRC falls about 20%. A pregnant woman desaturates fast.
  - GFR and renal plasma flow rise about 50%, so a 'normal' creatinine of 90 µmol/L in pregnancy is actually abnormal.
  - Pregnancy is hypercoagulable (fibrinogen and factors VII, VIII and X up; protein S down), so VTE risk is about 5-fold, highest postpartum.
  - Do a urine hCG in every woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain, bleeding, collapse, or before a drug, X-ray or operation. Positive hCG + pain + shock means ruptured ectopic until proven otherwise.
  - "Pregnancy normal ranges to quote: creatinine 35–70 µmol/L, PaCO₂ about 4 kPa, HCO₃⁻ 18–22 mmol/L, fibrinogen 4–6 g/L, ALP up 2–4 times, albumin 28–37 g/L."
  - Never physiological in pregnancy are RR above 20/min, SpO₂ below 95%, resting HR above 100, a diastolic murmur, raised ALT/AST and unilateral leg swelling.
  - A pregnant woman can lose up to about 30% of her blood volume before BP falls. Shock index (HR ÷ SBP) 0.9 or more is worrying and 1.4 or more is critical.

## In a nutshell

This lecture has two halves that belong together.

1. **Diagnosing pregnancy** is the first clinical decision in obstetrics. Every woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain, bleeding, collapse or who needs a drug or X-ray **is pregnant until proven otherwise**. In Uganda, a urine hCG test costs very little and prevents missed ectopic pregnancies, teratogenic prescriptions and wrong diagnoses.
2. **Physiological changes** are the body's preparation to grow a fetus and survive delivery: more blood, more cardiac output, more ventilation, more clotting, more insulin resistance. These changes are why pregnant women **tolerate haemorrhage until they suddenly crash**, why they **desaturate quickly** during eclampsia or anaesthesia, why **VTE** is a leading killer, and why normal laboratory values differ.

The one thing never to forget: **"normal" values change in pregnancy.** A creatinine, PaCO₂ or heart rate that is normal for a non-pregnant woman may be a warning sign in a pregnant one, and vice versa, since a lower Hb and higher WCC can be physiological.

## Definitions & classification

### Signs and symptoms of pregnancy: the three classic categories

| Category | Examples | Why not certain? |
|---|---|---|
| **Presumptive (mostly symptoms)** | Amenorrhoea; nausea/vomiting ("morning sickness", from about 6 weeks, peaking at 9–12 weeks); breast tenderness and enlargement; urinary frequency; fatigue; quickening (the first fetal movements the mother feels, at **18–20 weeks in a primigravida** and **16–18 weeks in a multigravida**); skin changes (chloasma, linea nigra, striae) | Can occur with stress, hormonal contraception, thyroid disease, hyperprolactinaemia, pseudocyesis, bowel gas |
| **Probable (signs found by the examiner)** | Uterine enlargement; **Hegar's sign** (softening of the isthmus, 6–10 weeks); **Goodell's sign** (softening of the cervix, about 6–8 weeks); **Chadwick's sign** (bluish/purple discolouration of the vagina and cervix from congestion, 6–8 weeks); **Osiander's sign** (increased pulsation in the lateral fornices); ballottement (about 16–20 weeks); Braxton Hicks contractions; **positive hCG test** | Fibroids, molar pregnancy, pelvic congestion, choriocarcinoma or hCG-secreting tumours can mimic them |
| **Positive (certain)** | **Fetal heart** heard (Doppler from about 10–12 weeks; Pinard from about 20 weeks); **fetal parts or movements felt by the examiner** (about 20 weeks); **fetus seen on ultrasound** | These come only from the fetus |

### Dating terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Gestational age (GA)** | Weeks from the first day of the LNMP. About 2 weeks more than the **conceptional (embryonic) age** |
| **Naegele's rule** | EDD = LNMP **+ 7 days − 3 months + 1 year** (the same as + 9 months + 7 days, or + 280 days). Adjust for a longer cycle: add (cycle length − 28) days |
| **Trimesters** | First up to 13+6 weeks; second 14–27+6 weeks; third 28 weeks to delivery |
| **Embryonic vs fetal period** | Embryo until 10 weeks' GA (8 weeks post-conception): organogenesis, the most teratogen-sensitive time. Fetus after that |

## Epidemiology & Uganda context

- Uganda has one of the highest fertility rates in the world (**about 4.5 children per woman**, UDHS 2022) and many unintended pregnancies, especially among adolescents (about 1 in 4 girls aged 15–19 has begun childbearing).
- **Late recognition of pregnancy** drives late ANC booking. Many women attend first in the second trimester, which means late dating, missed early folate and missed early risk assessment.
- **Ectopic pregnancy** is a common surgical emergency in Ugandan hospitals and often presents late, ruptured, with haemoperitoneum. A negative-until-proven urine hCG in any woman with lower abdominal pain is lifesaving.
- **Ultrasound** is widely available in towns (private clinics, HC IV and hospitals) but less so rurally. Clinical skills (bimanual size, fundal height, fetal heart with a Pinard or Doppler) still matter.
- Background anaemia, malaria, HIV and rheumatic heart disease interact with the physiological changes. A woman with **mitral stenosis** may first decompensate at **28–32 weeks** (when cardiac output nears its peak) or **immediately after delivery** (autotransfusion).

## Physiology

### Hormones driving the changes

| Hormone | Source | Key effects |
|---|---|---|
| **hCG** | Syncytiotrophoblast | Keeps the **corpus luteum** alive (progesterone until the placenta takes over at about 8–10 weeks); peaks at 8–10 weeks (about 100,000 IU/L), then falls to a plateau. Has **TSH-like activity** (transient low TSH, hyperemesis). Very high in **molar pregnancy and twins** |
| **Progesterone** | Corpus luteum, then placenta | **Smooth-muscle relaxation** (GI, ureters, veins, uterus, the "pro-gestation" hormone that keeps the uterus quiet); raises the respiratory centre's sensitivity to CO₂ (hyperventilation); raises body temperature by about 0.5 °C |
| **Oestrogens** (mostly oestriol) | Placenta (with fetal adrenal and liver precursors) | Uterine growth and blood flow; breast ductal growth; **raises clotting factors, TBG and cortisol-binding globulin**; skin changes |
| **hPL** (human placental lactogen) | Placenta | **Insulin resistance** (diverts glucose to the fetus), lipolysis |
| **Relaxin** | Corpus luteum, placenta | Ligament laxity (pelvic joints, symphysis), cervical remodelling |
| **Prolactin** | Anterior pituitary (which enlarges) | Prepares the breast for lactation (milk is held back by high progesterone until the placenta is delivered) |

### Cardiovascular system

| Parameter | Change | Why / clinical relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Cardiac output** | **Up 30–50%**, starting by 5–8 weeks and near peak by about 24–28 weeks | Stroke volume up first, then heart rate. Further **up to 60–80% in labour and immediately after delivery** (autotransfusion of about 500 mL from the contracting uterus), which is the danger time for cardiac disease |
| **Heart rate** | **Up 10–20 bpm** | A resting HR above 100 bpm is not normal and needs explaining (bleeding, sepsis, anaemia, PE, thyroid disease) |
| **Stroke volume** | Up 20–30% | Increased preload from volume expansion |
| **Systemic vascular resistance** | **Down about 20–30%** | Progesterone, nitric oxide, and a low-resistance placental bed |
| **Blood pressure** | Falls in the first and second trimesters (**nadir at about 20–24 weeks**, diastolic falls most), returns to baseline by term | Hypertension before 20 weeks suggests **chronic hypertension** (or molar pregnancy); physiological lowering can mask it |
| **Heart position and signs** | Heart pushed up and left (apex displaced); **ejection systolic flow murmur** in about 90%; third heart sound common | **Diastolic murmurs are never physiological**, so investigate them (mitral stenosis is common in Uganda) |
| **ECG** | Left axis deviation, T-wave inversion in III, ectopics | Do not over-read it |
| **Venous pressure in the legs** | Rises (uterine compression of the iliac veins and IVC) | Ankle oedema (about 80% of normal pregnancies), varicose veins, haemorrhoids, **DVT (commoner on the left)** |

> [!PEARL]
> **Aortocaval compression.** From about **20 weeks** the supine uterus compresses the IVC (and aorta), reducing venous return and cardiac output by up to 25–30%. It causes **supine hypotensive syndrome** (dizziness, pallor, fetal distress). In resuscitation, CPR, examination and CS, **tilt her 15° to the left or manually displace the uterus to the left**. In cardiac arrest after 20 weeks, if there is no response by **4 minutes**, a perimortem CS (resuscitative hysterotomy) is done to save the **mother**.

### Blood and haematology

| Parameter | Change | Clinical relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Plasma volume** | **Up 40–50%** (about 1,200–1,500 mL), mostly by 32–34 weeks | Protects against the blood loss of delivery. Driven by RAAS activation (oestrogen raises angiotensinogen) and water retention (osmotic threshold resets, with sodium about 4–5 mmol/L lower) |
| **Red cell mass** | **Up 20–30%** (more with iron supplements) | Rises less than plasma, giving **physiological (dilutional) anaemia**, with the lowest Hb at about 28–34 weeks |
| **Haemoglobin** | Falls (normal lower limit **11 g/dL** in pregnancy, WHO) | Below 11 is anaemia. **Poor plasma expansion** (high Hb/haematocrit) is a sign of **pre-eclampsia** |
| **Iron requirement** | About **1 g** in total over the pregnancy (fetus and placenta about 300 mg, red cell expansion about 450–500 mg, losses about 200 mg) | Most women cannot meet this from diet, hence **routine iron** |
| **White cells** | Up (up to about 15 × 10⁹/L; **up to 25 × 10⁹/L in labour and the early puerperium**) | A raised WCC alone does not prove infection |
| **Platelets** | Slight fall (dilution and consumption). **Gestational thrombocytopenia**: mild (usually above 70–100 × 10⁹/L), third trimester, asymptomatic | Platelets below 100 × 10⁹/L with hypertension suggests **HELLP** |
| **Clotting factors** | **Fibrinogen up** (to about 4–6 g/L), factors **VII, VIII, IX, X and vWF up**; **protein S down**, **activated protein C resistance**; fibrinolysis **reduced** (PAI-1 and PAI-2 up) | **Hypercoagulable state** prepares for placental separation but gives about a **5-fold VTE risk**, highest **postpartum** (especially after CS). A "normal" fibrinogen of 2 g/L in a bleeding woman is actually low, which suggests **DIC** |
| **ESR** | Rises markedly | Useless as a marker of inflammation in pregnancy |

**Virchow's triad in pregnancy:** stasis (venous compression, progesterone venodilation, immobility), hypercoagulability (the factors above), and vessel wall injury (delivery, CS).

### Respiratory system

| Parameter | Change | Clinical relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Tidal volume** | **Up 30–50%** | Progesterone sensitises the respiratory centre to CO₂ |
| **Respiratory rate** | **Unchanged** (or minimally up) | **RR above 20/min is abnormal**: think PE, pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, sepsis, DKA |
| **Minute ventilation** | **Up about 40–50%** | "Physiological dyspnoea of pregnancy" (breathless feeling, normal exam, normal SpO₂) |
| **Oxygen consumption** | Up about 20% | Fetus, placenta, maternal organs |
| **FRC (functional residual capacity)** | **Down about 20%** (diaphragm elevated by up to 4 cm) | Smaller O₂ reserve plus higher consumption means **rapid desaturation** in apnoea (eclamptic fit, intubation) |
| **Arterial blood gas** | **PaCO₂ falls to about 4.0 kPa (30 mmHg)**; pH about 7.42–7.44; **HCO₃⁻ falls to about 18–22 mmol/L** (renal compensation); PaO₂ slightly up | **Compensated respiratory alkalosis.** The low PaCO₂ gradient helps the fetus offload CO₂. A "normal" PaCO₂ of 5.3 kPa (40 mmHg) in a pregnant asthmatic suggests **tiring and impending respiratory failure** |
| **Upper airway** | Oedema and hyperaemia of the mucosa | Nasal congestion and nosebleeds; **difficult intubation** (use a smaller tube) |
| **Vital capacity** | Unchanged | Chest widens (subcostal angle increases) to compensate for the elevated diaphragm |

### Renal and urinary tract

| Parameter | Change | Clinical relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Renal plasma flow** | Up 50–80% | – |
| **GFR** | **Up about 50%** (from early pregnancy) | **Creatinine falls** (normal about 35–70 µmol/L; above about 77–80 µmol/L is suspicious); **urea falls**. Drugs cleared renally (e.g. some antibiotics, MgSO₄) are cleared faster |
| **Glucose handling** | Filtered load exceeds tubular reabsorption, so **glycosuria** is common | Glycosuria alone does not diagnose diabetes; screen with blood glucose/OGTT |
| **Protein** | Slight rise in excretion (normal below 300 mg/24 h) | Proteinuria of 300 mg/24 h or more (or PCR 30 mg/mmol or more) is abnormal |
| **Uric acid** | Falls early (GFR) then rises to non-pregnant levels at term | A **high urate** is associated with pre-eclampsia |
| **Ureters and pelvis** | **Dilated (hydroureter/hydronephrosis), right more than left**, from progesterone relaxation plus compression at the pelvic brim (the right ovarian vein and dextrorotation of the uterus) | Urinary stasis, so **asymptomatic bacteriuria progresses to pyelonephritis** (about 30% if untreated). Treat bacteriuria. Do not misdiagnose obstruction on ultrasound |
| **Bladder** | Frequency (early from uterine pressure, late from the head in the pelvis) | – |

### Gastrointestinal system and liver

- **Nausea and vomiting** (linked to hCG and oestrogen): affects about 70–80% of women, usually settles by 14–16 weeks. **Hyperemesis gravidarum** means dehydration, ketosis and more than 5% weight loss.
- **Progesterone relaxes smooth muscle**, which causes **heartburn/reflux** (lower oesophageal sphincter), delayed gastric emptying (**aspiration risk under general anaesthesia**: give an antacid and use a rapid-sequence induction), **constipation**, and **gallbladder stasis** (gallstones).
- **Liver:** size and blood flow essentially unchanged. **ALP rises 2–4 times** (placental isoenzyme, so it is normal). **Albumin falls** (dilution). Transaminases (ALT, AST), bilirubin and GGT are normal or slightly lower, so **raised ALT/AST in pregnancy is always abnormal** (HELLP, acute fatty liver, intrahepatic cholestasis, hepatitis).
- **Gums:** hyperaemia and bleeding (pregnancy gingivitis, epulis).
- **Pica** (eating soil or clay, "emumbwa") is common in Uganda and linked with iron deficiency and helminths.

### Endocrine and metabolic

| System | Change | Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Carbohydrate** | First half: insulin sensitivity is normal or increased (a tendency to **hypoglycaemia** with fasting). Second half: **insulin resistance** (hPL, cortisol, progesterone, prolactin) diverts glucose to the fetus | This unmasks **GDM**, so screen at **24–28 weeks** |
| **Lipids** | Triglycerides and cholesterol up | Energy store for late pregnancy and lactation |
| **Thyroid** | Gland enlarges modestly; **TBG up**, so **total** T4/T3 are up but **free** T4 is normal; **TSH falls in the first trimester** (hCG stimulates the TSH receptor) | Use trimester-specific ranges. Iodine needs rise. A goitre should still be examined |
| **Adrenal** | Cortisol (total and free) up; aldosterone, renin and angiotensin up | Striae, glucose intolerance; sodium and water retention |
| **Pituitary** | Enlarges (up to 2-fold, lactotroph hyperplasia); prolactin rises | **Sheehan's syndrome** (postpartum pituitary necrosis after massive PPH): failure of lactation, amenorrhoea |
| **Calcium** | Total calcium falls (albumin), ionised calcium normal; **1,25-(OH)₂ vitamin D doubles** so gut absorption doubles | The fetus takes about 30 g of calcium, mostly in the third trimester |

### Reproductive organs

- **Uterus:** from about 50–70 g and 10 mL capacity to about **1,000–1,100 g** and **about 5 L** at term. Growth is by **hypertrophy** (and early hyperplasia) of myometrial cells. **Uterine blood flow** rises from about 50 mL/min to about **500–750 mL/min at term** (about 10–15% of cardiac output), which is why **PPH can exsanguinate a woman within minutes**. The **lower segment** forms from the isthmus from about 28 weeks, is thin and less vascular, and is where we make the CS incision.
- **Fundal landmarks:** at 12 weeks the uterus becomes palpable just above the symphysis; at **16 weeks** it lies midway between the symphysis and umbilicus; at **20–22 weeks** at the umbilicus; at **36 weeks** at the xiphisternum; at 40 weeks it drops slightly with **lightening** (engagement in primigravidae).
- **Cervix:** softens (Goodell's) and becomes vascular (bluish). Glands proliferate to form the **mucus plug (operculum)**. Physiological **ectropion** (contact bleeding). Remodels near term (ripening).
- **Vagina:** congested (Chadwick's), increased discharge (**leucorrhoea**, white, non-offensive, **lactobacilli**, acidic), with a higher risk of **candidiasis**.
- **Ovaries:** ovulation stops. The **corpus luteum** supports the pregnancy until about 8–10 weeks. Removing it before then without progesterone support causes miscarriage.
- **Breasts:** enlarge, tender, **Montgomery's tubercles** (enlarged sebaceous glands of the areola), secondary areola, pigmented nipples, colostrum from about 16 weeks.

### Skin and musculoskeletal

- Pigmentation from **MSH/oestrogen/progesterone**: **linea nigra**, **chloasma/melasma** ("mask of pregnancy"), darkened areolae.
- **Striae gravidarum**, spider naevi and palmar erythema (oestrogen).
- **Lordosis** and back pain; **symphysis pubis dysfunction** (relaxin); **carpal tunnel syndrome** (fluid retention).

### Weight gain and nutrition

- Typical total **11–16 kg** for a woman of normal BMI (IOM). The components are fetus about 3.4 kg, placenta 0.6, amniotic fluid 0.8, uterus 0.9, breasts 0.4, blood 1.5, extracellular fluid 1.5 and fat about 3.5 kg.
- Energy needs rise by about 300 kcal/day in the second and third trimesters, so advise "**one extra meal a day**" in Uganda.

## Clinical acumen

### Focused history (diagnosing pregnancy)

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **First day of the LNMP**, and was it normal (flow, duration)? | Dating. A lighter "period" may have been implantation or threatened miscarriage bleeding |
| **Cycle length and regularity** | Naegele assumes 28 days. For a long cycle, add days |
| **Contraception**: type, when stopped | Depo-Provera can cause amenorrhoea without pregnancy; conceiving with an IUD in place raises ectopic risk; stopping the pill causes irregular cycles |
| **Breastfeeding?** | Lactational amenorrhoea can hide a new pregnancy |
| **Symptoms**: nausea, breast tenderness, frequency, quickening | Presumptive support. Severe vomiting suggests twins or a molar pregnancy |
| **Pain or bleeding** | **Ectopic** or **miscarriage** must be excluded early |
| **Home pregnancy test results** | Timing and repeat testing |
| **Previous pregnancies, ectopics, PID, tubal surgery** | Ectopic risk |
| **Medical conditions and drugs** | Teratogens (valproate, warfarin, ACE inhibitors, methotrexate, isotretinoin); plan folate 5 mg if on antiepileptics or with a previous NTD |

### Examination

- **General:** pallor, BP (baseline for later comparison), pulse, weight, thyroid, breasts (Montgomery's tubercles, secondary areola) with consent and a chaperone.
- **Abdomen:** palpate the fundus (12 weeks or more), fetal heart by Doppler (10–12 weeks or more), fetal parts (20 weeks or more). Look for **linea nigra and striae**.
- **Pelvic (bimanual) exam** when indicated: uterine size (a hen's egg is about 6 weeks, an orange about 8 weeks, a grapefruit about 12 weeks), softening (Hegar's/Goodell's), adnexal tenderness or mass (**ectopic**), cervical excitation.
- **Speculum:** bluish cervix (Chadwick's), the os (open or closed if bleeding), discharge.

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **Positive pregnancy test + pain or bleeding + empty uterus on scan** means **ectopic until proven otherwise**. Shock, shoulder-tip pain or peritonism means a ruptured ectopic: resuscitate and take her to theatre.
> - **Uterus much larger than dates + very high hCG + hyperemesis or early pre-eclampsia (before 20 weeks) + vaginal bleeding** means a **molar pregnancy**.
> - **Breathlessness with RR above 20/min, SpO₂ below 95%, orthopnoea, a diastolic murmur, or tachycardia above 100 bpm** is **not** physiological: think cardiac disease (RHD, peripartum cardiomyopathy), PE, pneumonia or severe anaemia.
> - **Unilateral leg swelling** (usually left) means DVT until proven otherwise.
> - **Generalised or facial oedema with hypertension** means pre-eclampsia.
> - **Pruritus of the palms and soles, no rash** means intrahepatic cholestasis (check bile acids and LFTs).

### Thinking like a clinician

- **"Every woman of reproductive age is pregnant until proven otherwise."** Before an X-ray, prescription, laparotomy or diagnosis of "PID" or "appendicitis", do a urine hCG.
- **Interpreting hCG with ultrasound:**
  - hCG above the discriminatory zone (about 1,500–3,500 IU/L) with **no intrauterine sac on TVS** suggests ectopic or complete miscarriage.
  - hCG below the zone with no sac means a **pregnancy of unknown location**: repeat hCG in 48 hours. A rise of 63% or more suggests a viable IUP; a fall of 50% or more suggests failing pregnancy; a suboptimal rise suggests ectopic.
  - Very high hCG suggests molar pregnancy, twins, or wrong dates.
- **Negative test but still suspect pregnancy?** Too early (repeat in 1 week), dilute urine (use the first morning sample), or the **hook effect** (extremely high hCG in a mole can give a false negative; dilute the sample).
- **Positive test but not pregnant?** Recent pregnancy or miscarriage (hCG can persist about 4–6 weeks), hCG injection (infertility treatment), gestational trophoblastic disease, rare tumours (germ cell).
- **Physiology vs pathology:** before attributing anything to "normal pregnancy", ask "**Is this within the expected range of change, and is the trend right?**"
  - Breathlessness: physiological if gradual, at rest or on talking, with a normal RR, SpO₂ and exam. Pathological if sudden, with tachypnoea, tachycardia, chest pain, cough or haemoptysis.
  - Oedema: physiological if bilateral ankle oedema, worse in the evening, with normal BP. Pathological if facial or hand oedema with hypertension, or unilateral.
  - Murmur: physiological if soft systolic ejection. Pathological if diastolic, loud (grade 3 or more), or with symptoms.
  - Hb: 10.5–11 in the second trimester may be mild dilution, but **below 11 is still treated** as anaemia in Uganda (WHO cut-off), and below 10 is almost always true anaemia.
- **Haemorrhage physiology pitfall:** because of the expanded blood volume, a pregnant woman can lose **1,000–1,500 mL (up to about 30% of blood volume) before BP falls**. **Tachycardia and a narrowing pulse pressure come first**; hypotension is a late, pre-arrest sign. Use the **shock index** (HR ÷ systolic BP): **0.9 or more is worrying, 1.4 or more is critical**.

## Clinical workup

This topic has two practical workups that you will use every day: **(A) confirming and dating a pregnancy**, and **(B) deciding whether a symptom, sign or result in a pregnant woman is normal physiology or disease**. Both follow the same steps.

### Step 0: First 5 minutes

1. **Is she pregnant?** Any woman aged about 12–50 with abdominal pain, bleeding, collapse, vomiting, or who needs a drug, X-ray or operation gets a **urine hCG** now. It takes 5 minutes and costs very little.
2. **Vital signs, read with pregnancy norms:**

| Sign | Normal in pregnancy | Pathological: act now |
|---|---|---|
| Heart rate | Up 10–20 bpm (roughly 80–95/min) | **Above 100/min at rest**: bleeding, sepsis, anaemia, PE, thyroid, cardiac disease |
| Blood pressure | Lower in the 2nd trimester (nadir 20–24 weeks) | **140/90 or more** = hypertension; **160/110 or more** = severe; hypotension = late sign of shock |
| Respiratory rate | **Unchanged** (12–20/min) | **Above 20/min** is abnormal: PE, pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, sepsis, DKA |
| SpO₂ | 95% or more | **Below 95%** is never physiological |
| Temperature | Up to about 0.5°C higher early on | **38.0°C or more** = fever |
| Shock index (HR ÷ SBP) | 0.7–0.9 | **0.9 or more** worrying; **1.4 or more** critical |

3. **Positive hCG + pain or bleeding + tachycardia or shock index 0.9 or more** → treat as a **ruptured ectopic** until proven otherwise: call for help, two large-bore cannulae, group and cross-match, urgent scan or straight to theatre if unstable.
4. **From 20 weeks, never leave her supine**: left lateral tilt (15–30°) or manual uterine displacement before you examine, resuscitate or transfer.

### Step 1: Focused history

**(A) Confirming and dating a pregnancy**

| Ask | Why: the answer that changes the plan |
|---|---|
| **First day of LNMP**; was it a normal period? | Naegele's EDD. A light "period" may have been bleeding in early pregnancy, so dates are uncertain → early scan |
| **Cycle length and regularity** | Long cycles: add (cycle length − 28) days. Irregular → scan dating |
| **Contraception** (DMPA, implant, pill, IUD) and **breastfeeding** | Amenorrhoea may be the method, not a pregnancy; IUD in situ → higher ectopic risk |
| **Pain, bleeding, shoulder-tip pain, fainting** | Ectopic or miscarriage → urgent scan, not routine booking |
| **Severe vomiting** | Twins, **molar pregnancy**, hyperemesis → early scan, hCG, U&E |
| **Previous ectopic, PID, tubal surgery, infertility** | Ectopic risk → locate the pregnancy by TVS |
| **Drugs** | Teratogens (valproate, warfarin, ACE inhibitors, methotrexate, isotretinoin) → switch now; folic acid **5 mg** if on antiepileptics or previous NTD |

**(B) Is this symptom physiological?** For every symptom ask: **onset** (gradual = more likely physiological; sudden = pathology), **severity** (does it stop her from normal activity?), **associated features** (the "red-flag companions" below), and **trend** (getting worse week by week?).

| Symptom | Reassuring (physiological) companions | Red-flag companions (pathology) |
|---|---|---|
| Breathlessness | Gradual, on talking or mild exertion, no cough | Sudden, at rest, orthopnoea, PND, chest pain, haemoptysis, palpitations |
| Swelling | Both ankles, evening, settles overnight | Face or hands, headache, visual symptoms (pre-eclampsia); one leg (DVT) |
| Palpitations | Brief, no syncope | Syncope, chest pain, known RHD, sustained HR above 100 |
| Vomiting | First trimester, eating and drinking something | Dehydration, weight loss, after 16 weeks (look for malaria, UTI, hepatitis, molar pregnancy) |
| Itching | Localised, with rash, stretch-mark area | **Palms and soles, no rash** (intrahepatic cholestasis) |
| Headache | Mild, early pregnancy, responds to paracetamol | After 20 weeks, severe, with visual disturbance or epigastric pain (pre-eclampsia) |
| Discharge | White, non-offensive, non-itchy | Itch, odour, colour, or watery (ruptured membranes) |

### Step 2: Focused examination

**General:** pallor, jaundice, oedema (where?), pulse character, JVP, thyroid (modest symmetrical enlargement can be normal; a nodule is not).

**Cardiorespiratory:** a **soft ejection systolic flow murmur** is found in most pregnant women and is normal. **A diastolic murmur, a loud (grade 3 or more) murmur, a displaced heaving apex, basal crepitations or an irregular pulse are never physiological.** Chest: clear, normal RR.

**Abdomen:** fundal height against the landmarks (**12 weeks** just above symphysis, **16** halfway to umbilicus, **20–22** at umbilicus, **36** at xiphisternum); linea nigra and striae; fetal heart with Doppler from 10–12 weeks, Pinard from about 20 weeks.

**Pelvic (only when indicated):** speculum for bleeding source, os open or closed, bluish cervix (Chadwick's); bimanual for uterine size (hen's egg 6 weeks, orange 8, grapefruit 12), softening, **adnexal tenderness or mass, cervical excitation** (ectopic).

**Legs:** calf size and tenderness on both sides (DVT is commoner on the **left**).

**Document** for a normal early pregnancy: "Pulse 88, BP 108/64, RR 16, SpO₂ 98%, no pallor, soft ejection systolic murmur only, uterus about 8 weeks' size, soft, non-tender, no adnexal mass, os closed."

### Step 3: Bedside tests

| Test | Normal in pregnancy | What an abnormal result means |
|---|---|---|
| **Urine hCG** (detects about 20–25 IU/L) | Positive from about the missed period | Negative with strong suspicion → repeat in 1 week or serum β-hCG; very high hCG can cause a false negative (hook effect) in a mole |
| **Urine dipstick** | Trace glucose or protein can be normal | **Protein 1+ or more with BP 140/90** → pre-eclampsia workup; **nitrites/leucocytes** → UTI; **ketones** 2+ or more with vomiting → dehydration/hyperemesis |
| **HemoCue Hb** | **11 g/dL or more** (10.5 or more acceptable in the 2nd trimester) | Below 11 anaemia; below 7 severe |
| **Pulse oximetry** | **95% or more** | Below 95% = pathology (PE, pneumonia, pulmonary oedema) |
| **RBS** | Fasting tends lower in early pregnancy | High → formal FBS/OGTT (glycosuria alone is not enough) |
| **Doppler / Pinard** | FHR **110–160 bpm** | Not heard when expected → ultrasound |
| **Bedside clotting test** (2 mL in a plain glass tube) | Clot by about 7 minutes (pregnancy is hypercoagulable) | **No clot by 7 minutes** or a clot that breaks up → coagulopathy/DIC |
| **Point-of-care ultrasound** (if trained) | Intrauterine sac, fetal heart | Empty uterus + free fluid in the pouch of Douglas/Morison's pouch + positive hCG → ruptured ectopic |

### Step 4: Laboratory & imaging

| Test | When | Expected (normal pregnancy) / abnormal finding | How it changes management (and Ugandan availability) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Serum quantitative β-hCG** | Pregnancy of unknown location, suspected ectopic or mole, follow-up after miscarriage/GTD | Detectable 8–11 days after conception; **rises 63% or more in 48 h** in a viable early IUP; peaks at 8–10 weeks. **Above the discriminatory zone (about 1,500–3,500 IU/L) with an empty uterus on TVS** = ectopic or complete miscarriage | Decides between expectant follow-up, methotrexate, or surgery. Regional/national referral and private labs |
| **Transvaginal ultrasound** | Early pregnancy with pain or bleeding; dating | Sac about 4.5–5 weeks, yolk sac about 5.5, **cardiac activity about 6 weeks**; CRL dates to ±5–7 days at 7–14 weeks | Location, viability, number, dates. Hospitals and some HC IVs (transabdominal much more common; sees things about 1 week later) |
| **Dating / anatomy ultrasound (transabdominal)** | At least once **before 24 weeks** | Singleton or twins, placenta site, anomalies | Scan EDD replaces LNMP EDD if they differ by more than 5–7 days (1st trimester) or 10–14 days (2nd trimester). Most HC IVs and hospitals |
| **FBC** | Booking, anaemia, infection, pre-eclampsia | **Hb 11 g/dL or more**; **WCC up to about 15 × 10⁹/L** (higher in labour and after steroids); platelets 150–400 (gestational thrombocytopenia usually above 100) | Platelets **below 100** → HELLP, ITP, DIC. Hb high for gestation in a hypertensive woman → poor plasma expansion (pre-eclampsia). HC IV and hospital |
| **Creatinine and urea** | Hypertension, sepsis, vomiting, before nephrotoxic drugs | **Creatinine 35–70 µmol/L**; above about 77–80 suspicious; **above 90 clearly abnormal**. Urea 2–4.5 mmol/L | A "normal" non-pregnant creatinine may mean renal impairment in pregnancy → pre-eclampsia with severe features, sepsis, dehydration. Hospital labs |
| **LFTs** | Vomiting after 16 weeks, jaundice, itching, pre-eclampsia | **ALP up 2–4× (placental), albumin low (28–37 g/L)** are normal. **ALT/AST above about 35 IU/L are abnormal** | Raised transaminases → HELLP, AFLP, cholestasis (with bile acids), viral hepatitis. Hospital labs |
| **Fibrinogen, PT/APTT** | PPH, abruption, IUFD, HELLP | **Fibrinogen 4–6 g/L**; PT/APTT normal or slightly short | **Fibrinogen below 2 g/L in PPH = severe coagulopathy** → blood products early. Regional/national referral only; use the bedside clotting test elsewhere |
| **ABG** | Breathlessness, asthma, sepsis, DKA | **pH about 7.42–7.44, PaCO₂ about 4.0 kPa (30 mmHg), HCO₃⁻ 18–22 mmol/L** | PaCO₂ of 5.3 kPa or more = respiratory fatigue → HDU/ICU. Mainly national referral/ICU |
| **TFTs** | Hyperemesis, goitre, palpitations, known thyroid disease | TSH lower in 1st trimester (about 0.1–2.5 mU/L); **free** T4 normal (total T4 up with TBG) | Low TSH with high hCG in hyperemesis is usually **transient gestational thyrotoxicosis**: do not start antithyroid drugs without a senior. Regional/national referral |
| **75 g OGTT** | 24–28 weeks if risk factors | Fasting below 5.1 mmol/L normal; GDM if fasting 5.1 or more, 1 h 10.0 or more, or 2 h 8.5 or more | Insulin resistance of late pregnancy unmasks GDM. Regional/national referral |
| **D-dimer, ESR, CRP** | Rarely useful | **All rise in normal pregnancy** | A positive D-dimer does not diagnose VTE: go straight to **compression Doppler ultrasound** (and CTPA/V/Q where available) if clinical suspicion |
| **ECG / echocardiogram** | Palpitations, murmur with symptoms, breathlessness at rest | ECG may show **left axis deviation, T inversion in III, ectopics** normally | Echo for any diastolic murmur (mitral stenosis) or suspected peripartum cardiomyopathy. Echo at regional/national referral (e.g. Uganda Heart Institute, Mulago) |

### Step 5: Putting it together

**Model one-line summary (physiology vs pathology):**

> "Mrs N., 27-year-old G2P1 at **32 weeks**, with **2 weeks of gradual breathlessness on exertion**. No orthopnoea, chest pain or cough. Pulse 92, BP 112/70, **RR 16**, **SpO₂ 98%**, soft ejection systolic murmur only, chest clear, ankle oedema only, Hb 11.4 g/dL. **Impression: physiological breathlessness of pregnancy**; no evidence of cardiac or respiratory disease."

**Problem list** (for the same patient): 1) breathlessness, likely physiological; 2) ankle oedema with normal BP and urine; 3) routine ANC due (IPTp, Hb recheck). **Working diagnosis:** normal singleton pregnancy at 32 weeks with physiological symptoms. **Plan:** reassure, safety-net (come back at once with breathlessness at rest, chest pain, swelling of the face or one leg, or headache).

**Worked example 1: reading an ABG in a pregnant woman**

| | Patient X (well, 30 weeks) | Patient Y (asthma attack, 30 weeks) |
|---|---|---|
| pH | 7.44 | 7.38 |
| PaCO₂ | 4.0 kPa (30 mmHg) | **5.4 kPa (41 mmHg)** |
| HCO₃⁻ | 20 mmol/L | 22 mmol/L |
| Interpretation | **Normal compensated respiratory alkalosis of pregnancy** | PaCO₂ looks "normal" for a non-pregnant adult but is **high for pregnancy**: she is **tiring**. Escalate to HDU/ICU and call the anaesthetist |

**Worked example 2: "normal" results that are abnormal in pregnancy**

A 34-week woman with BP 150/96 mmHg has: **creatinine 92 µmol/L**, **ALT 68 IU/L**, **ALP 310 IU/L**, **platelets 96 × 10⁹/L**, Hb 13.4 g/dL, albumin 29 g/L.

- Creatinine 92 is within many non-pregnant ranges but **clearly abnormal in pregnancy** (normal 35–70) → renal involvement.
- ALT 68 is **abnormal** (transaminases do not rise in normal pregnancy).
- ALP 310 is **expected** (placental ALP rises 2–4×): do not chase it.
- Platelets 96 are **below 100** → not gestational thrombocytopenia in this context.
- Hb 13.4 at 34 weeks is **high for gestation** → poor plasma expansion (haemoconcentration).
- Albumin 29 g/L is within the normal pregnancy range.
- **Conclusion:** pre-eclampsia with severe features (renal, hepatic and haematological involvement), possible evolving HELLP → admit, MgSO₄, control BP, plan delivery (see the pre-eclampsia topic).

> [!PEARL]
> When you present any pregnant woman's results, say the pregnancy-normal range out loud: "Her creatinine is 92, which is **high for pregnancy** where we expect 35 to 70." It shows the consultant you know the physiology and stops the team being falsely reassured.

## Differential diagnosis

### Amenorrhoea in a woman of reproductive age

| Condition | Distinguishing features | Key investigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Intrauterine pregnancy** | Symptoms of pregnancy, enlarging soft uterus | Urine/serum hCG; ultrasound |
| **Ectopic pregnancy** | Amenorrhoea + pain + spotting; adnexal tenderness; uterus smaller than dates | hCG + TVS (empty uterus, adnexal mass, free fluid) |
| **Molar pregnancy** | Uterus larger than dates, hyperemesis, bleeding, early pre-eclampsia, theca-lutein cysts | Very high hCG; "snowstorm" ultrasound |
| **Hormonal contraception** (DMPA, implant) | History of injection; hCG negative | hCG |
| **Lactational amenorrhoea** | Breastfeeding under 6 months | hCG to exclude a new pregnancy |
| **PCOS** | Oligomenorrhoea, hirsutism, obesity, acne | hCG negative; ultrasound ovaries; hormones |
| **Hyperprolactinaemia** | Galactorrhoea, headaches, visual field loss | Prolactin, hCG negative |
| **Premature ovarian insufficiency / menopause** | Hot flushes, age | FSH raised |
| **Pseudocyesis** | Firm belief of pregnancy, abdominal distension, no fetus | hCG negative, ultrasound empty |
| **Stress, weight loss, chronic illness (TB, HIV)** | Context | hCG negative |

### Uterus larger than dates

Wrong dates, **twins**, **molar pregnancy**, **polyhydramnios**, **fibroids**, **macrosomia**, full bladder.

## Investigations

All the tests (urine and serum hCG, TVS and transabdominal ultrasound, Doppler and Pinard, FBC, renal and liver tests, clotting, ABG, TFTs and OGTT), with their **normal pregnancy ranges** and what an abnormal result changes, are in **Clinical workup, Steps 3 and 4** above. The two rules to carry away: **urine hCG first** in any woman of reproductive age, and **interpret every result against pregnancy-specific ranges**.

## Management

This topic is about normal physiology, so "management" means **(1) confirming and dating the pregnancy, (2) managing common physiological symptoms safely, and (3) applying physiology to emergencies.**

### 1. Confirming and dating a pregnancy: step by step

1. **Take the menstrual history** and calculate the EDD by Naegele's rule (adjust for cycle length).
2. **Do a urine hCG test.** If negative but suspicion is high, repeat in 1 week or send serum β-hCG.
3. **If positive with pain or bleeding:** vital signs first, then urgent ultrasound. Manage a possible ectopic as an emergency (see the ectopic topic).
4. **If positive and well:** book ANC (ideally **before 12 weeks**). Start **folic acid 400 µg** (with iron per the Uganda ANC package), or **5 mg daily** if she has a previous NTD-affected baby, takes antiepileptics, has diabetes, sickle cell disease or BMI 30 or more.
5. **Dating ultrasound** (CRL at 7–14 weeks is best; WHO recommends one scan before 24 weeks). If the ultrasound and LNMP EDDs differ by more than 5–7 days in the first trimester (or more than 10–14 days in the second trimester), **use the scan date**.
6. **Record the EDD** on the ANC card and **never change it later** based on late scans or fundal height.

### 2. Managing common "physiological" complaints

| Complaint | Physiology | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Nausea and vomiting** | hCG, oestrogen | Small frequent dry meals, ginger, avoid triggers. If needed: **promethazine 25 mg orally 8-hourly** or **metoclopramide 10 mg orally 8-hourly**; pyridoxine (vitamin B6) 10–25 mg 8-hourly. Hyperemesis (dehydration, ketonuria, weight loss): admit for **IV normal saline with potassium**, **thiamine 100 mg** before any dextrose (Wernicke's), and IV/IM antiemetic |
| **Heartburn** | Progesterone relaxes the LOS; uterine pressure | Small meals, avoid lying flat after meals, raise the head of the bed. **Magnesium trisilicate** 10 mL 8-hourly or an aluminium hydroxide antacid (separate from iron by 2 hours) |
| **Constipation** | Progesterone slows transit; iron | Fluids, fruit and vegetables; lactulose 15 mL 12-hourly if needed |
| **Haemorrhoids and varicose veins** | Venous compression, progesterone | Avoid constipation; leg elevation; support stockings |
| **Ankle oedema** | Venous pressure, sodium retention, low albumin | Leg elevation, left lateral rest. **Always check BP and urine** |
| **Backache** | Lordosis, relaxin | Posture, flat shoes, paracetamol 1 g 6–8-hourly (max 4 g/day). **Avoid NSAIDs**, especially after 20 weeks |
| **Leg cramps** | Uncertain | Stretching, hydration |
| **Urinary frequency** | Uterine pressure, increased GFR | Reassure, but **dipstick** to exclude UTI |
| **Physiological breathlessness** | Progesterone-driven hyperventilation | Reassure only after a normal RR, SpO₂, chest and heart exam |
| **Vaginal discharge** | Oestrogen-driven leucorrhoea | Reassure if white, non-itchy, non-offensive. Treat candidiasis with **clotrimazole 500 mg pessary single dose** (or 100 mg nightly for 7 nights; longer courses work better in pregnancy). Avoid oral fluconazole in pregnancy |
| **Syncope / dizziness** | Low SVR, aortocaval compression | Avoid lying supine and standing up quickly; **lie on the left side** |

> [!DRUG]
> **Drugs in pregnancy that are generally safe:** paracetamol, amoxicillin, ampicillin, cephalosporins, erythromycin/azithromycin, metronidazole (after the first trimester is traditional, but it can be used), nitrofurantoin (avoid at term), methyldopa, nifedipine, labetalol, hydralazine, insulin, heparin, TLD, artemether–lumefantrine (all trimesters per updated WHO guidance), quinine.
> **Avoid:** ACE inhibitors/ARBs, warfarin (first trimester and near term), tetracyclines/doxycycline, fluoroquinolones (if alternatives exist), valproate, isotretinoin, methotrexate, misoprostol (unless indicated), NSAIDs in the third trimester (premature closure of the ductus arteriosus, oligohydramnios), statins, and iodine-131.

### 3. Applying physiology in emergencies: the "pregnancy-adapted" ABC

1. **Position:** left lateral tilt of 15–30° or **manual left uterine displacement** from 20 weeks, to relieve aortocaval compression.
2. **Airway:** expect a difficult airway (oedema, full stomach), so do a **rapid-sequence induction** with a smaller endotracheal tube and give **antacid prophylaxis** (e.g. sodium citrate 30 mL, or ranitidine/omeprazole) before CS.
3. **Breathing:** **pre-oxygenate** well (reduced FRC means rapid desaturation). Aim for **SpO₂ of 95% or more**.
4. **Circulation:** remember the blood-volume cushion. **Treat tachycardia as haemorrhage until proven otherwise**, use two large-bore cannulae, and do not wait for hypotension.
5. **Coagulation:** in PPH or abruption, watch fibrinogen (it should be high). **Below 2 g/L predicts severe PPH**, so give tranexamic acid and blood products early.
6. **Thromboprophylaxis:** after CS or in immobile, high-risk women give **enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily** (weight-adjusted) or unfractionated heparin 5,000 IU SC 12-hourly where available. Mobilise early and keep her hydrated.
7. **Perimortem CS:** in maternal cardiac arrest with a uterus at or above the umbilicus, start CPR with left uterine displacement. If there is no return of circulation, **deliver by 4–5 minutes** to improve maternal resuscitation.

> [!UGANDA]
> In many Ugandan HC IVs, **ABG, fibrinogen and D-dimer are unavailable**. Rely on the bedside: HR, RR, SpO₂, shock index, urine output, capillary refill, conjunctival pallor, and the **bedside clotting test** (WHO: 2 mL of venous blood in a small plain glass tube, tilted every minute; **no clot by 7 minutes**, or a soft clot that breaks up easily, suggests coagulopathy/DIC; some units use the 20-minute whole-blood clotting test instead). **D-dimer rises normally in pregnancy**, so a positive result is unhelpful.

## Complications

What happens when the physiological adaptation **fails or is exceeded**:

| Normal change | When it goes wrong |
|---|---|
| Plasma volume expansion | Poor expansion goes with **pre-eclampsia** and FGR. Excess demand on a diseased heart causes **pulmonary oedema** in mitral stenosis or peripartum cardiomyopathy |
| Increased cardiac output | Decompensation of **rheumatic heart disease** at 28–32 weeks, in labour and immediately postpartum |
| Hypercoagulability | **DVT/PE** (a leading direct cause of maternal death worldwide), **cerebral venous thrombosis** |
| Insulin resistance | **Gestational diabetes**, macrosomia, shoulder dystocia |
| Ureteric dilatation and stasis | **Pyelonephritis**, preterm labour, sepsis |
| Reduced FRC and gastric emptying | **Hypoxia** during fits or anaesthesia; **aspiration pneumonitis** (Mendelson's syndrome) |
| Aortocaval compression | **Supine hypotension**, fetal distress; failed resuscitation |
| Uterine blood flow of about 700 mL/min | **Massive PPH** within minutes |
| Increased thyroid demand | Unmasked hypothyroidism or goitre |
| Pituitary enlargement | **Sheehan's syndrome** after PPH-induced hypotension |

## Ward-round & exam pearls

### Model summary line

> "Mrs K is a **26-year-old G1P0** who presents with **7 weeks of amenorrhoea**, nausea and breast tenderness. Her urine hCG is positive and a transvaginal scan shows a **single intrauterine gestational sac with a fetal pole of CRL 11 mm and cardiac activity**, consistent with **7 weeks + 2 days**, matching her LNMP dates. **Diagnosis: a viable singleton intrauterine pregnancy at 7 weeks.** Plan: book ANC, start iron–folate, booking bloods, and advice for nausea."

### Likely viva questions with answers

1. **What are the positive signs of pregnancy?** Fetal heart heard, fetal movements or parts felt by the examiner, and the fetus visualised on ultrasound.
2. **Why is a pregnant woman's Hb lower?** Plasma volume rises about 50% but red cell mass only about 25%, so haemodilution occurs. It lowers viscosity and improves placental perfusion.
3. **Why does a pregnant woman desaturate quickly?** FRC is down about 20% (elevated diaphragm) and O₂ consumption is up about 20%, so the oxygen reserve is used up quickly during apnoea.
4. **Why are pregnant women prone to pyelonephritis?** Progesterone-induced ureteric dilatation and pressure at the pelvic brim cause urinary stasis, and glycosuria adds to it. Asymptomatic bacteriuria therefore ascends.
5. **What ABG do you expect in normal pregnancy?** Compensated respiratory alkalosis: pH about 7.44, PaCO₂ about 4 kPa, HCO₃⁻ about 18–22 mmol/L.
6. **Why does BP fall in mid-pregnancy?** Progesterone and nitric oxide cause vasodilation, and the low-resistance placental circulation lowers SVR, with the nadir at about 20–24 weeks.
7. **When is a heart murmur in pregnancy abnormal?** When it is diastolic, grade 3 or more, radiates widely, or comes with symptoms or signs (orthopnoea, arrhythmia, cyanosis).
8. **How long can a urine pregnancy test stay positive after delivery or miscarriage?** Up to about 4–6 weeks as hCG clears.

> [!EXAM]
> A favourite: "**A 30-week pregnant woman has a PaCO₂ of 5.5 kPa during an asthma attack. Comment.**" Answer: this is **high for pregnancy** (normal is about 4 kPa) and means **respiratory fatigue and impending failure**. Escalate to HDU/ICU.

### OSCE tips

- When asked to "confirm pregnancy", state: history (LNMP, symptoms), a **urine hCG**, and an ultrasound (location, viability, number, dating).
- Calculate an EDD aloud: LNMP **10 March 2025**: add 7 days (17 March 2025), subtract 3 months (17 December 2024), add 1 year, which gives an **EDD of 17 December 2025**.
- Explain physiology in patient-friendly language when counselling ("Your body makes extra blood for the baby; the water part increases more, so your blood level looks lower. That is why we give iron").

## Mnemonics & memory aids

**Things that go UP: "the 50% club"**

Roughly **half again**: **plasma volume** (40–50%), **cardiac output** (30–50%), **minute ventilation** (40–50%), **GFR and renal plasma flow** (50% or more). Smaller rises: red cell mass (20–30%), heart rate (10–20 bpm), tidal volume, O₂ consumption (20%), WCC, ESR, fibrinogen and clotting factors VII, VIII and X, ALP, cortisol, TBG, prolactin.

**Things that go DOWN: "Down with SPICE-FR"**

- **S**VR (and so mid-pregnancy BP)
- **P**aCO₂ (and so HCO₃⁻)
- **I**nsulin sensitivity (second half)
- **C**reatinine and urea
- **E**xpiratory reserve volume
- **F**RC
- **R**ed cell concentration (Hb), plus albumin and protein S

**Signs of early pregnancy by the "letters of the alphabet":** **C**hadwick (Colour, bluish), **G**oodell (Gentle cervix, soft), **H**egar (Hollow feel of the isthmus, soft). **C, G, H** come in alphabetical order, roughly matching the order in which you would examine: look (C), then feel the cervix (G), then bimanual (H).

**Fetal heart detection "10–20" rule:** Doppler at about **10** weeks, Pinard at about **20** weeks.

**Fundal height "12–16–20–36":** symphysis at 12 weeks, halfway at 16, umbilicus at 20 (to 22), xiphisternum at 36.

### Classic exam traps

- **"Hb 10.5 g/dL at 28 weeks is just dilution, so no treatment."** → Physiological haemodilution is real, but in Uganda (WHO) **below 11 g/dL is anaemia** and is treated and investigated.
- **"PaCO₂ 5.3 kPa is normal."** → Normal for a non-pregnant adult, but **high for pregnancy** (normal about 4 kPa): in an asthmatic it signals **respiratory fatigue**.
- **"Creatinine 90 µmol/L is normal."** → In pregnancy creatinine should be **35–70**; 90 is **clearly abnormal** (think pre-eclampsia, sepsis, dehydration).
- **"Raised ALP means liver disease."** → ALP rises **2–4×** from the placenta. It is **ALT/AST** that should never rise.
- **"Breathlessness in pregnancy is physiological."** → Only with a **normal RR, SpO₂ and examination**. A raised RR or low SpO₂ is pathology (PE, pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, mitral stenosis).
- **"Positive D-dimer, so she has a PE."** → D-dimer rises in normal pregnancy. Use **compression Doppler / imaging**.
- **"Glycosuria means diabetes."** → The renal threshold falls in pregnancy; diagnose GDM only by **blood glucose (OGTT)**.
- **"BP is normal, so she is not bleeding much."** → Pregnant women keep their BP until about 30% blood loss: **tachycardia and a rising shock index come first**.

### Questions seniors ask

**Name the positive signs of pregnancy.** Fetal heart heard, fetal parts or movements felt by the examiner, and the fetus seen on ultrasound. Everything else (including hCG) is presumptive or probable.

**The hCG is 2,800 IU/L and the TVS shows an empty uterus. What do you think?** hCG is above the discriminatory zone, so an intrauterine sac should usually be visible: **ectopic pregnancy** (or a complete miscarriage) until proven otherwise. Check for an adnexal mass and free fluid; if she is stable, repeat hCG in 48 hours with a senior plan; if unstable, theatre.

**Why does a pregnant woman desaturate so quickly?** FRC falls about 20% and O₂ consumption rises about 20%, so her oxygen reserve is small and used up fast: pre-oxygenate and protect the airway early.

**Which murmur in pregnancy worries you?** Any **diastolic** murmur, a murmur of grade 3 or more, or any murmur with symptoms. In Uganda think **rheumatic mitral stenosis**: get an echo.

**When does a woman with heart disease decompensate, and why?** At **28–32 weeks** (cardiac output near peak), **in labour**, and **immediately after delivery** (autotransfusion of about 500 mL from the contracting uterus).

**Why must she lie tilted to the left?** From about 20 weeks the supine uterus compresses the IVC and aorta, cutting venous return and cardiac output by up to 25–30%: supine hypotension and fetal distress.

**Why are pregnant women prone to VTE, and when is the risk highest?** Virchow's triad: stasis (venous compression, progesterone), hypercoagulability (fibrinogen, VII, VIII, X up; protein S down) and vessel injury at delivery. Risk is about 5-fold, **highest postpartum**, especially after CS.
