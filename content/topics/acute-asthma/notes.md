## In a nutshell

**Acute asthma** (an "asthma attack" or "exacerbation") is a sudden or gradual worsening of breathlessness, cough, wheeze or chest tightness in a child with asthma, caused by **bronchospasm, airway wall oedema and mucus plugging** in the small airways. In Uganda it is one of the commonest reasons a school-age child is carried into the emergency room at night, and children still die from it, usually because the attack was **under-graded**, treated with too little salbutamol, given oxygen late, or sent home without a plan.

The whole tutorial rests on three moves:

1. **Grade the attack in two minutes**: talk/feed, SpO₂, respiratory rate, pulse, chest sounds, conscious level. Mild–moderate, severe or life-threatening.
2. **Treat by grade, immediately**: oxygen to 94–98%, salbutamol every 20 minutes for the first hour, steroid early; add ipratropium, then **IV magnesium**, then aminophylline, and call for help when the child is not responding.
3. **Prevent the next attack**: inhaler technique with a spacer, a controller (inhaled corticosteroid), trigger avoidance, a written action plan and follow-up.

> [!PEARL]
> The one thing never to forget: **a quiet chest is not a better chest.** A child who stops wheezing, becomes drowsy and breathes more slowly while SpO₂ falls is about to arrest. That is a **silent chest**: the airways are so narrow that no air moves. Treat as life-threatening and call the most senior person.

## Definitions & classification

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Asthma** | Chronic inflammatory airway disease with **variable** respiratory symptoms (wheeze, cough, breathlessness, chest tightness) and **variable expiratory airflow limitation** (GINA 2025) |
| **Acute asthma / exacerbation** | Progressive worsening of symptoms and lung function beyond the child's usual day-to-day variation, needing a change in treatment |
| **Wheeze** | Continuous, high-pitched, mainly **expiratory** musical sound from narrowed intrathoracic airways (compare **stridor**, an inspiratory sound from the larynx/trachea) |
| **Preschool wheeze** | Wheeze in children <5 years. Two patterns: **episodic viral wheeze** (only with colds, well in between) and **multiple-trigger wheeze** (also with exercise, laughing, smoke, at night); the second is more likely to be asthma |
| **Status asthmaticus** | Older term for a severe attack that does not respond to repeated standard bronchodilator therapy; now called **acute severe** or **life-threatening asthma** |
| **Near-fatal asthma** | An attack needing ventilation or with PaCO₂ rising |

### Severity grading of the acute attack (UCG 2023, adapted from BTS/SIGN and GINA)

| Feature | Mild–moderate | Severe | Life-threatening |
|---|---|---|---|
| Talking / feeding | Talks in sentences; feeds | **Cannot complete sentences in one breath**; too breathless to talk or feed | Unable to talk; exhausted |
| Behaviour | Alert, may be agitated | Agitated, sits hunched forward | **Drowsy, confused**, floppy |
| SpO₂ in air | **≥92%** | **<92%** | <92%, often much lower; **cyanosis** |
| Respiratory rate | ≤40 (2–5 y), ≤30 (>5 y) | **>40 (2–5 y); >30 (>5 y)** | May be **falling** (exhaustion), poor effort |
| Pulse | ≤140 (2–5 y), ≤125 (>5 y) | **>140 (2–5 y); >125 (>5 y)** | **Bradycardia**, arrhythmia, **hypotension** |
| Accessory muscles | Not used or mild | **Used** (sternomastoid, subcostal/intercostal recession, nasal flaring) | Exhausted, paradoxical breathing |
| Chest sounds | Wheeze | Loud wheeze (both phases) | **Silent chest** |
| PEF (≥5–6 y who can do it) | **>50%** predicted/best | **33–50%** | **<33%** |

Any **one** feature of a higher grade puts the child in that grade. Not all features need to be present. **If the parent or child says this is the worst attack ever, believe them.**

### Severity of the underlying asthma (for the discharge plan)

| GINA 2025 term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Well controlled** (in the last 4 weeks) | Daytime symptoms ≤2/week (≤1/week for ≤5 y), no night waking, reliever ≤2/week, no activity limitation |
| **Partly controlled** | 1–2 of the above not met |
| **Uncontrolled** | 3–4 not met |
| **Risk factors for a fatal attack** | Previous ICU/intubation; ≥2 admissions or ≥3 ER visits in the last year; using >1 salbutamol canister a month; not on ICS; recent oral steroids; poor adherence; psychosocial problems; food allergy; poor perception of severity |

UCG 2023 still uses the older "intermittent / mild persistent / moderate persistent / severe persistent" steps for chronic treatment (see Management). Know both vocabularies.

## Epidemiology & Uganda context

- Asthma is **the most common chronic disease of childhood** worldwide (WHO fact sheet, 2026); it affected an estimated **363 million people in 2023** and caused about 442,000 deaths, mostly in low- and middle-income countries.
- In Ugandan schools surveys have found wheeze in roughly **one in ten** children, more in urban Kampala than rural areas, and most are **undiagnosed or using only salbutamol tablets or syrup**.
- **Triggers you will hear about in Kampala**: viral colds (the commonest), **smoke** from charcoal stoves and firewood, kerosene lamps, cigarette smoke, dust (house dust mites, dusty roads, dry season), cold early mornings, cockroaches, pets, exercise, strong perfumes, aspirin/NSAIDs.
- **What is available**:

| Level | Usually available | Often missing |
|---|---|---|
| **HC III** | Salbutamol tablets and inhaler, prednisolone tablets, sometimes a nebuliser, adrenaline | Oxygen, pulse oximeter, spacer |
| **HC IV / general hospital** | Oxygen concentrator, pulse oximeter, nebuliser, salbutamol nebules, hydrocortisone, aminophylline, beclomethasone inhaler | Ipratropium, IV magnesium (stocked for eclampsia in maternity!), peak flow meter |
| **Regional / national referral (Mulago, Mengo, Lubaga, Nsambya)** | All the above, ipratropium, MgSO₄, blood gas (some), HDU, CXR | PICU beds are very few (Mulago, some private hospitals) |

> [!UGANDA]
> **Magnesium sulfate is on every Ugandan labour ward** (50% ampoules for eclampsia), so it is usually available even when the paediatric ward has none. Borrow it, but **calculate the paediatric dose carefully** (40–50 mg/kg, max 2 g) and dilute it: the eclampsia loading dose (4 g IV) would be a dangerous overdose in a child.

> [!UGANDA]
> Many Ugandan children are still treated with **oral salbutamol tablets or syrup** and **oral aminophylline**. They work slowly, cause tremor and tachycardia, and are no substitute for an inhaler with a spacer. A **500 mL plastic water bottle spacer** (a hole cut in the base to fit the inhaler mouthpiece, sealed with tape) is free and works.

## Pathophysiology

### Why the airway narrows

1. **Chronic eosinophilic inflammation** (Th2 cytokines IL-4, IL-5, IL-13; mast cells, IgE) makes the airway **hyper-responsive**.
2. A trigger (usually a **rhinovirus** cold) causes:
   - **Bronchial smooth-muscle spasm** (reverses quickly with **β₂-agonists**; ipratropium blocks vagal tone),
   - **Mucosal oedema** and inflammatory cell influx (reversed over **hours** by **corticosteroids**),
   - **Mucus plugging** (the reason severe attacks respond slowly and can cause atelectasis).
3. Narrowing is worse in **expiration** (airways are compressed when intrathoracic pressure rises), so air is trapped: **hyperinflation**.

### Why children decompensate quickly

- Small airways: resistance is proportional to 1/radius⁴; a little oedema narrows a child's airway far more than an adult's.
- A compliant chest wall: the ribs are drawn in (recession) rather than lifting the chest, so work of breathing is wasted.
- Fewer collateral channels and a diaphragm that fatigues earlier.

### Blood gases through an attack

| Phase | PaO₂ / SpO₂ | PaCO₂ | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early | Normal or slightly low | **Low** (hyperventilation) | Expected |
| Worsening | Falling (V/Q mismatch) | **Normal** | **Danger**: a "normal" CO₂ in a breathless child means tiring |
| Life-threatening | Low | **Rising** (>6 kPa / 45 mmHg) | Respiratory failure: needs HDU/ICU and ventilation |

### Why the drugs work (and their side effects)

| Drug | Mechanism | Main side effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Salbutamol** | β₂-agonist: ↑cAMP, relaxes airway smooth muscle in minutes | Tachycardia, tremor, **hypokalaemia**, raised **lactate**, hyperglycaemia, restlessness |
| **Ipratropium** | Antimuscarinic: blocks vagal bronchoconstriction | Dry mouth; additive to salbutamol in the first hours only |
| **Corticosteroids** | Switch off inflammatory gene transcription, ↓oedema; restore β-receptor response | Effect from **3–4 h**; hyperglycaemia, mood change; short courses are safe |
| **Magnesium sulfate** | Blocks calcium channels in smooth muscle → bronchodilation | Flushing, **hypotension**, weakness, loss of reflexes at high levels |
| **Aminophylline** | Phosphodiesterase inhibitor, adenosine antagonist | **Narrow therapeutic window**: vomiting, tachyarrhythmia, **seizures** |
| **Oxygen** | Corrects V/Q mismatch hypoxaemia | None in asthma at these doses (unlike COPD) |

> [!PEARL]
> The salbutamol paradox: a child who has had hourly nebulised salbutamol can be **tachycardic, tremulous, anxious, with a high lactate and a low potassium**. These are drug effects, not necessarily a worse attack. Look at the **work of breathing and SpO₂** to judge the asthma, and check K⁺ if you have been giving salbutamol continuously.

## Clinical acumen

### Focused history

Take this **while the first salbutamol is running**:

- **Is this asthma?** Previous episodes of wheeze, night cough, exercise cough, relief with inhalers, atopy (eczema, allergic rhinitis), family history. A first-ever wheeze in an infant is more often **bronchiolitis**.
- **This attack**: when it started, trigger (cold, smoke, exercise, food, drug), treatment already taken (how many puffs, with a spacer? tablets?), how they responded.
- **Danger history**: previous **ICU or intubation**, admissions or emergency visits in the past year, oral steroid courses, how much salbutamol they use per month.
- **Usual treatment and adherence**: is there an inhaled steroid at home? Is it taken? Who gives it? Does the child have a spacer?
- **Fever, chest pain, foul sputum** (pneumonia), **sudden onset while eating or playing** (foreign body), **rash, lip swelling, food or sting** (anaphylaxis).
- **Background**: HIV status (and cotrimoxazole/ART), TB contact, growth, immunisations (UNEPI, including PCV and measles), home fuel (charcoal, firewood, kerosene), smokers, school, cost of inhalers.

### Examination

1. **Look first, touch later**: posture (tripod, sitting up), ability to talk or feed, colour, alertness, sweating.
2. **Count** respiratory rate for a full minute; heart rate; **SpO₂ in air** (if safe) then on oxygen.
3. **Work of breathing**: nasal flaring, tracheal tug, subcostal/intercostal recession, sternomastoid use, abdominal breathing; **pulsus paradoxus** is no longer used routinely.
4. **Chest**: hyperinflation, air entry on both sides, wheeze (expiratory, both phases, or **silent**), **crackles or bronchial breathing** (pneumonia, collapse), **unequal air entry** (pneumothorax, foreign body, mucus plug).
5. **Look for complications**: subcutaneous emphysema (crepitus in the neck), tracheal deviation.
6. **Rest of the child**: temperature, hydration, eczema, nasal polyps (think cystic fibrosis), clubbing (asthma does **not** cause clubbing: think bronchiectasis, CF, TB), growth (weight-for-age and height Z-scores), BCG scar.

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> **Life-threatening asthma, act now and call the most senior person:** silent chest, cyanosis, SpO₂ <92% despite oxygen, poor respiratory effort, exhaustion, **drowsiness or confusion**, bradycardia or hypotension, PEF <33%, a normal or rising CO₂. Also: any child who has needed salbutamol every 20 minutes for an hour and is not better.

> [!REDFLAG]
> **Not asthma, or asthma plus something:** unilateral wheeze or reduced air entry (foreign body, pneumothorax); fever with focal crackles (pneumonia); stridor (upper airway); urticaria, facial swelling and hypotension (**anaphylaxis: IM adrenaline first**); a heart murmur, hepatomegaly and crackles (heart failure); chronic cough, weight loss and a TB contact.

### Thinking like a clinician

- **Grade, then treat, then re-grade every 20–30 minutes.** The grade changes, and so must the plan.
- **"All that wheezes is not asthma."** Under 2 years, think bronchiolitis first. Sudden onset with choking = foreign body until proven otherwise. **Unilateral** findings are never asthma alone.
- **Fever is not part of asthma.** UCG 2023: "if fever present, refer to pneumonia". Viral triggers cause low-grade fever, but a high fever with focal signs means look for pneumonia and treat it.
- **Don't be reassured by a falling respiratory rate** unless the child also looks better and SpO₂ is rising.
- **Salbutamol tablets at home do not count as treatment.** A child "on treatment" may never have had an inhaled drug.
- **Agitation is hypoxia until proven otherwise**: give oxygen, never diazepam or promethazine.
- **Anaphylaxis can look like asthma.** Wheeze + urticaria/angioedema + hypotension after a food, drug or sting → **IM adrenaline 0.01 mg/kg (0.01 mL/kg of 1:1000), max 0.5 mg**, into the outer thigh, before anything else.
- **Every admission is a failure of prevention.** Ask yourself: why did this child get this bad? (No ICS? No spacer? Charcoal stove? Cost?)

## Clinical workup

### Step 0: First 5 minutes

1. **ETAT triage**: respiratory distress = **emergency sign** if severe (unable to talk/feed, central cyanosis, SpO₂ <90%, exhaustion). Move the child to the **resuscitation area**.
2. **Sit the child up** on the parent's lap; do not force them to lie down.
3. **Oxygen** by nasal prongs (1–2 L/min) or face mask (5–10 L/min, UCG: at least 5 L/min) to keep **SpO₂ 94–98%**.
4. **Grade the attack** using the table above in under 2 minutes.
5. **First salbutamol now**: spacer (2–10 puffs) or nebulised **2.5 mg** (<5 y) / **2.5–5 mg** (≥5 y), driven by oxygen at 6–8 L/min if severe.
6. **Severe or life-threatening**: add **ipratropium 250 µg** to the nebuliser; call for senior help; prepare IV access; **prednisolone** (or IV hydrocortisone if vomiting or drowsy).
7. **Weigh the child** (or estimate: (age + 4) × 2 kg for 1–10 years) so every subsequent drug is weight-based.

> [!REDFLAG]
> If the child is **drowsy, cyanosed or has a silent chest**: life-threatening asthma. Back-to-back nebulised salbutamol + ipratropium on oxygen, IV access, **IV hydrocortisone**, **IV magnesium** within the first hour, and call the most senior clinician and anaesthetist. Prepare bag-valve-mask. If the child stops breathing, start paediatric life support.

### Step 1: Focused history

- [ ] **Age** → <2 years: bronchiolitis/viral wheeze more likely; ≥5–6 years: can do PEF.
- [ ] **Onset and trigger** → sudden choking = foreign body; food/sting/drug = anaphylaxis; cold = viral exacerbation.
- [ ] **Treatment already given** (number of puffs, spacer, nebuliser, steroid) → a child who had 10 puffs at home and is still severe is a **poor responder**.
- [ ] **Previous ICU, intubation, admissions, steroid courses** → high risk of a fatal attack: lower your threshold to escalate.
- [ ] **Controller use and adherence** → informs discharge plan.
- [ ] **Fever, focal pain, purulent sputum** → pneumonia (antibiotics).
- [ ] **Contacts with TB, weight loss** → TB can present with wheeze from lymph-node compression of a bronchus.
- [ ] **HIV status, cotrimoxazole, ART** → PCP, LIP (lymphoid interstitial pneumonitis) and TB in the differential; drug interactions (theophylline with rifampicin and some ARVs).
- [ ] **Immunisation and nutrition** → measles/pertussis; malnutrition changes fluids and risks.
- [ ] **Home environment** → charcoal/firewood smoke, smokers, dust: trigger control.

### Step 2: Focused examination

**General**: posture, talk/feed, colour, consciousness (AVPU), sweating, hydration, weight.

**Vital signs**: RR (full minute), HR, SpO₂ (in air if safe, then on O₂), temperature, BP if severe (hypotension is late and ominous), capillary refill.

**Respiratory**: recession (subcostal, intercostal, suprasternal), nasal flaring, accessory muscles, chest shape (hyperinflation, Harrison sulci in chronic asthma), tracheal position, air entry equal?, wheeze, crackles, silent areas.

**Other**: eczema, allergic shiners, nasal polyps, clubbing (not asthma), murmurs, hepatomegaly, growth.

| Document these positives | And these negatives |
|---|---|
| Talk/feed ability and AVPU | "No silent chest; air entry equal" |
| SpO₂ in air and on O₂; RR, HR | "No focal crackles, no bronchial breathing" |
| Recession and accessory muscle use | "No stridor; no urticaria or angioedema" |
| Response after each salbutamol (time-stamped) | "Trachea central; no surgical emphysema" |

### Step 3: Bedside tests

| Test | How | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Pulse oximetry** | Correct-size probe, warm finger or toe, wait for a good waveform | **<92% in air = severe**; guides oxygen (target 94–98%). May overestimate SpO₂ in dark skin: read with the clinical picture |
| **Peak expiratory flow** (≥5–6 y) | Best of 3 blows standing; compare with personal best or predicted for height | <50% severe, <33% life-threatening; repeat 15–30 min after each treatment |
| **Random blood sugar** | Glucometer | Hypoglycaemia in a child who has not fed; hyperglycaemia from salbutamol/steroids |
| **Malaria RDT** (fever) | Finger prick | Co-existing malaria is common; fever needs a cause |
| **Response to salbutamol** | Re-grade 15–20 min after each dose | The most important "test"; a good response supports asthma |

### Step 4: Laboratory & imaging

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chest X-ray** | **Not routine.** Only if: life-threatening attack, poor response, suspected pneumothorax, focal signs, fever, first episode with doubt about diagnosis, foreign body | Hyperinflation, peribronchial thickening (normal for asthma); **pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, collapse/consolidation, foreign body** | Chest drain for tension/large pneumothorax; antibiotics for pneumonia; bronchoscopy for foreign body. Available at HC IV (some) and all hospitals |
| **Blood gas** (capillary or arterial) | Life-threatening asthma or poor response; HDU | Normal/rising **pCO₂ (>6 kPa / 45 mmHg)** with hypoxaemia = respiratory failure; lactic acidosis from salbutamol | HDU/ICU, consider ventilation. **Mulago/private ICUs only**; many RRHs have no gas machine |
| **Serum potassium** | Repeated or continuous salbutamol; on aminophylline | **Hypokalaemia** (<3.5 mmol/L) | Add potassium to IV fluids. RRH/NRH |
| **Full blood count** | Fever, suspected infection | Neutrophilia (pneumonia; but steroids also raise WBC), eosinophilia (atopy, worms) | Antibiotics only for clear bacterial infection |
| **Theophylline level** | On aminophylline infusion with vomiting, arrhythmia or seizures | Toxic level | Stop infusion. Rarely available in Uganda: watch clinically |
| **HIV test** | Status unknown | Positive | ART, cotrimoxazole; widen differential (LIP, PCP, TB) |
| **GeneXpert (sputum/gastric/stool)** | Persistent cough >2 weeks, weight loss, TB contact | MTB detected | TB treatment (see the childhood TB topic) |

### Step 5: Putting it together

**Model one-line summary**
> "A 7-year-old boy weighing 22 kg (weight-for-age Z-score −0.5), fully immunised, HIV-negative, known asthmatic on salbutamol inhaler only, with a 1-day history of worsening breathlessness after a cold. On arrival he could speak only in words, SpO₂ 89% in air, RR 38, HR 138, widespread wheeze, PEF 40% of predicted: **acute severe asthma** triggered by a viral URTI, in a child with poorly controlled asthma on no controller."

**Problem list**
1. Acute severe asthma, on treatment, response being assessed.
2. Hypoxaemia (SpO₂ 89% in air).
3. Poorly controlled chronic asthma: no ICS, no spacer, charcoal stove at home.
4. Viral URTI trigger (no evidence of pneumonia).

**Working diagnosis**: *acute severe exacerbation of asthma, viral trigger, uncontrolled background asthma.*

> [!EXAM]
> **Worked example 1: re-grading after treatment.** At 0 min: SpO₂ 89%, RR 38, HR 138, speaks in words, PEF 40% → **severe**. Given O₂, salbutamol 5 mg + ipratropium 250 µg nebulised ×3 over 1 h, prednisolone 40 mg (2 mg/kg would be 44 mg; max 40 mg). At 60 min: SpO₂ 95% on 2 L, RR 28, HR 150, speaks in sentences, PEF 65%, tremor. **Interpretation**: responding well; the higher HR and tremor are salbutamol effects. Plan: space salbutamol to 1–2-hourly, then 4-hourly; wean oxygen; continue prednisolone for 3 days; start ICS before discharge.

> [!EXAM]
> **Worked example 2: a "normal" blood gas that is not normal.** A 9-year-old girl after 2 hours of back-to-back nebulisers: drowsy, RR 22, quiet chest, SpO₂ 91% on mask. Capillary gas: pH 7.30, **pCO₂ 6.4 kPa (48 mmHg)**, lactate 3.5. **Interpretation**: a breathless asthmatic should be blowing off CO₂ (low pCO₂). A normal or high pCO₂ with drowsiness and a falling RR = **exhaustion and respiratory failure**. This is life-threatening asthma: IV magnesium now (if not given), consider aminophylline or IV salbutamol in HDU, and call the anaesthetist for possible intubation.

## Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing features | Key investigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Bronchiolitis** | <2 years (peak 2–6 months), first episode, coryza then cough, **fine crackles** and wheeze, little response to salbutamol | Clinical; SpO₂ |
| **Pneumonia** | Fever, fast breathing, chest indrawing, **focal crackles/bronchial breathing**, no response to bronchodilators | CXR, FBC |
| **Foreign body aspiration** | Sudden choking while eating/playing (groundnuts, beans, maize), **unilateral** wheeze or reduced air entry, no atopy | Inspiratory and expiratory CXR; bronchoscopy |
| **Anaphylaxis** | Trigger (food, drug, sting), urticaria, angioedema, hypotension, vomiting | Clinical; **IM adrenaline** |
| **Croup / upper airway obstruction** | **Inspiratory stridor**, barking cough, hoarse voice | Clinical |
| **Heart failure** (VSD, RHD, cardiomyopathy) | Murmur, gallop, **hepatomegaly**, basal crackles, poor growth, sweating with feeds | CXR (cardiomegaly), echo |
| **Pulmonary TB / TB lymph nodes** | Persistent cough >2 weeks, weight loss, fever, TB contact; wheeze from node compression | Xpert, CXR (hilar nodes), TST |
| **HIV-related lung disease** (LIP, PCP, bronchiectasis) | HIV-positive, clubbing, parotid swelling (LIP), hypoxia out of proportion (PCP) | HIV test, CXR |
| **Recurrent aspiration / GORD** | Cough with feeds, cerebral palsy, vomiting | Clinical; swallow assessment |
| **Bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis** | Chronic wet cough, **clubbing**, failure to thrive | CXR, sweat test (rarely available) |
| **Tropical pulmonary eosinophilia / Loeffler (ascaris larval migration)** | Wheeze, eosinophilia, worms | FBC (eosinophils), stool |

## Investigations

| Test | What you are looking for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| SpO₂ | <92% in air = severe | Guides oxygen and grade |
| PEF (≥5 y) | % predicted or best | Objective severity and response |
| CXR (selected) | Pneumothorax, consolidation, foreign body | Excludes complications and mimics |
| Blood gas (severe) | Rising pCO₂ | Detects respiratory failure |
| Potassium, glucose | Hypokalaemia, hyper/hypoglycaemia | Drug side effects |
| Spirometry with reversibility (when well, ≥5–6 y) | FEV₁/FVC <0.8–0.9; **FEV₁ rise ≥12%** after salbutamol (UCG 2023) | Confirms asthma diagnosis later in clinic |

## Management

### A. Principles

1. **Treat by grade and re-grade every 20–30 minutes.**
2. **Inhaled β₂-agonist is the backbone**; a spacer is as good as a nebuliser in mild–moderate and most severe attacks.
3. **Give a steroid early** (within the first hour) for any attack needing more than a single salbutamol dose.
4. **Escalate stepwise and quickly** when there is no response: ipratropium → IV magnesium → aminophylline (or IV salbutamol in HDU) → ventilation.
5. **No sedatives, no routine antibiotics, no cough syrups, no mucolytics, no chest physiotherapy** in the acute attack.
6. **Before discharge**: controller, technique, spacer, action plan, follow-up.

### B. Emergency algorithm (based on UCG 2023, GINA 2025, BTS/SIGN)

1. **Assess and grade** (2 minutes). Weigh.
2. **All grades**: sit up; **oxygen** if SpO₂ <94% (target **94–98%**).
3. **Salbutamol**:
   - **Mild–moderate**: pMDI 100 µg/puff via spacer, **2–10 puffs** (one puff at a time, 5 tidal breaths per puff), **every 20 minutes for 1 hour**; or nebulised 2.5 mg (<5 y) / 2.5–5 mg (≥5 y).
   - **Severe/life-threatening**: **nebulised salbutamol 2.5–5 mg + ipratropium 250 µg**, oxygen-driven (6–8 L/min), every 20 minutes (back-to-back if life-threatening).
4. **Steroid in the first hour**: prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg orally (max 20 mg <2 y, 30 mg 2–5 y, 40 mg ≥6 y), or **hydrocortisone 4 mg/kg IV** (max 100 mg) 6-hourly if vomiting, drowsy or life-threatening.
5. **Re-grade at 1 hour**:
   - **Good response** (talking, SpO₂ ≥94% in air, PEF >60–80%): space salbutamol to 1–4-hourly; observe 1–4 h; plan discharge.
   - **Incomplete response**: admit; continue salbutamol 1–2-hourly + ipratropium for the first 2 hours (then 4–6-hourly); steroid; oxygen.
   - **Poor response / life-threatening**: **IV magnesium sulfate 40–50 mg/kg (max 2 g) over 20 min**; senior review; HDU.
6. **Still not improving**: **aminophylline** 5 mg/kg IV over 20 min (omit if on oral theophylline), then 1 mg/kg/h infusion with cardiac monitoring; or **IV salbutamol** (HDU only, 15 µg/kg over 10 min, then infusion 1–5 µg/kg/min) where available.
7. **Respiratory failure** (exhaustion, rising CO₂, falling consciousness): anaesthetist/ICU; bag-valve-mask; intubation by the most experienced person (ketamine induction).
8. **Throughout**: monitor SpO₂, RR, HR, work of breathing every 15–30 min; K⁺ and glucose if prolonged treatment; IV fluids at **maintenance only** if not drinking (no boluses unless shocked).

> [!DRUG]
> **Never sedate** a breathless asthmatic child (diazepam, promethazine, phenobarbital, chloral). Restlessness is hypoxia. Sedation removes the drive that is keeping them alive.

> [!UGANDA]
> UCG 2023 dosing differs slightly from international guides: **prednisolone 1 mg/kg** (GINA/BTS 1–2 mg/kg), **hydrocortisone 4 mg/kg (max 100 mg)**, **ipratropium 250 µg** in children <12 y, **aminophylline 5 mg/kg slow IV**, and **no magnesium** listed for children. Most Kampala referral wards (Mulago, Mengo, Nsambya) now use **IV MgSO₄ before aminophylline**, in line with GINA 2025 and BTS/SIGN. Quote UCG in the written test and the international sequence when asked "what else could you give?"

### D. Setting-specific plan

| Setting | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Home / VHT** | Salbutamol 2–4 puffs via spacer, repeat every 20 min up to 10 puffs; if not better within 1 hour or any danger sign, go to the nearest facility (action plan) |
| **HC III** (UCG level for mild–moderate) | Salbutamol by spacer or nebuliser every 20–30 min, prednisolone 1 mg/kg; observe 30–60 min. **Refer** if not improving, relapse within 3–4 h, or severe features |
| **HC IV / general hospital** (UCG level for severe) | Admit; oxygen; salbutamol + ipratropium; prednisolone or hydrocortisone; aminophylline (UCG) or **MgSO₄** (borrowed from maternity) if not responding; refer life-threatening asthma after first aid |
| **Regional / national referral** | All of the above; MgSO₄; HDU monitoring; blood gas; CXR; IV salbutamol; PICU/ventilation (Mulago and a few private hospitals) |

**Referral checklist**: oxygen during transfer (cylinder or concentrator with inverter), a health worker who can give salbutamol on the way, steroid given, referral note with times and doses, call ahead.

### E. Admitted child: ward care

- **Observations** every 15–30 min initially (RR, HR, SpO₂, work of breathing, talk/feed), then 1–4-hourly as improving.
- **Salbutamol** weaned stepwise: 20-minutely → hourly → 2-hourly → 4-hourly. Switch nebuliser → spacer as soon as possible (it trains technique).
- **Oxygen** weaned when SpO₂ ≥94% in air.
- **Fluids**: encourage oral fluids; if IV needed, **maintenance only** (Holliday–Segar: 100 mL/kg/day for the first 10 kg, 50 mL/kg/day for the next 10 kg, 20 mL/kg/day thereafter); avoid overload (high negative intrathoracic pressure promotes pulmonary oedema; SIADH can occur).
- **Antibiotics only** if fever with focal signs, CXR consolidation or clear bacterial infection.
- **Teach** inhaler + spacer technique on the ward, every day, to the parent and child.

### F. Discharge plan (the part that prevents the next attack)

**Ready for discharge when**:
- Stable on salbutamol **3–4-hourly** (not needing it more often),
- **SpO₂ ≥94% in air** (UCG flowchart: >94%),
- PEF **>60–80%** of best/predicted (if able),
- Eating, drinking and talking normally,
- Parent/child has demonstrated **correct inhaler and spacer technique**.

**Send home with**:
1. **Prednisolone** to complete **3 days** (UCG 2023 for children; up to 5 days).
2. **Salbutamol pMDI + spacer**: 2–4 puffs 4-hourly for 1–2 days, then as needed.
3. **Start or step up a controller**: e.g. **beclomethasone 100 µg twice daily** via spacer (UCG Step 2 for children: 100–200 µg 12-hourly), or ICS-formoterol in adolescents.
4. **Written asthma action plan** (green/yellow/red zones): what to do when well, when getting worse, and when to come straight to hospital.
5. **Trigger advice**: move cooking smoke out of the house or improve ventilation, no smoking indoors, damp-dust, treat colds early.
6. **Follow-up** within **2 days to 1 week** (UCG: review in 48 h for mild cases; 1–2 weeks for chronic plan), and at 1–3 months.
7. **School letter** to allow the inhaler at school and during sports.

### G. Chronic control: stepwise treatment

**GINA 2025** (current international standard):

| Step | Children ≤5 y | Children 6–11 y | Adolescents ≥12 y (Track 1, preferred) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | As-needed SABA (consider short course of ICS at the start of viral illness) | Low-dose ICS taken **whenever SABA is taken** | **As-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol** (AIR) |
| **2** | **Daily low-dose ICS** (preferred) | **Daily low-dose ICS** | As-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol |
| **3** | **Double** low-dose ICS | Low-dose ICS-LABA, or medium-dose ICS, or very-low-dose ICS-formoterol **MART** | **Low-dose MART** (ICS-formoterol maintenance and reliever) |
| **4** | Continue controller and **refer** | Medium-dose ICS-LABA or low-dose MART; refer | Medium-dose MART |
| **5** | – | Phenotype; add-on (e.g. tiotropium, biologics); specialist | Add-on LAMA; phenotype; biologics; specialist |

**Low daily ICS doses (GINA 2025)**: beclomethasone dipropionate (HFA/extrafine) **50–100 µg/day** (6–11 y), 100 µg/day (≤5 y); budesonide DPI 100–200 µg/day; fluticasone propionate 50–100 µg/day. **Always via a spacer in children.**

> [!UGANDA]
> **UCG 2023 chronic asthma steps** (older format): Step 1 intermittent → salbutamol as needed; Step 2 mild persistent → **beclomethasone 100–200 µg every 12 h** (children) + salbutamol prn; Step 3 moderate persistent → beclomethasone 100–400 µg 12-hourly (5–12 y); **children <5 y refer to a specialist**; Step 4 severe → **refer** (especially children <12 y). If no inhaler: salbutamol tablets (2–5 y 1–2 mg per dose; <2 y 100 µg/kg) as a last resort. Rescue prednisolone course: <1 y 1–2 mg/kg; 1–5 y up to 20 mg; 5–15 y up to 40 mg daily for 3–5 days. ICS-formoterol (budesonide-formoterol) is available in Kampala private pharmacies and some referral clinics but is expensive; **beclomethasone + salbutamol inhalers are what most Ugandan families can get.** GINA 2025 no longer recommends **SABA-only** treatment for anyone ≥6 years, because it increases the risk of severe attacks.

**Every review**: check **symptom control, inhaler technique, adherence, triggers, growth** (ICS at low dose has a tiny effect on height, outweighed by the benefit), and step down after 3 months of good control.

## Complications

| Complication | Clues | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Respiratory failure / arrest** | Exhaustion, silent chest, drowsiness, rising CO₂ | ICU, ventilation |
| **Pneumothorax / pneumomediastinum** | Sudden deterioration, unilateral reduced air entry, **subcutaneous emphysema** | CXR; needle decompression then chest drain if tension |
| **Atelectasis / lobar collapse** (mucus plug) | Focal reduced breath sounds, CXR collapse | Usually resolves; physiotherapy after the acute phase |
| **Secondary pneumonia** | Fever, focal signs | Antibiotics |
| **Drug toxicity** | Salbutamol: tachycardia, hypokalaemia, lactic acidosis; aminophylline: vomiting, arrhythmia, seizures; Mg: hypotension | Reduce or stop drug, check K⁺ |
| **Dehydration** | Poor intake, fast breathing | Oral or IV maintenance fluids |
| **Long-term** | Missed school, poor growth (uncontrolled asthma or repeated oral steroids), fixed airflow limitation, psychosocial stress, death | Controller therapy, education |

## Ward-round & exam pearls

**Model case presentation (child):**
> "Brian is a 7-year-old boy from Kawempe, weighing 22 kg (weight-for-age Z-score −0.5, height-for-age Z-score 0), fully immunised by UNEPI including PCV and both measles-rubella doses, HIV-negative (tested last year), on a normal family diet. He is a known asthmatic diagnosed at 4 years, using only a salbutamol inhaler without a spacer; no inhaled steroid. He presented at 2 a.m. with one day of cough and worsening breathlessness after a cold; his mother cooks on a charcoal stove indoors. He has had two admissions this year, no ICU stay. On arrival he could say only words, was sitting forward, SpO₂ 89% in air, RR 38, HR 138, temperature 37.6 °C, with intercostal recession and widespread expiratory wheeze, equal air entry, no crackles; PEF 40% of predicted. Assessment: **acute severe asthma**, viral trigger, on a background of uncontrolled asthma. He received oxygen, three back-to-back nebulisers of salbutamol 5 mg with ipratropium 250 µg, and prednisolone 40 mg. At 1 hour he speaks in sentences, SpO₂ 95% on 2 L, RR 28, PEF 65%. Plan: salbutamol 2-hourly then 4-hourly by spacer, prednisolone for 3 days, wean oxygen, start beclomethasone 100 µg twice daily via a bottle spacer, teach technique, written action plan, smoke advice, review in clinic in 1 week."

### How to present this tutorial (Edith presents, Week 1 Tuesday, 08:00–09:30, Dr Mawanda)

**Your goal**: in about **40 minutes of presenting + 30 minutes of discussion**, leave every classmate able to **grade an attack, treat it for the first hour, and write a discharge plan**. Dr Mawanda will judge you on clarity, accuracy of doses, and whether you can handle questions.

**Suggested structure and timing**

| Time | Segment | What to say / do |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 min | **Hook case** | Read a 4-line vignette (e.g. Brian above, at 2 a.m., speaking in words, SpO₂ 89%). Ask: "What grade is this, and what is your first action?" Take 2 answers. Don't correct yet |
| 3–8 min | **Definition & burden** | GINA 2025 definition (variable symptoms + variable airflow limitation); acute exacerbation definition; one Uganda slide: triggers (charcoal smoke, viral colds), what is available at HC III vs RRH |
| 8–14 min | **Pathophysiology in one diagram** | Draw the airway cross-section: smooth muscle (→ salbutamol, ipratropium, Mg), oedema/inflammation (→ steroids), mucus. Show the CO₂ phases (low → normal → high) |
| 14–22 min | **Severity grading table** | The single most important slide. Put the UCG 2023 table up and walk the columns. Emphasise **silent chest** and **normal CO₂ = danger** |
| 22–32 min | **First-hour management algorithm** | Oxygen 94–98% → salbutamol every 20 min (spacer dose and nebuliser dose) → steroid in first hour → ipratropium if severe → IV MgSO₄ 40–50 mg/kg max 2 g → aminophylline → ICU. Then show the **drug table** with mg/kg and max doses. Highlight UCG vs GINA differences in one [UGANDA] box |
| 32–36 min | **Discharge & chronic control** | Discharge criteria, the 7-item discharge pack, GINA 2025 steps in one simplified table, the bottle spacer (bring one if you can and **demonstrate** it) |
| 36–40 min | **Back to the hook case** | Answer it fully; summarise 5 take-home messages |
| 40–70 min | **Questions and discussion** | Use the viva questions below as backup; ask the class two MCQs from the study set |

**Tips for presenting well**
- **Open with the case and close with the same case.** It makes the talk feel complete.
- **One idea per slide or board section**; no paragraphs. Tables beat text. If there is no projector, draw the **severity table** and the **algorithm** on the board before 08:00.
- **Say doses out loud with units and maxima** ("prednisolone one to two milligrams per kilogram, maximum forty milligrams in a seven-year-old"). Tutors notice dose errors more than anything else.
- **Name your sources**: "UCG 2023 says… GINA 2025 adds…". Have the UCG page numbers (acute asthma pp. 331–336, chronic asthma pp. 337–339) ready.
- **Demonstrate** the spacer technique (5 breaths per puff, one puff at a time). A 30-second demonstration is remembered better than any slide.
- **Prepare a one-page handout**: the severity table, the first-hour algorithm, and the drug table.
- **If you don't know an answer**, say: "I'm not sure; I'll check the UCG and get back to you after the ward round." Then do it.
- **Rehearse once with a timer** the night before; most first-time presenters overrun by 15 minutes.

**Questions Dr Mawanda is likely to ask you (prepare these)**
1. *How do you distinguish asthma from bronchiolitis in a 14-month-old?* Age, first vs recurrent episode, crackles vs wheeze, atopy, response to salbutamol.
2. *Why is a silent chest dangerous?* No airflow; impending respiratory arrest.
3. *What is the dose and route of magnesium, and why dilute it?* 40–50 mg/kg IV over 20 min, max 2 g; 50% solution is hyperosmolar and irritant; dilute to ≤20%.
4. *Why don't we give antibiotics?* Exacerbations are mostly viral; antibiotics only for clear bacterial infection.
5. *What would you do differently at a HC III?* Spacer salbutamol, oral prednisolone, observe 1 hour, refer severe cases with oxygen if available.
6. *How does GINA 2025 differ from UCG 2023 for chronic asthma?* GINA: no SABA-only treatment ≥6 y; ICS whenever SABA used (6–11 Step 1); ICS-formoterol as reliever in ≥12; UCG still uses SABA-only Step 1 and beclomethasone at Step 2.

### Likely viva questions with answers

1. *Define asthma.* A chronic inflammatory airway disease with variable symptoms of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough, and variable expiratory airflow limitation (GINA 2025).
2. *Name three features of life-threatening asthma.* Silent chest, cyanosis/SpO₂ <92%, poor respiratory effort, exhaustion, altered consciousness, hypotension, PEF <33%.
3. *Salbutamol dose by spacer and by nebuliser?* 2–10 puffs (100 µg each) via spacer; 2.5 mg (<5 y) or 2.5–5 mg (≥5 y) nebulised; every 20 min in the first hour.
4. *When do you give ipratropium?* Severe or life-threatening attacks, 250 µg every 20–30 min for the first 2 hours, with salbutamol.
5. *Side effects of salbutamol?* Tachycardia, tremor, hypokalaemia, lactic acidosis, hyperglycaemia.
6. *Why is aminophylline less favoured?* Narrow therapeutic index (arrhythmias, seizures, vomiting), interactions, little added benefit over inhaled therapy + steroids.
7. *What makes a child at high risk of dying from asthma?* Previous ICU/intubation, frequent admissions, overuse of SABA, no ICS, poor adherence, psychosocial problems.

### OSCE tips

- **Inhaler/spacer station**: shake, attach, seal lips (or mask over nose and mouth for <3–4 y), one puff, 5 tidal breaths (or one slow deep breath and hold 10 s for older children), wait 30 s, repeat; rinse mouth after ICS; clean spacer weekly, air-dry (reduces static).
- **PEF station**: stand, reset the needle, deep breath, lips sealed, short sharp blow, best of three, compare with chart for height.
- **Counselling station**: explain reliever vs preventer ("blue opens the airway now; brown/orange calms the swelling over weeks, take it every day even when well").

## Mnemonics & memory aids

- **Life-threatening asthma: "33, 92, CHEST"**: PEF **<33%**, SpO₂ **<92%**, **C**yanosis, **H**ypotension, **E**xhaustion/altered consciousness, **S**ilent chest, **T**ired (poor effort).
- **Treatment ladder: "O SHIMA"**: **O**xygen, **S**albutamol, **H**ydrocortisone/prednisolone, **I**pratropium, **M**agnesium, **A**minophylline (then ICU).
- **Discharge "S-P-A-C-E-R"**: **S**teroid course, **P**reventer inhaler, **A**ction plan, **C**heck technique, **E**nvironment/triggers, **R**eview date.
- **Salbutamol side effects: "TTT-L"**: **T**achycardia, **T**remor, **T**oo-little potassium, **L**actate.

### Classic exam traps

- **"The wheeze has gone, so he's better."** Not if he is drowsy with a falling RR: **silent chest**.
- **"Give diazepam because the child is agitated."** Never. Agitation = hypoxia.
- **"Start amoxicillin for every admitted asthmatic."** No: antibiotics only with evidence of bacterial infection.
- **"Nebuliser is always better than a spacer."** No: spacer is as effective in mild–moderate attacks and teaches technique.
- **"Aminophylline loading dose for everyone."** No: omit the load if the child takes oral theophylline/aminophylline; and MgSO₄ comes first in current guidelines.
- **"Prednisolone must be tapered."** Not after a 3–5-day course.
- **"First wheeze at 6 months = asthma."** Usually bronchiolitis.
- **"A normal pCO₂ is reassuring."** In a breathless asthmatic it means tiring.

### Questions seniors ask

**What is your first action for a child with severe asthma?**
Sit them up, oxygen to SpO₂ 94–98%, and start salbutamol (with ipratropium if severe) every 20 minutes while you grade the attack and weigh the child.

**When would you give magnesium, and how much?**
For severe or life-threatening asthma not responding to an hour of inhaled salbutamol + ipratropium and a steroid: 40–50 mg/kg IV over 20 minutes, max 2 g, diluted, with BP monitoring.

**How do you know your treatment is working?**
Re-grade every 20–30 min: talks in sentences, falling RR and work of breathing, SpO₂ rising, PEF rising; tachycardia alone can be a salbutamol effect.

**What would you send this child home with?**
Prednisolone to complete 3 days, salbutamol with a spacer, an inhaled steroid, a written action plan, trigger advice, a school letter and a review in 2–7 days.

**Why does this child keep coming back?**
Usually no controller, poor technique (no spacer), poor adherence, indoor smoke, or wrong diagnosis (TB, foreign body, heart disease). Check all five.

**How would you treat anaphylaxis presenting with wheeze?**
IM adrenaline 0.01 mg/kg (max 0.5 mg) into the thigh, repeat in 5 minutes if needed, oxygen, fluids for shock, then salbutamol, steroid and antihistamine as add-ons.

**Which children with asthma need a specialist?**
Under-5s needing more than Step 2 (UCG), any child at Step 4, life-threatening attacks, poor growth, doubt about the diagnosis.
