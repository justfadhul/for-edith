## In a nutshell

The newborn has a narrow margin of safety: little energy reserve, immature immunity and a big surface area. The same few problems cause most admissions and deaths after the first day: **sepsis**, **jaundice**, **hypoglycaemia**, **hypothermia**, **local infections** (cord, eyes, skin) and **birth injuries**, often together in the same baby.

Why it matters in Uganda: **infection** is one of the three leading causes of neonatal death (with prematurity and asphyxia). Severe jaundice causes **kernicterus**, which still leaves Ugandan children with athetoid cerebral palsy and deafness. Hypothermia and hypoglycaemia are cheap to prevent and deadly when missed.

The approach is the **IMNCI** one: look for **danger signs** first, classify, treat (often first-dose antibiotics and glucose before referral), keep warm, keep feeding, and counsel the mother on when to return.

> [!PEARL]
> The one thing never to forget: **a newborn shows almost every serious illness the same way** (poor feeding, lethargy, temperature instability, fast breathing, jaundice). Check **temperature and glucose**, and if any danger sign is present, **start antibiotics** after a blood culture (where available) and **do not wait** for results.

## Definitions & classification

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Neonatal period** | First **28 days** of life (early neonatal = first 7 days) |
| **Early-onset sepsis (EOS)** | Onset in the first **72 hours** (some use 7 days); usually maternal organisms acquired in labour |
| **Late-onset sepsis (LOS)** | Onset after **72 hours**; community or hospital-acquired (hands, equipment, cord) |
| **Possible serious bacterial infection (PSBI)** | IMNCI classification for a young infant with any danger sign; treat as sepsis |
| **Physiological jaundice** | Appears **after 24 h**, peaks day 3-5 (term), mild, baby well, gone by **14 days** (term) / 21 days (preterm) |
| **Pathological jaundice** | **First 24 h**, rapidly rising, above treatment thresholds, **palms/soles yellow**, baby unwell, conjugated, or prolonged |
| **Neonatal hypoglycaemia** | Blood glucose **below 2.6 mmol/L** (operational threshold in Ugandan units; WHO Pocket Book 2.2; NICE/BAPM 2.0) |
| **Hypothermia** | Axillary **below 36.5 C**: cold stress 36.0-36.4, moderate 32.0-35.9, **severe below 32** |
| **Omphalitis** | Infection of the umbilical stump; "**local**" (red, pus) vs **periumbilical flare** (redness spreading to the abdominal wall = serious) |
| **Ophthalmia neonatorum** | Conjunctivitis in the first **28-30 days** of life, classically gonococcal or chlamydial |

## Epidemiology & Uganda context

- Sepsis in Ugandan and other sub-Saharan newborn units is commonly caused by **Klebsiella pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli** and other Gram-negatives; **Group B Streptococcus** is less often isolated than in HICs but does occur. **Resistance to ampicillin and gentamicin is common** in hospital-acquired infections, which is why second-line drugs and cultures matter.
- **Hand hygiene, chlorhexidine cord care, exclusive breastfeeding and KMC** are the main prevention tools. Harmful traditional practices (cow dung, ash or herbs on the cord; "sucking" the baby's mouth; prelacteal feeds) still occur.
- **Jaundice**: G6PD deficiency is common in Uganda, as are ABO incompatibility, sepsis and prematurity; Rh disease is less common because few Ugandan women are Rh-negative, but it is severe when it occurs. Many babies are discharged within 24 h, so **jaundice is often first seen at home** and presents late.
- **Facilities**: HC III/IV can give first-dose IM antibiotics, glucose/feeds, KMC and refer; phototherapy and bilirubin measurement are mainly at **general/regional and national referral hospitals** (and private-not-for-profit hospitals such as Mengo, Nsambya, Lubaga); **exchange transfusion** at a few referral centres.

## Pathophysiology

**Why newborns get infected easily:** low complement and neutrophil reserves, little IgM, IgG mainly from the mother (crosses after 32 weeks, so preterms lack it), thin skin, an open umbilical wound, and exposure to maternal organisms in labour (prolonged ROM, chorioamnionitis). Sepsis spreads quickly to the meninges (up to a quarter of septic newborns have meningitis).

**Why newborns get jaundiced:** high red cell mass with a short lifespan (70-90 days) → a large bilirubin load; immature **UDP-glucuronyl transferase** in the liver; more **enterohepatic circulation** (gut beta-glucuronidase, sterile gut, slow passage of meconium). Unconjugated bilirubin is fat-soluble and, when very high (or with acidosis, sepsis, prematurity, low albumin, drugs that displace it such as ceftriaxone), crosses the blood-brain barrier and stains the **basal ganglia** → **acute bilirubin encephalopathy** and **kernicterus**.

**Why newborns get hypoglycaemic:** after the cord is cut, glucose supply stops; the baby relies on glycogen (small in preterm/SGA), gluconeogenesis and ketones from milk. Risk is greatest in **preterm, SGA, infants of diabetic mothers (hyperinsulinism), asphyxia, sepsis, hypothermia and delayed feeding**.

**Why newborns get cold:** large surface area, wet skin at birth (evaporation), little fat, cannot shiver (rely on brown fat, which uses oxygen and glucose). Cold → hypoglycaemia, acidosis, apnoea, sepsis, death.

## Clinical acumen

### Focused history

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Age in hours/days** | EOS vs LOS; jaundice timing (day 1 = pathological) |
| **Feeding**: breastfeeding? how often? last good feed? other fluids/prelacteal feeds? | Poor feeding is the most common sign of illness; cause of hypoglycaemia and dehydration |
| **Maternal fever, ROM over 18 h, foul liquor, UTI in labour** | EOS risk; give antibiotics |
| **Gestation and birth weight** | Preterm/LBW: sepsis, jaundice and hypoglycaemia risk; lower bilirubin thresholds |
| **Mother's blood group (and Rh), previous baby with jaundice/exchange** | ABO/Rh haemolysis |
| **Family history of G6PD deficiency or jaundice; exposure to naphthalene/herbs** | Haemolysis triggers |
| **Stool and urine colour** | Pale stools/dark urine = conjugated (cholestatic) jaundice |
| **Cord care**: what was applied? | Omphalitis, tetanus |
| **Eye discharge onset** | Day 2-5 gonococcal; day 5-14 chlamydial |
| **Delivery**: vacuum, breech, shoulder dystocia, big baby | Birth injuries |
| **Maternal HIV (last VL), syphilis (RPR), HBsAg, TB** | Prophylaxis and congenital infection |
| **Immunisation**: BCG, OPV0 (HepB birth dose) | Missed doses; BCG ulcer vs abscess |

### Examination

- **Vital signs**: temperature (axillary), RR (count 60 s; repeat if 60 or more), HR, SpO2, CRT, weight (compare with birth weight).
- **Danger signs** (WHO/UCG): not feeding, convulsions, lethargy/unconscious, movement only when stimulated, RR 60 or more, severe indrawing, grunting, temperature above 37.5 or below 35.5 C, central cyanosis.
- **Jaundice**: press the skin in good daylight; note how far down it extends (face → chest → abdomen → limbs → **palms and soles**). Look for pallor, hepatosplenomegaly, cephalhaematoma, bruising.
- **Neurology** (in jaundice): lethargy, poor suck, hypotonia → hypertonia, retrocollis, opisthotonus, high-pitched cry (acute bilirubin encephalopathy).
- **Cord**: redness, pus, smell, **flare** spreading onto the abdominal wall, bleeding.
- **Eyes**: swelling, pus (copious = gonococcal), corneal haze.
- **Skin**: pustules (count: fewer than 10 vs 10 or more), bullae, abscess, cellulitis, peeling.
- **Mouth**: thrush (white patches that do not wipe off).
- **Bones and nerves**: arm movement (Erb's palsy), clavicle crepitus, scalp swellings.

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - Any **danger sign** (above) = PSBI: first-dose antibiotics and urgent referral/admission
> - **Jaundice on day 1**, **yellow palms/soles**, jaundice with **lethargy, poor suck, high-pitched cry, arching**
> - **Pale stools / dark urine** with jaundice (biliary atresia)
> - **Glucose below 2.6 mmol/L** with symptoms, or persisting despite feeds
> - **Temperature below 35.5 C** or above 37.5 C
> - **Periumbilical flare** or bleeding from the cord
> - **Copious purulent eye discharge** with lid swelling in the first week
> - **Bulging fontanelle**, convulsions (meningitis)
> - **Bilious vomiting** or abdominal distension (surgical emergency/NEC)

### Thinking like a clinician

- **Sepsis looks like everything.** Hypothermia, hypoglycaemia, jaundice, apnoea, poor feeding and "just not right" can each be the only sign. Lower your threshold to treat.
- **Fever in a newborn is serious, but so is a low temperature.** Hypothermia is more common than fever in neonatal sepsis, especially in preterms.
- **Early jaundice = haemolysis or sepsis; late or prolonged jaundice = breast milk, hypothyroidism, sepsis/UTI, or cholestasis.** Always look at the stool colour.
- **A jaundiced baby who is sleepy and feeding poorly is either septic or developing bilirubin encephalopathy.** Both are emergencies.
- **Do not give ceftriaxone to a deeply jaundiced or preterm neonate** if an alternative exists (it displaces bilirubin from albumin), and never mix it with IV calcium.
- **A cold baby is a hypoglycaemic baby until proven otherwise**, and a hypoglycaemic baby may be septic.
- **Not all skin spots are infection**: erythema toxicum (blotchy red with white/yellow centres, day 2-3, baby well), milia, miliaria and Mongolian spots are benign. Pustules with surrounding redness in a baby with other signs are not.

## Clinical workup

### Step 0: First 5 minutes

1. **ABC**: open airway; oxygen by nasal prongs if SpO2 below 90% or cyanosed/severe distress; bag-mask if apnoeic or gasping.
2. **Temperature**: if below 36.5 C start rewarming (skin-to-skin, incubator/radiant warmer); if above 37.5 C undress/adjust environment (no paracetamol).
3. **Glucose** (glucometer): below 2.6 mmol/L → **10% dextrose 2 mL/kg IV** (or EBM/10% dextrose by NGT if no IV).
4. **Danger sign present?** → IV/IO access, **blood culture**, **ampicillin + gentamicin** now; **phenobarbital 20 mg/kg** if convulsing.
5. **Shock?** (CRT above 3 s, weak pulses, HR above 180) → **normal saline 10 mL/kg** over 10-20 min, reassess.
6. **Vitamin K** if not given.
7. Call for senior help; explain to the mother.

### Step 1: Focused history

- [ ] Age in hours/days and onset of the problem: EOS vs LOS; jaundice timing.
- [ ] Feeding and urine/stool output: dehydration, hypoglycaemia, cholestasis.
- [ ] Labour risk factors (ROM over 18 h, maternal fever, foul liquor).
- [ ] Gestation and birth weight; percentage weight change.
- [ ] Maternal blood group/Rh, previous jaundiced baby, family G6PD.
- [ ] What was applied to the cord or eyes; home remedies.
- [ ] Delivery mode and trauma.
- [ ] Maternal HIV (VL), syphilis, HBsAg; immunisations given.

### Step 2: Focused examination

- General: activity, colour (pink/pale/yellow/blue), cry, posture.
- Vitals, weight, CRT; signs of dehydration (sunken fontanelle, dry mucosa).
- Jaundice extent and palms/soles; liver and spleen.
- Neurology: tone, suck, fontanelle.
- Cord, eyes, skin, mouth.
- Chest (RR, indrawing, grunting) and abdomen (distension).
- Limbs and scalp for birth injury.

### Step 3: Bedside tests

| Test | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Glucose** | Hypoglycaemia (below 2.6) or stress hyperglycaemia (sepsis) |
| **Temperature** | Hypothermia grade or fever |
| **SpO2** | Hypoxaemia (pneumonia, sepsis, CHD) |
| **Transcutaneous bilirubin (TcB)** | Screening in babies of 35 weeks or more after 24 h (NICE); confirm with serum if high or day 1 |
| **Weight** | Weight loss above 10% suggests poor intake/dehydration |
| **HemoCue Hb** | Pallor, haemolysis, bleeding |
| **Urine dipstick (bag/clean catch)** | UTI in prolonged jaundice or LOS |
| **Malaria RDT/film** | Fever in a malaria-endemic area (congenital/neonatal malaria is rare but possible) |

### Step 4: Laboratory & imaging

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management | Availability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Blood culture** | Every suspected sepsis, **before antibiotics** | Organism and sensitivity | Choice and duration; stop at 48 h if negative and well | Regional/national referral, PNFP hospitals |
| **FBC** | Sepsis, jaundice, pallor | WBC below 5 or above 20, immature neutrophils, platelets below 100; Hb low and reticulocytes high in haemolysis | Supports sepsis; transfusion; haemolysis work-up | HC IV and above |
| **CRP** | Sepsis | Rising at 18-24 h; normal values at 0 and 18-24 h help stop antibiotics | Duration | Referral hospitals |
| **Lumbar puncture (CSF)** | Sepsis with seizures, bulging fontanelle, lethargy, or positive culture | WBC above about 20/mm3, low glucose, high protein, organisms | **21 days** of IV antibiotics | Hospital |
| **Total serum bilirubin (TSB)** | Jaundice day 1, palms/soles yellow, TcB high, preterm | Plot on threshold chart by age in hours | Phototherapy/exchange | General/regional referral |
| **Conjugated (direct) bilirubin** | Jaundice beyond 14 days (term)/21 days (preterm), pale stools | **Above 25 umol/L** (NICE) = cholestasis | Urgent referral for biliary atresia | Referral hospitals |
| **Blood group, Rh and DAT (Coombs) of baby and mother** | Early jaundice | ABO (mother O, baby A/B) or Rh incompatibility; DAT positive | Lower thresholds, IVIG/exchange | Referral hospitals |
| **G6PD screen** | Unexplained haemolysis, family history, male | Deficient | Avoid triggers; counsel family | Limited (tertiary/private) |
| **Thyroid function** | Prolonged jaundice | Low T4, high TSH | Levothyroxine | Referral hospitals |
| **Eye swab: Gram stain and culture** | Purulent conjunctivitis | Gram-negative intracellular diplococci = gonococcus | Confirms; contact tracing | Referral hospitals |
| **Pus swab** | Abscess, omphalitis with flare | S. aureus (MRSA?) | Antibiotic choice | Referral hospitals |
| **X-ray** | Suspected fracture (clavicle, humerus), pneumonia | Fracture, consolidation | Immobilisation; pneumonia treatment | Most hospitals |

### Step 5: Putting it together

**Model summary line:** "A **6-day-old** term male (birth weight 3.2 kg, now 2.9 kg, **9% loss**) born at home, brought with **2 days of poor feeding and fever 38.4 C**, with a **red umbilicus with pus and redness spreading 2 cm onto the abdominal wall**, RR 64, glucose 2.3 mmol/L, jaundice to the abdomen, palms not yellow. Cow ghee was applied to the cord. Mother HIV-negative at ANC; no BCG yet."

**Working diagnosis:** late-onset neonatal sepsis from **omphalitis with periumbilical flare** (staphylococcal risk), with hypoglycaemia and dehydration; jaundice probably physiological but worsened by sepsis and poor intake.

**Worked example: plotting bilirubin.** A term (39 weeks) baby at **36 hours** has TSB **240 umol/L**. On the NICE table, the phototherapy threshold at 36 h is **225 umol/L** and exchange **400**. So: **start phototherapy**, repeat TSB in 4-6 h, look for the cause (jaundice this high this early suggests haemolysis), and continue breastfeeding.

## Differential diagnosis

**The baby who is "not feeding well and sleepy":**

| Condition | Distinguishing features | Key investigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Sepsis/meningitis** | Risk factors, temperature instability, bulging fontanelle, mottling | Culture, CRP, LP |
| **Hypoglycaemia** | Jittery, sweaty, apnoea, seizures; SGA/IDM/preterm | Glucose |
| **Hypothermia** | Cold peripheries, temp below 36.5 | Temperature |
| **Severe jaundice/bilirubin encephalopathy** | Deep jaundice, poor suck, then arching, high-pitched cry | TSB |
| **Dehydration / breastfeeding failure** | Weight loss above 10%, few wet nappies, hypernatraemia | Weight, sodium |
| **HIE** | History of asphyxia, onset from birth | Clinical |
| **Congenital heart disease** | Cyanosis, murmur, weak femoral pulses, hepatomegaly | SpO2 pre/post-ductal, echo |
| **Neonatal tetanus** | Day 3-14, could suck then stopped, trismus, spasms, alert between spasms | Clinical |
| **Inborn error of metabolism** | Well at birth then vomiting, encephalopathy after feeds | Ammonia, lactate, ketones |

**Jaundice by timing:**

| Onset | Likely causes |
|---|---|
| **First 24 h** (always pathological) | **Haemolysis** (ABO, Rh, G6PD, spherocytosis), sepsis, congenital infection (TORCH, syphilis) |
| **Day 2-14** | Physiological, breastfeeding (poor intake) jaundice, sepsis, haemolysis, bruising/cephalhaematoma, polycythaemia, prematurity |
| **After 14 days (term)/21 days (preterm)** | Breast milk jaundice (unconjugated, well baby), **UTI/sepsis**, **hypothyroidism**, G6PD; **conjugated**: **biliary atresia**, neonatal hepatitis, choledochal cyst |

| Test | What you are looking for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose | Below 2.6 mmol/L | Treat immediately |
| Blood culture | Bacteraemia | Guides antibiotics |
| FBC | Infection, haemolysis, anaemia | Diagnosis and transfusion |
| CRP | Inflammation trend | Stopping antibiotics |
| CSF | Meningitis | Duration and drug choice |
| TSB, conjugated bilirubin | Severity and type of jaundice | Phototherapy/exchange; cholestasis |
| Blood group, DAT, G6PD, TSH | Cause of jaundice | Specific treatment and counselling |

## Management

### 1. Neonatal sepsis (possible serious bacterial infection)

**Who to treat:**
- Any **danger sign** (PSBI/very severe disease).
- **Risk factors** (ROM over 18 h, maternal fever above 38 C, foul liquor) in a baby under 1 day: **ampicillin + gentamicin for at least 2 days, then reassess** (WHO); stop if well and cultures negative.

**Steps:**
1. Stabilise (ABC, warmth, glucose, fluids/feeds).
2. **Blood culture** (and LP when stable) **before** the first dose if it causes no delay.
3. **Ampicillin + gentamicin** (first line, WHO 2013 and WHO 2024 SBI recommendations; UCG 2023).
4. **Supportive care**: warmth; feeds (EBM by NGT if unable to suck) or IV 10% dextrose; oxygen if SpO2 below 90%; no paracetamol for fever (undress, control environment).
5. **Review at 48-72 h**: improving → complete course; not improving → **change** (ceftriaxone, or per culture; cloxacillin if staphylococcal; meropenem at referral hospitals for resistant Gram-negatives).
6. **Duration**: stop at **48 h** if culture-negative and baby well (NICE NG195 allows stopping at **36 h** with negative culture and reassuring CRP); **7-10 days** for clinical/culture-proven sepsis (UCG 10 days); **21 days** for meningitis (UCG/WHO).

**Antibiotic doses (WHO Pocket Book 2013; UCG 2023):**

| Drug | Dose | Route | Frequency | Duration / notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ampicillin** | **50 mg/kg** (meningitis **100 mg/kg**) | IV/IM | **Every 12 h in week 1**; **every 8 h weeks 2-4** (UCG hospital protocol: 6-hourly IV) | With gentamicin; 7-10 days (21 days meningitis) |
| **Gentamicin** | **Week 1: 5 mg/kg** (normal weight), **3 mg/kg** LBW (WHO) / **4 mg/kg** preterm (UCG); **weeks 2-4: 7.5 mg/kg** | IV/IM | **Once daily** (24-hourly; longer intervals in very preterm or AKI) | Ototoxic/nephrotoxic; check levels where available |
| **Benzylpenicillin** (HC II pre-referral or alternative to ampicillin) | **50,000 IU/kg** | IM/IV | Pre-referral single dose (UCG); 12-hourly week 1, 8-hourly after | Plus gentamicin |
| **Cloxacillin** (staphylococcal risk: omphalitis with flare, many pustules, abscess) | **50 mg/kg** (WHO range 25-50 mg/kg) | IV/IM | **Every 6 h** (UCG) | With **gentamicin 5-7 mg/kg once daily** (UCG) |
| **Ceftriaxone** (second line; meningitis) | **100 mg/kg once daily** (UCG); meningitis **50 mg/kg 12-hourly or 100 mg/kg once daily** (WHO) | IV/IM | Once (or twice) daily | **Avoid in hyperbilirubinaemia/preterm** if possible; **never with IV calcium**; cefotaxime 50 mg/kg 12-6-hourly is safer where available |
| **Metronidazole** (NEC, anaerobes) | 7.5 mg/kg (some protocols 15 mg/kg load) | IV/oral | 12-hourly week 1, 8-hourly after | With ampicillin + gentamicin for NEC |

> [!DRUG]
> **Worked example**: 3.0 kg baby, day 3 → ampicillin **150 mg IV 12-hourly** + gentamicin **15 mg IV once daily**. Same baby on day 10 → ampicillin **150 mg 8-hourly** + gentamicin **22.5 mg once daily** (7.5 mg/kg).

> [!UGANDA]
> **If referral is not possible** (UCG/IMNCI), continue **ampicillin + gentamicin IM** for **7 days** at the health centre, keep warm, and support feeding. WHO's outpatient PSBI regimens (once-daily IM gentamicin plus oral amoxicillin for 7 days) are used for young infants whose families cannot accept referral, but never for babies with critical signs (unconscious, convulsions, not feeding at all, apnoea).

### 2. Neonatal jaundice

**Assess every baby:** look in daylight; **any jaundice in the first 24 h** → measure bilirubin urgently (NICE: within 2 h). Check palms/soles. Babies of 35 weeks or more after 24 h → TcB (or serum if no TcB).

**Kramer's zones** (rough guide only; always confirm high values):

| Zone | Extent | Approx. bilirubin (umol/L) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Head and neck | About 100 |
| 2 | Upper trunk (to umbilicus) | About 150 |
| 3 | Lower trunk and thighs | About 200 |
| 4 | Arms and lower legs | About 250 |
| 5 | **Palms and soles** | **Above 250** |

**WHO Pocket Book thresholds (umol/L):**

| Age | Phototherapy: healthy 35 weeks or more | Phototherapy: below 35 weeks or any risk factor* | Exchange: healthy 35 weeks or more | Exchange: below 35 weeks or risk factor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | **Any visible jaundice** | Any visible jaundice | 260 | 220 |
| Day 2 | 260 | 170 | 425 | 260 |
| Day 3 or more | 310 | 250 | 425 | 340 |

*Risk factors: small (below 2.5 kg or below 37 weeks), haemolysis, sepsis.

**NICE CG98 (updated 2023) threshold table, 38 weeks or more (umol/L):**

| Age (h) | 0 | 6 | 12 | 18 | 24 | 30 | 36 | 42 | 48 | 60 | 72 | 84 | 96+ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Phototherapy** | 100 | 125 | 150 | 175 | 200 | 212 | 225 | 237 | 250 | 275 | 300 | 325 | **350** |
| **Exchange** | 100 | 150 | 200 | 250 | 300 | 350 | 400 | 450 | 450 | 450 | 450 | 450 | **450** |

For babies below 38 weeks use NICE's gestation-specific graphs (lower lines; rule of thumb for phototherapy after 72 h ≈ gestation in weeks × 10 − 100 umol/L). The **AAP 2022** guideline (USA) uses hour-specific curves by gestation (35 weeks or more) with **neurotoxicity risk factors** and an "**escalation of care**" threshold 34 umol/L (2 mg/dL) below the exchange line; many Ugandan units still use the WHO table or NICE charts.

**Phototherapy: how to do it well**
1. Baby **naked** (nappy only), **eyes covered**, under blue light (460-490 nm) at the right distance; turn 2-3-hourly or use double lights for intensive phototherapy.
2. **Continue breastfeeding** (short breaks, or EBM by cup); give **10-20% extra fluid** if under radiant heat or if dehydrated.
3. Monitor temperature, hydration, weight; **repeat TSB 4-6 h** after starting (rising fast or haemolysis), then 6-12-hourly.
4. **Stop** when TSB is **50 umol/L below the phototherapy threshold** (NICE); check for **rebound** 12-18 h later.
5. **Do not use sunlight** as treatment (unreliable, burns, hypothermia/hyperthermia).

**Exchange transfusion** (double volume = **160 mL/kg** in term babies; about 200 mL/kg in preterm) for TSB above the exchange threshold or **signs of acute bilirubin encephalopathy**, at a centre that can do it; keep intensive phototherapy going during transfer.
**IVIG 500 mg/kg** over 4 h (NICE) for Rh/ABO haemolytic disease if TSB keeps rising above 8.5 umol/L per hour despite intensive phototherapy (AAP 2022: 0.5-1 g/kg if at escalation threshold with positive DAT).

**Prolonged jaundice**: check **conjugated bilirubin**, stool and urine colour, TSH, urine culture, G6PD; **pale stools or conjugated above 25 umol/L** → urgent referral (biliary atresia needs a Kasai procedure, ideally **before 60 days**). Breast milk jaundice: well, thriving baby with unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia: **continue breastfeeding**.

### 3. Hypoglycaemia

**At-risk babies** (check glucose before the second feed, 2-4 h after birth, then before feeds): preterm, LBW/SGA, infant of a diabetic mother, asphyxia, sepsis, hypothermia, poor feeding, maternal beta-blockers (labetalol).

| Situation | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Glucose **below 2.6**, **no symptoms**, baby can feed | **Breastfeed or give EBM** (e.g. 10 mL/kg by cup/NGT) now; recheck in **30-60 min** |
| Glucose **below 2.6** with **symptoms** (jittery, lethargic, apnoea, seizure) or not feeding, or persistent despite feeds | **10% dextrose 2 mL/kg IV** over 5 min, then **10% dextrose infusion** (60 mL/kg/day day 1 → GIR about 4-6 mg/kg/min); recheck in **30 min** |
| No IV access | **EBM or 10% dextrose by NGT** (Pocket Book), or sugar solution by cup (UCG HC level); refer |
| Persistent despite **GIR above 8 mg/kg/min** | Think **hyperinsulinism** (IDM, SGA), adrenal insufficiency; refer; specialists may use glucagon or hydrocortisone |

> [!NOTE]
> Thresholds differ: Ugandan neonatal units commonly treat below **2.6 mmol/L** (used throughout these notes), WHO Pocket Book uses **2.2 mmol/L** for sick infants, and NICE/BAPM use **2.0 mmol/L** (with IV treatment for any value below 1.0 or symptomatic). Follow your unit's protocol and say which you used.

### 4. Hypothermia

1. **Prevent**: dry at birth, skin-to-skin, hat, **delay bathing 24 h**, warm room (at least 25 C), KMC for small babies.
2. **Cold stress (36.0-36.4)**: skin-to-skin, extra layers, cover head, warm room, breastfeed; recheck in 1 h.
3. **Moderate (32.0-35.9)**: KMC or incubator/radiant warmer (or a warm cot and room); check **glucose**, feed, look for sepsis; recheck every 30-60 min until normal.
4. **Severe (below 32)**: rapid rewarming in an incubator/radiant warmer, **IV 10% dextrose**, oxygen, **antibiotics**, monitor for apnoea and bradycardia.
5. Remove wet clothes; avoid hot-water bottles directly on skin (burns).

### 5. Cord, eye and skin infections

| Condition | Features | Treatment (UCG 2023 / WHO) |
|---|---|---|
| **Local umbilical infection** | Red umbilicus or pus, redness **not** spreading | Clean with boiled cooled water, dry, **gentian violet 0.5%** 3 times daily; **oral amoxicillin** (UCG: 250 mg DT ¼ tab under 1 month/4 kg, ½ tab 1-2 months/4-6 kg, **12-hourly for 5 days**); review in 2 days |
| **Omphalitis with periumbilical flare** | Redness/swelling spreading onto the abdominal wall, pus, baby may be unwell | Admit; **cloxacillin 50 mg/kg IV 6-hourly + gentamicin**; blood culture; 7-10 days; watch for necrotising fasciitis and portal vein thrombosis |
| **Cord bleeding** | Oozing from the stump | Retie the cord; vitamin K if not given; refer if persists (UCG) |
| **Sticky eye / mild conjunctivitis** | Small amount of discharge, lids not swollen | Wash with clean water/breast milk; **tetracycline or chloramphenicol eye ointment 4 times daily for 5 days** (WHO); review 48 h |
| **Gonococcal ophthalmia** | **Day 2-5**, copious pus, **swollen lids**, risk of corneal perforation | **Ceftriaxone 125 mg IM once** (UCG; WHO 50 mg/kg, max 125 mg) **plus azithromycin 20 mg/kg orally daily for 3 days** (UCG, covers chlamydia); irrigate frequently with saline; admit; treat **both parents** for gonorrhoea and chlamydia and screen for HIV/syphilis |
| **Chlamydial conjunctivitis** | Day 5-14, watery then purulent, milder; may have pneumonia at 4-12 weeks | **Azithromycin 20 mg/kg daily for 3 days** (or oral erythromycin 50 mg/kg/day in 4 doses for 14 days); treat parents |
| **Skin pustules, fewer than 10** | Small pustules, baby well | Clean, **gentian violet 0.5%** 2-3 times daily; review in 2 days |
| **Skin pustules 10 or more, bullae, abscess, cellulitis** | Many/large pustules, swelling, hardness | Treat as **serious infection**: **cloxacillin + gentamicin** IV; drain abscess |
| **Oral thrush** | White plaques that do not wipe off | **Gentian violet** paint 4 times daily for 7 days (UCG) or **nystatin 100,000 IU (1 mL) 4 times daily for 7 days**; treat the mother's nipples |

> [!PEARL]
> Topical tetracycline at birth **prevents** ophthalmia neonatorum but **does not treat** established gonococcal infection; it needs systemic ceftriaxone (UCG: topical tetracycline has no added benefit in active disease).

### 6. Birth injuries

| Injury | Recognition | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Caput succedaneum** | Soft, pitting oedema over the presenting part, **crosses sutures**, present at birth | Reassure; resolves in **1-3 days** |
| **Cephalhaematoma** | Firm swelling **limited by suture lines** (usually parietal), appears hours after birth | Reassure; resolves in **weeks to 3 months**; **do not aspirate**; watch for **jaundice** and anaemia |
| **Subgaleal haemorrhage** | Boggy, fluctuant swelling **crossing sutures**, spreading to neck/forehead, **pallor, tachycardia, shock**, after **vacuum** | **Emergency**: saline 10 mL/kg, **blood**, vitamin K, FFP, monitor head circumference |
| **Erb's palsy (C5-C6)** | Arm adducted, internally rotated, elbow extended, wrist flexed ("**waiter's tip**"); asymmetric Moro; grasp intact | Gentle handling, pin sleeve to the vest in the first week, then **passive physiotherapy**; most recover in weeks to months; refer if no biceps recovery by **3 months** |
| **Klumpke's palsy (C8-T1)** | Claw hand, absent grasp, ± **Horner syndrome** | Physiotherapy; refer |
| **Clavicle fracture** | Crepitus, swelling, reduced arm movement, asymmetric Moro (often after shoulder dystocia) | Reassure; heals in **7-10 days** with callus; pin sleeve, gentle handling, analgesia |
| **Humerus/femur fracture** | Breech or difficult delivery; swelling, pain, no movement | X-ray; splint; orthopaedics |
| **Facial nerve palsy** | Asymmetric cry, eye does not close on the affected side (forceps) | Eye lubricant/protection; usually recovers |
| **Bruising of buttocks/legs after breech** | Bruises, swelling | Reassure (UCG): resolves in 1-2 weeks; watch jaundice; do not force the legs |
| **Sternomastoid tumour** | Firm lump in sternomastoid at 2-3 weeks, torticollis | Stretching physiotherapy |

### 7. Respiratory distress and feeding problems (brief)

- **Respiratory distress** (RR 60 or more, indrawing, grunting): oxygen to SpO2 above 90% (90-95% in preterms), **antibiotics** (pneumonia/sepsis cannot be excluded), consider CPAP, look for **transient tachypnoea** (CS, mild, settles in 24-72 h), **meconium aspiration**, **RDS** (preterm), **pneumothorax**, **congenital heart disease** (cyanosis, murmur, weak femorals) and **hypothermia/hypoglycaemia**.
- **Feeding problems** (IMNCI): poor attachment, fewer than 8 feeds/24 h, other foods/drinks, thrush, low weight → correct positioning and attachment, feed more often day and night, stop other fluids, treat thrush, review in 2 days; refer for breastfeeding counselling if not improving.

## Complications

| Problem | Complications |
|---|---|
| Sepsis | Meningitis (hydrocephalus, deafness, CP, epilepsy), septic shock, DIC, NEC, osteomyelitis/septic arthritis, death |
| Jaundice | **Acute bilirubin encephalopathy → kernicterus** (athetoid CP, sensorineural deafness, upward gaze palsy, dental enamel dysplasia, intellectual impairment) |
| Hypoglycaemia | Seizures, occipital brain injury, visual impairment, developmental delay |
| Hypothermia | Hypoglycaemia, acidosis, apnoea, pulmonary haemorrhage, sclerema, death |
| Omphalitis | Necrotising fasciitis, sepsis, portal vein thrombosis → portal hypertension later |
| Gonococcal ophthalmia | Corneal ulceration, perforation, **blindness** |
| Birth injuries | Shock and death (subgaleal), permanent brachial plexus palsy |

## Ward-round & exam pearls

### Model presentation

"**Baby Namusoke**, a **4-day-old** female, born at term by SVD at a HC IV, birth weight **3.0 kg**, today **2.75 kg (8% loss)**. Presented with **yellow eyes since day 2**, now spreading to the legs, and **poor feeding for 1 day**. Exclusively breastfed, **6 feeds in 24 h**, 3 wet nappies, yellow stools. Mother is **blood group O positive**, baby **B positive**, DAT not done. No fever, no family history of G6PD deficiency known.

On examination she is jaundiced to the **legs (Kramer 4)**, palms and soles not yellow, alert with a good cry, normal tone and suck, temperature 36.9 C, RR 46, no hepatosplenomegaly, umbilicus clean. TSB at **84 hours is 290 umol/L**, just **below** the NICE phototherapy line at 84 h (**325 umol/L**).

**Assessment:** neonatal jaundice, probably physiological/breastfeeding-related with possible **ABO incompatibility**; she is near the phototherapy threshold, and feeding is inadequate. **Plan:** support breastfeeding (8-12 feeds/day, EBM top-ups by cup), repeat TSB in 6-12 h, start phototherapy if above threshold, FBC and reticulocytes, DAT, G6PD if available, review danger signs.

Mother **HIV-negative** in labour. **BCG and OPV0 given** at birth. Weight-for-age Z-score about −1."

### Viva questions (with answers)

1. **Name the WHO/UCG neonatal danger signs.** Not feeding well, convulsions, drowsy/unconscious or moves only when stimulated, RR 60 or more, severe chest indrawing, grunting, temperature above 37.5 or below 35.5 C, central cyanosis.
2. **First-line antibiotics for neonatal sepsis and doses?** Ampicillin 50 mg/kg 12-hourly (week 1)/8-hourly (weeks 2-4) + gentamicin 5 mg/kg (3-4 mg/kg LBW/preterm) once daily week 1, 7.5 mg/kg weeks 2-4.
3. **Why is jaundice on day 1 pathological?** Physiological processes cannot raise bilirubin that fast; it implies haemolysis or infection.
4. **How do you stop phototherapy?** When TSB is 50 umol/L below the threshold; check rebound in 12-18 h.
5. **What are the features of kernicterus?** Choreoathetoid CP, sensorineural deafness, upward gaze palsy, dental enamel dysplasia.
6. **How do you treat gonococcal ophthalmia?** Ceftriaxone 125 mg IM once + azithromycin 20 mg/kg daily × 3 days, saline washes, treat both parents.
7. **Caput vs cephalhaematoma?** Caput crosses sutures, present at birth, resolves in days; cephalhaematoma is limited by sutures, appears later, lasts weeks.

### OSCE tips

- **Counsel a mother on danger signs and cord care** (chlorhexidine 7 days, nothing else on the stump, keep dry, fold nappy below).
- **Explain phototherapy** to a mother: why, eye pads, keep breastfeeding, temperature checks, how long.
- **Prescribe** antibiotics for a given weight and age; show mg/kg and the total dose.

### Classic exam traps

- **"Jaundice at 12 hours: it's physiological, review tomorrow."** → Wrong. Day-1 jaundice is **always pathological**; measure bilirubin now.
- **"Put the jaundiced baby in the morning sun."** → Wrong. Sunlight is unreliable and unsafe; use **phototherapy**.
- **"Stop breastfeeding for jaundice."** → Wrong. **Continue breastfeeding**; poor intake worsens jaundice.
- **"Febrile neonate: give paracetamol and observe."** → Wrong. Treat as **sepsis**; control the environment; no antipyretics (UCG).
- **"Gentamicin 7.5 mg/kg on day 2 of life."** → Too high: **5 mg/kg** in week 1 (3-4 mg/kg if LBW/preterm); 7.5 mg/kg is for **weeks 2-4**.
- **"Ceftriaxone for a deeply jaundiced preterm with sepsis."** → Avoid if possible (displaces bilirubin); use ampicillin + gentamicin or cefotaxime.
- **"Gonococcal ophthalmia: tetracycline ointment is enough."** → Wrong. Needs **systemic ceftriaxone** (and treat parents).
- **"Aspirate the cephalhaematoma."** → Wrong. Leave it; it resolves (aspiration risks infection).

### Questions seniors ask

1. **"What is the baby's age in hours, and where is the bilirubin on the chart?"** Give the TSB, the threshold for that age/gestation, and the decision.
2. **"Why is this baby on antibiotics, and when will you stop?"** Name the risk factor/sign, the culture result plan and the duration.
3. **"What dose of gentamicin, and why that dose?"** mg/kg by age and weight band; check renal function.
4. **"Is this jaundice conjugated or unconjugated? What colour are the stools?"** Pale stools = urgent biliary atresia work-up.
5. **"What is the glucose, and how often are you checking it?"** Value, threshold, frequency, treatment given.
6. **"Is this omphalitis local or spreading?"** Flare onto the abdominal wall changes oral to IV treatment.
7. **"What will you tell the mother before discharge?"** Danger signs, cord care, exclusive breastfeeding, warmth, return for jaundice of palms/soles, immunisation at 6 weeks.
