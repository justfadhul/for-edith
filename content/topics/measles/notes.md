## In a nutshell

**Measles (rubeola)** is an acute viral illness caused by the measles virus (a *Morbillivirus*). It is one of the most contagious infections known: one case can infect **12–18 susceptible people**. It begins with fever and the "three Cs" (**c**ough, **c**oryza, **c**onjunctivitis), then **Koplik spots**, then a **maculopapular rash** spreading from the head down.

Measles itself is self-limiting. Children die from what follows, especially **pneumonia, diarrhoea and dehydration, malnutrition, croup, encephalitis and corneal ulceration**. The virus also **suppresses immunity for weeks to months**. The poorest children (malnourished, vitamin A deficient, HIV-positive, unvaccinated) do worst. Uganda is in the middle of repeated outbreaks because too many children miss the **second dose (MR2) at 18 months**.

> [!PEARL]
> The one thing never to forget: **every child with measles gets vitamin A, by age, today**, plus a hunt for complications (chest, ears, eyes, mouth, hydration, nutrition) and isolation. And every contact who is not vaccinated gets the vaccine.

## Definitions & classification

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Suspected measles (WHO/IDSR case definition)** | Any person with **fever** and a **generalised maculopapular (non-vesicular) rash** and **cough, coryza or conjunctivitis**, or any person a clinician suspects has measles |
| **Laboratory-confirmed** | Measles **IgM** positive (serum, ideally 3–28 days after rash onset) or measles RNA detected by PCR (throat/nasopharyngeal swab, urine) |
| **Epidemiologically linked** | Suspected case linked to a laboratory-confirmed case during an outbreak |
| **Non-severe measles** | No danger signs, no complications needing admission |
| **Measles with eye or mouth complications (IMCI)** | Pus draining from the eye, or mouth ulcers (not deep or extensive) |
| **Severe complicated measles (IMCI)** | **Any general danger sign**, **clouding of the cornea**, or **deep or extensive mouth ulcers** (WHO Pocket Book adds pneumonia, dehydration, stridor, severe malnutrition) |
| **Measles outbreak (Uganda IDSR)** | Usually 3 or more confirmed cases in a district within a month (check your current district guidance); a single confirmed case triggers investigation |

### The stages

| Stage | Timing | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Incubation** | About 10–12 days from exposure to first symptoms (range 7–21 days to rash) | Asymptomatic; virus multiplies in the respiratory tract and lymph nodes |
| **Prodrome (catarrhal stage)** | 2–4 days (up to 7) | High fever (often 39–40.5 °C), **cough** (harsh, "barking"), **coryza**, **conjunctivitis** (red, watery eyes, photophobia), irritability, misery, poor appetite, diarrhoea. **Koplik spots** (white/grey "grains of salt" on a red base on the buccal mucosa opposite the lower molars) appear **1–2 days before the rash** and fade 1–2 days after it |
| **Exanthem (rash stage)** | Days 3–7 of illness | **Erythematous maculopapular rash** starting at the **hairline, face and behind the ears**, spreading **down the trunk and limbs over 3 days**, reaching hands and feet. Spots become **confluent** (blotchy). Fever peaks 2–3 days after the rash starts, then falls |
| **Recovery** | From about day 4 of the rash | The rash fades in the order it appeared, leaving **brownish staining** and fine **desquamation** (spares palms and soles); in malnourished children, dark peeling "flaky paint" skin. Cough may last 1–2 weeks. Immune suppression persists for weeks to months |

> [!EXAM]
> **"Fever should fall 2–3 days after the rash appears."** If the fever **persists beyond day 3–4 of the rash** or returns, look for a **complication**: pneumonia, otitis media, diarrhoea, encephalitis, or TB reactivation.

## Epidemiology & Uganda context

- **Global (WHO fact sheet):** measles killed an estimated **95,000 people in 2024**, mostly unvaccinated or under-vaccinated children under 5. Vaccination averted about **59 million deaths between 2000 and 2024**. First-dose coverage was **84% in 2025**, below the 95% needed to stop transmission.
- **Uganda (MoH, 2025–2026):**
  - National measles vaccination coverage rose from 79% (2016) to 90% (2024), still below the **95% target**.
  - In 2025, **MR1 coverage was 92% but MR2 coverage only 64%**. Many caregivers don't know that Uganda gives a second dose at 18 months.
  - In 2025, **66 districts** confirmed outbreaks, with **over 10,163 cases and 60 deaths**; about a third of deaths were in **Karamoja**, where malnutrition worsens outcomes.
  - Among cases, **75% were under 5**, **64% had never received MR**, and 33% had only one dose.
  - Between January and April 2026, 20 more districts confirmed outbreaks. The MoH ran a **nationwide MR campaign on 1–5 October 2026** for **all children aged 9–59 months, regardless of previous vaccination**, integrated with the October Integrated Child Health Days.
- **Who dies?** Children under 5 (especially under 1), the **malnourished** (SAM), **vitamin A deficient**, **HIV-positive** and otherwise immunocompromised children, children in crowded camps (refugee settlements), and those who present late.

> [!UGANDA]
> **Uganda's schedule (UNEPI):** **MR1 at 9 months** and **MR2 at 18 months** (measles-rubella combined vaccine, introduced in the 2019 campaign; MR2 added later). During outbreaks, the MoH vaccinates children from **6 months** (a "zero dose" that does not count towards the routine schedule) and runs campaigns for **9–59 months**. **Measles samples** (serum for IgM) go via the hub system to the **Uganda Virus Research Institute (UVRI), Entebbe**, the national measles laboratory.

## Pathophysiology

1. **Entry and spread**: the virus enters through respiratory epithelium and conjunctiva, infecting **alveolar macrophages and dendritic cells** via the **SLAM (CD150) receptor**, which is found on immune cells. It is carried to **regional lymph nodes** (days 2–4), then a **primary viraemia** spreads it to the reticuloendothelial system.
2. **Secondary viraemia** (around day 7–10) seeds the **skin, respiratory tract, gut, conjunctiva, liver and brain**. The virus then exits through epithelial cells via the **nectin-4** receptor on their basal side, which is why it is shed so efficiently from the airway.
3. **The rash** is largely an **immune reaction** (T cells attacking infected endothelial cells in the skin), so it appears as antibodies and cell-mediated immunity develop. That is why **severely immunocompromised children may have little or no rash** but devastating pneumonia or encephalitis.
4. **Immune suppression**: the virus infects and kills **memory T and B cells** ("**immune amnesia**"), erasing antibody memory against other infections for months to years. This explains the **secondary bacterial pneumonia, diarrhoea, TB reactivation and a negative tuberculin test** after measles.
5. **Vitamin A**: measles **consumes vitamin A** (increased use and urinary loss, reduced intake). Low vitamin A damages epithelial surfaces (cornea, gut, airway) and immunity. Supplementation **restores levels and reduces death and blindness**, which is why it is given even to well-nourished children.
6. **Gut and nutrition**: mucosal damage → diarrhoea, protein-losing enteropathy; with fever, anorexia, mouth ulcers and harmful food taboos (withholding meat, fish or milk) → **acute malnutrition and kwashiorkor**.
7. **Brain**: acute encephalitis (direct infection), **ADEM** (autoimmune demyelination), **MIBE** (direct infection in immunosuppressed) and **SSPE** (a defective virus persisting in neurons for years).

## Clinical acumen

### Focused history

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Fever, cough, runny nose, red eyes: when did each start? When did the rash start and where? | Confirms the classic sequence; the day of rash tells you infectiousness and when fever should settle |
| Contact with a rash illness, school/church/market, refugee settlement, outbreak district | Epidemiological link; outbreak response |
| **Immunisation card**: MR1 (9 months), **MR2 (18 months)**, campaign doses | Unvaccinated or 1 dose = susceptible; vaccine failure is possible but rare after 2 doses |
| **Vitamin A** in the last month (routine 6-monthly supplementation, campaign) | Avoid duplicating too-recent doses (UCG: give if not given in last month for measles treatment) |
| **Feeding**: breastfeeding, appetite, food taboos, weight loss | Malnutrition risk; counter harmful myths |
| Diarrhoea, vomiting, ability to drink | Dehydration |
| Fast or difficult breathing, noisy breathing (stridor) | Pneumonia, croup |
| Ear pain or discharge | Otitis media |
| Eye pain, photophobia, discharge, "something white on the eye" | Keratitis, ulceration, xerophthalmia |
| Mouth pain, refusing feeds | Stomatitis, candidiasis, cancrum oris |
| Convulsions, drowsiness, behaviour change, weakness | Febrile seizure, encephalitis |
| **HIV status** (child and mother), TB contact, chronic cough | Severe/prolonged measles, giant-cell pneumonia; TB flare after measles |
| Traditional eye or skin remedies | Chemical eye injury, skin infection |

### Examination

1. **ETAT triage**: airway (stridor), breathing (rate, indrawing, SpO₂), circulation, consciousness, convulsions, dehydration.
2. **Weight, length/height, MUAC, oedema of both feet** → weight-for-height Z-score; **SAM** changes management.
3. **Rash**: distribution, confluence, desquamation, any vesicles (think chickenpox), purpura (think meningococcus), skin infection.
4. **Mouth**: Koplik spots (early), ulcers (how deep and extensive?), thrush, **cancrum oris** (gangrene of the cheek).
5. **Eyes**: conjunctivitis, discharge, **corneal clouding**, ulceration, **Bitot's spots** and dryness (xerophthalmia) → vitamin A deficiency.
6. **Ears**: discharge, tympanic membrane.
7. **Chest**: RR for age, indrawing, crackles, wheeze, **stridor at rest** (severe croup).
8. **Hydration**: sunken eyes, skin pinch, ability to drink.
9. **Neurology**: consciousness, neck stiffness, focal signs.
10. **Lymph nodes, liver, spleen, oral thrush** (HIV clues).

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **General danger signs**: unable to drink/breastfeed, vomiting everything, convulsions, lethargy/unconsciousness
> - **Clouding of the cornea** or corneal ulcer (risk of blindness within days)
> - **Deep or extensive mouth ulcers**; gangrene of the cheek (**cancrum oris**)
> - Chest indrawing, SpO₂ <90%, **stridor at rest**
> - Severe dehydration; bloody diarrhoea
> - **Severe acute malnutrition** (MUAC <11.5 cm, WHZ <−3, oedema of both feet)
> - Infant under 6 months, HIV-positive or immunocompromised child
> - Altered consciousness, seizures, new focal signs (encephalitis)

### Thinking like a clinician

- **"Rash in a febrile child: is it measles?"** Check the **sequence** (prodrome of 3 Cs for 2–4 days → rash from the head downward) and the **Koplik spots**. Measles children look **miserable**; rubella and roseola children usually look well.
- **Fever falls as the rash appears** → think **roseola** (HHV-6, under 2 years). **Fever persists after the rash** → measles complication.
- **Malnourished child with measles** = high risk: give vitamin A, admit, start nutritional rehabilitation, look hard for pneumonia, diarrhoea and TB.
- **Child in a measles outbreak with cough and fast breathing** = pneumonia (often bacterial): give antibiotics, oxygen if hypoxic.
- **Convulsion during measles**: febrile seizure is possible, but drowsiness or focal signs = **encephalitis**; check glucose and do an LP if safe.
- **Months after measles**: weight loss, cough, fever → **TB** (measles activates TB and makes the tuberculin test falsely negative for weeks).
- **Teen with school decline and myoclonic jerks** → **SSPE**: ask about measles in infancy.
- **Common student mistakes**: forgetting vitamin A or giving the wrong dose for age; prescribing steroid eye drops; admitting a measles case to the general ward without isolation; not checking the vaccination status of the siblings, ward contacts and staff; not notifying; underestimating mouth ulcers that stop a child eating.

> [!EXAM]
> Viva favourite: **"Why do we give vitamin A to a well-nourished child with measles?"** Answer: measles itself causes an acute fall in serum vitamin A (increased use and loss, reduced intake). Two doses restore levels, **reduce mortality (especially in children under 2) and pneumonia**, and **prevent corneal damage and blindness**. WHO and UCG recommend it for **every** case, regardless of nutritional status.

## Clinical workup

### Step 0: First 5 minutes

1. **Mask and separate**: move the child and caregiver to an **isolation area/room** (airborne precautions; only staff with documented immunity attend; N95 if available).
2. **ETAT**: airway (stridor), breathing (SpO₂, indrawing), circulation, coma, convulsions, dehydration.
3. **Oxygen** if SpO₂ <90%, central cyanosis or severe respiratory distress.
4. **Check glucose** if lethargic, convulsing or not feeding.
5. **Weigh, MUAC, oedema**: identify SAM immediately.
6. **Give vitamin A (first dose) now**, and the first dose of antibiotic if there are danger signs, pneumonia, corneal clouding or deep mouth ulcers.

### Step 1: Focused history

- [ ] **Sequence and dates** (fever, 3 Cs, rash onset) → confirms measles; sets isolation end (4 days after rash onset).
- [ ] **Contacts/outbreak** → notification and outbreak response.
- [ ] **MR1 and MR2 status** (card), recent campaigns → susceptibility; catch-up for siblings.
- [ ] **Vitamin A received recently** → avoid exceeding doses.
- [ ] **Feeding, diarrhoea, vomiting** → dehydration, malnutrition.
- [ ] **Breathing, stridor, ear, eye and mouth symptoms** → complications.
- [ ] **Convulsions, consciousness** → encephalitis.
- [ ] **HIV (child and mother), TB contact** → severity, TB flare.
- [ ] **Traditional remedies** (eye drops, herbs) → chemical eye injury.

### Step 2: Focused examination

- **General**: temperature, weight, MUAC, WHZ, oedema, hydration, pallor.
- **Skin**: rash stage and distribution, desquamation, secondary infection.
- **Eyes**: conjunctiva, cornea (clouding, ulcer), Bitot's spots, photophobia.
- **Mouth**: Koplik spots, ulcers (depth/extent), thrush, cheek gangrene.
- **ENT**: ear discharge, tonsils.
- **Chest**: RR, indrawing, crackles, wheeze, stridor; SpO₂.
- **Neurology**: AVPU, neck, fontanelle, focal signs.
- **Document negatives**: "cornea clear, mouth ulcers superficial, no stridor, no indrawing, MUAC 13.2 cm, no oedema".

### Step 3: Bedside tests

| Test | How | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **SpO₂** | Pulse oximeter | <90% → oxygen; pneumonia severity |
| **Blood glucose** | Glucometer | Hypoglycaemia in a child not feeding or convulsing |
| **MUAC and weight-for-height** | Tape, scale, WHO charts | SAM (MUAC <11.5 cm, WHZ <−3, oedema) |
| **Malaria RDT** | Finger prick | Co-infection (fever in Uganda) |
| **Fluorescein staining** (if available) | Drop, blue light | Corneal ulcer |
| **HemoCue Hb** | Finger prick | Anaemia |

### Step 4: Laboratory & imaging

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Measles IgM (serum)** | First suspected cases in a new area; per surveillance protocol (in a confirmed outbreak, sample only a few cases) | IgM positive (best 3–28 days after rash; may be negative in the first 3 days) | Confirms outbreak, triggers response. Sent through the district surveillance officer to **UVRI** |
| **Throat/nasopharyngeal swab or urine for PCR** | Early (within 5 days of rash) with serum | Measles RNA; genotyping | Outbreak tracking. UVRI |
| **Full blood count** | Admitted children | Lymphopenia, low platelets (rare), high WBC with bacterial infection | Antibiotics; transfusion |
| **Blood culture** | Severe pneumonia, sepsis | *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*, Gram-negatives | Antibiotic choice. RRH/NRH |
| **Chest X-ray** | Severe or non-resolving pneumonia, suspected TB | Bronchopneumonia, lobar consolidation, interstitial (giant cell) pneumonia, hilar nodes (TB) | Antibiotics, TB workup. Hospitals |
| **Electrolytes, urea** | Severe dehydration, SAM | Hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia | Fluids (ReSoMal in SAM) |
| **HIV test** | All admitted with unknown status | Positive | ART, cotrimoxazole |
| **LP** | Encephalitis signs | Lymphocytes, raised protein, normal glucose | Excludes bacterial meningitis |
| **TB workup** (Xpert on sputum/gastric aspirate/stool) | Persistent cough, weight loss after measles | MTB detected | TB treatment |
| **EEG, CSF and serum measles antibody** | Suspected SSPE | Periodic high-voltage complexes; very high CSF measles IgG | Diagnosis of SSPE. Mulago/specialist |

### Step 5: Putting it together

**Model one-line summary**
> "A 2-year-old girl, 9.5 kg (weight-for-height Z −2.4, MUAC 12.1 cm, no oedema), who received MR1 but not MR2, presenting on day 3 of a generalised maculopapular rash after 4 days of fever, cough, coryza and conjunctivitis, with fast breathing (RR 52), lower chest indrawing and SpO₂ 89%: measles complicated by severe pneumonia and moderate acute malnutrition."

**Problem list:** (1) measles, day 3 of rash (infectious until day 4 after rash onset); (2) severe pneumonia with hypoxaemia; (3) moderate acute malnutrition; (4) incomplete immunisation (MR2); (5) household contacts unvaccinated.

> [!EXAM]
> **Worked example 1: vitamin A by age.**
> - 5-month-old: **50,000 IU** day 1 and day 2 (UCG: and day 14). From a 200,000 IU capsule: **¼ capsule** (about 2 drops of a capsule with 8 drops; check the capsule labelling).
> - 10-month-old: **100,000 IU** (½ capsule of 200,000, or one 100,000 IU blue capsule).
> - 3-year-old: **200,000 IU** (one red capsule).
> The UCG 2023 schedule is **day 1, day 2 and day 14**; WHO gives the third dose (2–4 weeks later) only if there are **eye signs** of vitamin A deficiency.

> [!EXAM]
> **Worked example 2: is this rash measles?**
> A 14-month-old has 3 days of high fever and is still feeding; on day 4 the fever disappears and a pink rash appears on the trunk; the child is happy. **Roseola** (HHV-6): the rash follows defervescence, starts on the trunk, and the child looks well.
> A 4-year-old has 4 days of fever, cough, red eyes and white spots on the buccal mucosa, then a rash from behind the ears spreading to the trunk, and the fever is higher on the second day of the rash: **measles**.

## Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing features | Key investigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Rubella** | Milder, low-grade fever, **post-auricular and suboccipital lymphadenopathy**, pink rash that clears in 3 days; arthralgia in older girls | Rubella IgM (sent with measles samples) |
| **Roseola infantum (HHV-6)** | Under 2 years; 3–5 days of high fever, **rash appears as the fever falls**, child well; febrile seizures | Clinical |
| **Scarlet fever** | Sore throat, **sandpaper rash**, strawberry tongue, circumoral pallor, peeling of fingers | Throat swab/ASO |
| **Chickenpox** | **Vesicles** in crops at different stages, itchy | Clinical |
| **Drug eruption / SJS** | New drug (cotrimoxazole, nevirapine, antiepileptics, sulfa), target lesions, mucosal erosions, skin peeling | History; stop the drug |
| **Kawasaki disease** | Fever ≥5 days, non-purulent conjunctivitis, red cracked lips, hand/foot changes, cervical node; no cough or coryza | Clinical, echocardiogram |
| **Meningococcal septicaemia** | **Non-blanching purpura**, shock | Glass test, blood culture |
| **Dengue / other arboviruses** | Travel/outbreak area, rash, myalgia, low platelets | Dengue NS1/IgM |
| **Infectious mononucleosis + amoxicillin** | Older child, tonsillitis, rash after amoxicillin | Clinical |
| **Enterovirus (hand, foot and mouth)** | Vesicles on hands, feet, mouth | Clinical |
| **Parvovirus B19** | "Slapped cheek", lacy rash on limbs | Clinical |

## Investigations

Measles is a **clinical diagnosis** in most cases (UCG 2023). Laboratory confirmation is for **surveillance**: the first suspected cases in a district, or a sample of cases during an outbreak, using **serum IgM** (with throat swab or urine PCR) sent to UVRI. Investigations on the ward target **complications** (SpO₂, glucose, Hb, chest X-ray, cultures, HIV test, TB workup). The full table is in **Clinical workup, Step 4**.

## Management

There is **no specific antiviral** for measles (ribavirin is experimental and not used in Uganda). Management is: **isolate, vitamin A, nutrition and fluids, eye and mouth care, and active treatment of complications.**

### A. Every child with measles

1. **Isolate** at home or in an isolation room (airborne precautions) until **4 days after the rash appears** (longer if immunocompromised).
2. **Vitamin A** (table below).
3. **Paracetamol 10–15 mg/kg** every 6 hours for fever or pain (no aspirin).
4. **Fluids and feeding**: continue breastfeeding; frequent small meals; extra fluids; ORS for diarrhoea (with zinc). **Counter food myths**: children with measles **should** eat meat, fish, eggs and milk (UCG 2023).
5. **Eye care**: clean the eyes with clean water/saline 2–3 times a day; **tetracycline 1% eye ointment** if there is discharge (UCG: every 12 h for 5 days; WHO Pocket Book: 3 times a day for 7 days). **Never use steroid eye preparations.**
6. **Mouth care**: clean with salted water 4 times a day; **gentian violet 0.25%** to ulcers (UCG/WHO); nystatin for thrush.
7. **Check immunisation** of the child (MR2 after recovery if due) and **all contacts**.
8. **Notify** the district surveillance focal person (measles is notifiable).
9. **Follow up in 2–3 days** (outpatients), or sooner if danger signs appear.

**Vitamin A dosing**

| Age | Dose | Schedule (UCG 2023) | Schedule (WHO) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **<6 months** | **50,000 IU** orally | Day 1 (at diagnosis), day 2, day 14 | Day 1 and day 2; third dose 2–4 weeks later if eye signs |
| **6–11 months** | **100,000 IU** orally | Day 1, day 2, day 14 | As above |
| **≥12 months** | **200,000 IU** orally | Day 1, day 2, day 14 | As above |

> [!UGANDA]
> **Three doses or two?** UCG 2023 gives **three doses to every measles case (day 1, day 2 and day 14)**. The WHO Pocket Book gives **two doses 24 hours apart** and a **third dose 2–4 weeks later only if there are eye signs** of vitamin A deficiency (and before discharge for admitted children). Follow UCG on Ugandan wards, and say why if asked. Cut the capsule and squeeze the drops into the mouth; don't give the capsule whole to a young child. If the child had a routine vitamin A dose in the past month, discuss with your senior (UCG: give if not given in the last month).

### B. Severe complicated measles (IMCI / UCG 2023)

Any **general danger sign**, **clouding of the cornea**, or **deep or extensive mouth ulcers**:

1. Give **vitamin A** (first dose).
2. Give the **first dose of an antibiotic for severe bacterial infection**: UCG: **ampicillin 50 mg/kg IM + gentamicin 7.5 mg/kg IM** (or benzylpenicillin 50,000 IU/kg IM at HC II).
3. **Tetracycline eye ointment** if clouding of the cornea or pus draining from the eye; cover the eye with a pad.
4. **Refer urgently** to hospital; keep warm, breastfeed or give fluids on the way; check glucose.

### C. Managing complications in hospital

| Complication | Management (doses per kg) |
|---|---|
| **Pneumonia** (over half bacterial) | **Fast breathing or chest indrawing, no danger sign** (WHO 2024 pneumonia and diarrhoea guideline): **oral amoxicillin at least 40 mg/kg per dose 12-hourly** for **5 days** (3 days for fast breathing only in low-HIV areas); in measles, have a low threshold to admit. **Severe pneumonia** (any danger sign, stridor or **SpO₂ <90%**): **ampicillin 50 mg/kg IV/IM 6-hourly + gentamicin 7.5 mg/kg IV/IM once daily**, or **ceftriaxone 80–100 mg/kg/day**; add **cloxacillin 50 mg/kg 6-hourly** if staphylococcal (pneumatoceles, empyema, skin sepsis). **Oxygen** to keep SpO₂ ≥90% |
| **Croup (laryngotracheobronchitis)** | Supportive care, keep the child calm with the mother, oxygen if hypoxic, avoid unnecessary throat examination. **WHO Pocket Book: do not give steroids for measles croup** (unlike ordinary viral croup, where dexamethasone 0.15–0.6 mg/kg is used). **Nebulised adrenaline** (0.5 mL/kg of 1:1,000, max 5 mL) for stridor at rest as a temporary measure; intubation or tracheostomy for impending obstruction (refer) |
| **Otitis media** | **Amoxicillin 40 mg/kg 12-hourly** for 5 days; dry wicking if discharge |
| **Diarrhoea / dehydration** | ORS (Plan A/B/C as per dehydration), **zinc** (UCG 2023: 10 mg/day under 6 months, 20 mg/day from 6 months, for 10 days; WHO 2024 suggests 5 mg/day); bloody diarrhoea → ciprofloxacin 15 mg/kg 12-hourly 3 days; persistent diarrhoea → nutrition |
| **Mouth ulcers / stomatitis** | Salt-water cleaning, **gentian violet 0.25%**; **severe or smelly ulcers**: **benzylpenicillin 50,000 IU/kg 6-hourly + metronidazole 7.5 mg/kg 8-hourly for 5 days** (WHO PB); NGT feeding if the child cannot eat |
| **Cancrum oris (noma)** | Penicillin + metronidazole, NGT feeding, nutritional rehabilitation, wound care; plastic surgery later (high mortality if untreated) |
| **Eye complications** | Vitamin A, tetracycline eye ointment, eye pad for ulcers, **ophthalmology referral for corneal ulcer** (atropine 1% drops for pain if ulcerated, per ophthalmologist); never steroids |
| **Severe acute malnutrition** | WHO 2023 / UCG SAM protocol: F-75 then F-100 or RUTF, antibiotics, no fluid boluses unless shock (ReSoMal for dehydration), vitamin A, measles vaccine if not already vaccinated |
| **Febrile convulsion** | Diazepam 0.5 mg/kg PR (max 10 mg); paracetamol; check glucose |
| **Acute encephalitis** | Supportive: airway, seizure control (diazepam then phenobarbital 15–20 mg/kg), fluids, glucose; LP to exclude bacterial meningitis; ceftriaxone until excluded |
| **TB flare** | Screen any child with persistent cough or weight loss after measles; treat TB per national guidelines |

### D. Infection control and outbreak response

| Action | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Isolation** | Single room or measles ward/bay with doors closed; caregivers stay with the child; isolation for **4 days after rash onset** (whole illness if immunocompromised) |
| **Staff** | Only staff with **2 documented doses of MCV or prior measles**; N95 masks if available; hand hygiene |
| **Ward contacts** | **Vaccinate all other children aged 6 months or more** in the ward/OPD who are not fully vaccinated (WHO PB); a dose given at 6–8 months does not count, so they still need MR1 at 9 months and MR2 at 18 months |
| **Post-exposure prophylaxis** | **MR vaccine within 72 hours** of exposure for susceptible contacts aged 6 months or more; **normal human immunoglobulin within 6 days** for high-risk contacts who cannot be vaccinated (infants under 6 months, pregnant women, immunocompromised), where available (rarely in Uganda) |
| **Notification** | Report suspected cases to the district within **24 hours** (IDSR); collect samples for the first cases |
| **Community** | Line-list cases, ring or district-wide vaccination (Uganda: 9–59 months, or 6 months to 14 years depending on age pattern), vitamin A, health education |

### E. Prevention: measles vaccination

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Vaccine** | Live attenuated **measles-rubella (MR)** vaccine (MMR in some private clinics) |
| **Uganda schedule** | **MR1 at 9 months, MR2 at 18 months**; catch-up for any child missing doses; campaign doses (e.g. October 2026 for 9–59 months) are given regardless of previous status |
| **Dose and route** | **0.5 mL subcutaneous** (upper arm) |
| **Efficacy** | About **85% after one dose at 9 months** (maternal antibody interferes), **about 95% or more after two doses** |
| **Cold chain** | Freeze-dried, reconstitute with the diluent supplied; use within **6 hours** of reconstitution; discard at the end of the session; protect from light |
| **Side effects** | Fever and mild rash 7–12 days after vaccination; febrile seizures rarely; ITP very rarely |
| **Contraindications** | **Severe immunosuppression** (e.g. advanced HIV with very low CD4, chemotherapy, high-dose steroids), **pregnancy**, previous anaphylaxis to a vaccine component |
| **NOT contraindications** | **Malnutrition** (vaccinate on admission with SAM if unvaccinated and 6 months or more, again at discharge per protocol), mild illness, HIV infection without severe immunosuppression (WHO: HIV-positive children may get an extra dose at 6 months), recent exposure to measles |
| **Vaccine programme failures** | Missed MR2 (64% coverage in 2025), cold-chain breaks, hard-to-reach communities, vaccine hesitancy and myths |

> [!UGANDA]
> **Use every contact.** UCG 2023 IMCI says: check the immunisation card at **every** sick-child visit and **give all missed doses on that visit** (unless the child is being referred). A child admitted with pneumonia or malaria who missed MR1 or MR2 should be vaccinated before discharge. The October Integrated Child Health Days also give vitamin A and deworming.

### F. Setting-specific plan

| Setting | Action |
|---|---|
| **VHT / HC II** | Recognise, vitamin A, paracetamol, ORS, TEO, isolate at home, refer severe complicated measles with first-dose antibiotic; report |
| **HC III** | As above plus admission for moderate cases (e.g. dehydration, non-severe pneumonia) if isolation is possible; MR vaccination of contacts |
| **HC IV / general hospital** | Isolation bay, oxygen, IV antibiotics, ORS/IV fluids, SAM management, sample collection |
| **RRH / NRH** | Severe pneumonia (CPAP/oxygen), encephalitis, corneal ulcer (ophthalmology), noma surgery, ICU |

## Complications

| System | Complication | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Respiratory** | **Pneumonia** (viral giant-cell, secondary bacterial: *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*, Hib, Gram-negatives), **croup/laryngotracheobronchitis**, bronchiolitis obliterans, bronchiectasis later | **Commonest cause of death** |
| **ENT** | **Otitis media** (commonest complication), mastoiditis, sinusitis | |
| **GI** | **Diarrhoea and dehydration**, stomatitis, mouth ulcers, **cancrum oris (noma)**, appendicitis, hepatitis | |
| **Nutritional** | **Acute malnutrition, kwashiorkor**, growth faltering, vitamin A deficiency | Measles precipitates SAM |
| **Eye** | Conjunctivitis, keratitis, **corneal ulceration**, **xerophthalmia/keratomalacia**, **blindness**; harmful traditional remedies | Leading cause of childhood blindness in Africa |
| **Neurological** | **Febrile seizures**; **acute encephalitis** (about 1 in 1,000; seizures, coma, permanent brain damage); **ADEM** (post-infectious, days–weeks after rash); **measles inclusion body encephalitis** (immunocompromised, 1–6 months later); **SSPE** (years later) | |
| **Immune** | **Immune amnesia**, TB reactivation, false-negative tuberculin test | Lasts weeks to months (or longer) |
| **Haematological** | Thrombocytopenia, haemorrhagic ("black") measles, DIC | Rare |
| **Cardiac** | Myocarditis, pericarditis | Rare |
| **Pregnancy** | Maternal pneumonia, miscarriage, preterm birth, congenital measles | |
| **Death** | Case fatality 1–3 per 1,000 in high-income settings; up to 3–6% or more in malnourished, crowded settings | |

### SSPE in more detail

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | A slowly progressive, **fatal** brain disease caused by a defective (mutant) measles virus persisting in neurons |
| **Risk** | About **4–11 per 100,000 measles cases** overall, but much higher (estimates of 1 in 1,000–1,700) when measles occurs **under 2 years** (before the vaccine is effective); not caused by the vaccine |
| **Latency** | Mean **7–10 years** after measles |
| **Stages** | (1) Behaviour change, **declining school performance**, irritability → (2) **myoclonic jerks** (periodic), seizures, ataxia → (3) rigidity, dementia, autonomic instability → (4) vegetative state, death usually within **1–3 years** |
| **Diagnosis** | **EEG: periodic high-voltage complexes** (Radermecker); **CSF: markedly raised measles IgG** (raised CSF:serum antibody index, oligoclonal bands); MRI white-matter changes; fundus: chorioretinitis |
| **Treatment** | No cure; isoprinosine/interferon and antiepileptics are palliative |
| **Prevention** | **Measles vaccination**, and protecting young infants through high community coverage (herd immunity) |

## Ward-round & exam pearls

**Model case presentation**
> "Baby A, a 2-year-old girl from Kassanda district, **weight 9.5 kg (weight-for-age Z −2.6), height 82 cm (height-for-age Z −1.8), weight-for-height Z −2.4, MUAC 12.1 cm, no oedema**, admitted yesterday with 4 days of fever, cough, runny nose and red eyes, followed by a rash that began behind the ears 3 days ago and spread to the feet. Her elder brother had a similar illness 2 weeks ago. She received **BCG, OPV, pentavalent, PCV, rotavirus and MR1 at 9 months, but not MR2** at 18 months; vitamin A and albendazole last given 8 months ago. She is still breastfed with family foods twice a day; the family stopped giving her beans and fish 'because of the measles'. Mother is **HIV negative** (ANC test); the child's status is unknown.
> On admission: temperature 39.2 °C, **RR 54, lower chest indrawing, SpO₂ 88% in air**, HR 150, CRT 2 s, confluent maculopapular rash with early brown staining on the face, bilateral conjunctivitis with clear corneas, superficial mouth ulcers, no stridor, alert. RDT negative.
> **Assessment: measles complicated by severe pneumonia (hypoxaemic) and moderate acute malnutrition, with incomplete immunisation.**
> **Management so far:** isolation, oxygen by nasal prongs (now SpO₂ 95% on 1 L/min), **vitamin A 200,000 IU** yesterday and today (day 14 dose due), ampicillin 475 mg IV 6-hourly + gentamicin 70 mg IV daily, paracetamol, salt-water mouth care and gentian violet, tetracycline eye ointment, feeding counselling.
> **Plan:** continue antibiotics (switch to oral amoxicillin when off oxygen), wean oxygen, HIV test, supplementary feeding (RUSF/MAM programme), **MR2 after recovery**, notify the district, vaccinate unvaccinated siblings, isolation until 4 days after rash onset (ends tomorrow)."

**Likely viva questions**

1. *Describe the rash of measles.* Erythematous maculopapular, starts at hairline/behind ears on day 3–4 of illness, spreads down over 3 days, becomes confluent, fades in the same order with brown staining and fine desquamation.
2. *What are Koplik spots?* Small white/grey spots on a red base on the buccal mucosa opposite the molars, appearing 1–2 days before the rash; pathognomonic.
3. *Vitamin A doses?* 50,000 / 100,000 / 200,000 IU for <6 months / 6–11 months / ≥12 months; UCG day 1, 2 and 14.
4. *Commonest cause of death?* Pneumonia (often secondary bacterial).
5. *When is a measles patient infectious?* 4 days before to 4 days after rash onset.
6. *What is SSPE?* A fatal progressive encephalitis 7–10 years after measles, with myoclonus, dementia, periodic EEG complexes and high CSF measles antibody.
7. *Uganda's measles vaccine schedule?* MR1 at 9 months, MR2 at 18 months.

**OSCE tips:** in a counselling station, explain isolation, vitamin A (three doses in Uganda), feeding (no taboos), danger signs (fast breathing, not drinking, eye pain or white spot on the eye, convulsions), and **vaccinating siblings**. In a vaccination station, demonstrate checking the card, the vial vaccine monitor (VVM), reconstitution with the correct diluent, **0.5 mL subcutaneous** injection, discarding reconstituted vaccine after 6 hours, and recording.

## Mnemonics & memory aids

- **"3 Cs and a K"**: **C**ough, **C**oryza, **C**onjunctivitis + **K**oplik spots (before the rash).
- **"4 before, 4 after"**: infectious from 4 days before to 4 days after the rash appears.
- **Vitamin A "50-100-200"**: 50,000 IU (<6 months), 100,000 IU (6–11 months), 200,000 IU (≥12 months); **UCG "1-2-14"** (days).
- **Complications "MEASLES"**: **M**alnutrition (and mouth ulcers), **E**ncephalitis (and eye damage), **A**cute otitis media, **S**SPE, **L**aryngotracheobronchitis (croup), **E**nteritis (diarrhoea), **S**econdary bacterial pneumonia.
- **Severe complicated measles "DEM"**: **D**anger sign, **E**ye (corneal clouding), **M**outh (deep or extensive ulcers).
- **MR schedule "9 and 18"**.

## High-yield summary

### Numbers & doses to know

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Incubation | About **10–12 days** to prodrome; about **14 days** (7–21) to rash |
| Prodrome | **2–4 days** (3 Cs, Koplik spots 1–2 days before rash) |
| Infectious period | **4 days before to 4 days after** rash onset |
| Isolation | Until **4 days after rash onset** (whole illness if immunocompromised) |
| Secondary attack rate | **≥90%** in susceptible household contacts; R0 12–18 |
| Vitamin A | **50,000 IU** (<6 m), **100,000 IU** (6–11 m), **200,000 IU** (≥12 m) |
| Vitamin A schedule | **UCG: day 1, 2, 14**; WHO: day 1 and 2 (+ 3rd at 2–4 weeks if eye signs) |
| Tetracycline eye ointment | 1%, UCG every 12 h × 5 days (WHO 3 times a day × 7 days) |
| Gentian violet | **0.25%** for mouth ulcers |
| Severe mouth ulcers | Benzylpenicillin 50,000 IU/kg 6-hourly + metronidazole 7.5 mg/kg 8-hourly × 5 days |
| Pre-referral antibiotics (UCG) | Ampicillin 50 mg/kg IM + gentamicin 7.5 mg/kg IM |
| Post-exposure vaccine | Within **72 hours**, from **6 months** |
| Immunoglobulin | Within **6 days** for high-risk contacts |
| MR schedule (Uganda) | **9 and 18 months**; outbreak dose from 6 months |
| MR dose | **0.5 mL subcutaneous**; discard reconstituted vaccine after **6 hours** |
| Efficacy | ~85% (1 dose at 9 m), ~95%+ (2 doses) |
| Herd immunity threshold | **95%** coverage |
| Acute encephalitis | About **1 per 1,000** cases |
| SSPE | **4–11 per 100,000** (higher if infected <2 years); onset 7–10 years later |

### Classic exam traps

- **"Vitamin A only for malnourished children."** Wrong: **every** child with measles.
- **"Steroid eye drops for measles conjunctivitis."** Wrong: steroids worsen corneal infection; use tetracycline ointment.
- **"Measles is over once the rash appears."** Wrong: the dangerous complications come with or after the rash; persistent fever means a complication.
- **"Malnutrition is a contraindication to measles vaccine."** Wrong: malnourished children need it most.
- **"A dose at 6 months in an outbreak counts as MR1."** Wrong: still give MR1 at 9 months and MR2 at 18 months.
- **"Admit to the general ward."** Wrong: isolate; vaccinate other children on the ward.
- **"Stop meat and fish during measles."** Wrong (and a common local myth): feed more, not less.
- **"Antibiotics treat measles."** Wrong: antibiotics treat complications (pneumonia, otitis, severe mouth ulcers), not the virus.

### Questions seniors ask

**What makes this measles "severe complicated"?**
Any general danger sign, clouding of the cornea, or deep or extensive mouth ulcers (IMCI); WHO PB adds pneumonia, dehydration, stridor and severe malnutrition.

**Why is pneumonia so common and dangerous in measles?**
The virus damages the respiratory epithelium and causes immune suppression (immune amnesia), so secondary bacterial pneumonia (pneumococcus, *S. aureus*) follows; over half of measles pneumonia is bacterial.

**How do you protect the other children on this ward?**
Isolate the case, check the vaccination status of every child aged 6 months or more and vaccinate within 72 hours of exposure, check staff immunity, and notify the district.

**Her fever has continued for 5 days after the rash. What will you look for?**
Pneumonia, otitis media, mouth infection, diarrhoea with dehydration, encephalitis, bacteraemia, and TB reactivation; examine the chest, ears, mouth and eyes and check SpO₂.

**Why does Uganda still have measles outbreaks when MR1 coverage is over 90%?**
MR1 at 9 months protects only about 85% of children, and MR2 coverage was only 64% in 2025; zero-dose children cluster in hard-to-reach areas, and measles needs about 95% immunity to stop spreading.

**A teenager has declining school performance and jerks. Why ask about measles?**
SSPE: measles in infancy can lead to fatal encephalitis 7–10 years later; check EEG and CSF measles antibody.
