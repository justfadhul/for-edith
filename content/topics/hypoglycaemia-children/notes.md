## In a nutshell

**Hypoglycaemia** means a blood glucose level low enough to harm the brain. Glucose is the brain's main fuel, and a child's brain is large compared with the body, so children use **two to three times more glucose per kg** than adults and have **small glycogen stores**. A sick, fasting or malnourished child can run out within hours.

In Uganda, hypoglycaemia is mostly seen in children with **severe malaria, sepsis, severe acute malnutrition (SAM)**, in **newborns** (preterm, small, cold, asphyxiated, babies of diabetic mothers), and in children who have **not eaten** for many hours. It is also a feature of **poisoning** (alcohol, sulphonylureas), **insulin treatment** in type 1 diabetes, and rare endocrine and metabolic diseases.

It is **easy to miss** (it looks like the illness itself: drowsiness, convulsions, coma), **easy to diagnose** (a glucometer reading in 30 seconds) and **easy to treat** (10% dextrose). Missing it causes **brain damage, epilepsy and death**.

> [!PEARL]
> The one thing never to forget: **check the glucose in every child who is unconscious, convulsing, severely malnourished, in shock or unable to feed. If you cannot check it, treat it**: 10% dextrose 5 mL/kg IV (2 mL/kg in a newborn).

## Definitions & classification

There is no single "normal" cut-off. We use **operational thresholds**: the level at which we act.

| Group | Treat when glucose is below | Source / comment |
|---|---|---|
| **Well-nourished child (ETAT, sick child)** | **2.5 mmol/L (45 mg/dL)** | WHO Pocket Book 2013, ETAT |
| **Child with SAM** | **3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL)** | WHO SAM protocol, UCG 2023 (chapter 19) |
| **Severe malaria (criterion of severity)** | **2.2 mmol/L (40 mg/dL)** | UCG 2023 malaria table; WHO malaria guidelines |
| **General (UCG 2023 section 1.1.6)** | Symptoms usually occur **<3.0 mmol/L** (55 mg/dL) | Adults and children |
| **Neonate** | **2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL)** in Ugandan newborn units; WHO Pocket Book treats **<2.2 mmol/L** | See [!UGANDA] below and the Neonatal Resuscitation topic |
| **Child with diabetes (ISPAD 2022)** | **Level 1 <3.9 mmol/L** (alert, treat with fast sugar); **Level 2 <3.0 mmol/L** (clinically significant); **Level 3** = severe event needing another person's help (coma/convulsion) | ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines 2022 |
| **Persistent/recurrent hypoglycaemia work-up** | Keep glucose **>3.3 mmol/L (60 mg/dL)** in the first 48 h of life, and **>3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL)** after 48 h if a persistent disorder is suspected | Pediatric Endocrine Society 2015 (specialist targets) |

**Conversion**: mmol/L × 18 = mg/dL (2.5 mmol/L = 45 mg/dL; 3.0 = 54; 2.2 = 40).

**Whipple's triad** (used to confirm a true hypoglycaemic disorder in older children): (1) symptoms of hypoglycaemia, (2) a low plasma glucose measured at that time, and (3) relief of symptoms when glucose is raised.

### Classification by cause

| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Too little supply** | Fasting, vomiting, poor feeding, SAM, delayed breastfeeding in newborns, malabsorption |
| **Too much use** | Sepsis, **malaria** (parasites consume glucose), fever, hypothermia, convulsions, respiratory distress, asphyxia |
| **Too little production** (liver) | Prematurity/SGA (low glycogen), liver failure, glycogen storage disease, galactosaemia, hereditary fructose intolerance, gluconeogenesis defects, **alcohol** (blocks gluconeogenesis) |
| **Hormone deficiency** | **Cortisol** (CAH, Addison's, sudden stop of steroids, hypopituitarism), **growth hormone** (hypopituitarism: think micropenis + prolonged jaundice + hypoglycaemia in a newborn) |
| **Too much insulin (hyperinsulinism)** | **Infant of a diabetic mother**, **quinine**, insulin overdose in diabetes, **sulphonylurea ingestion** (grandparent's glibenclamide), congenital hyperinsulinism, Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome, insulinoma (rare), perinatal stress hyperinsulinism (SGA, asphyxia) |
| **Ketotic hypoglycaemia** | Thin toddlers (18 months–5 years) who become hypoglycaemic after an overnight fast or during an intercurrent illness; ketones high, insulin suppressed; a diagnosis of exclusion |
| **Fatty-acid oxidation defects** | Hypoglycaemia **without ketones** during fasting/illness (e.g. MCAD deficiency); may present as "sudden infant death" |

## Epidemiology & Uganda context

- Hypoglycaemia is found in roughly **one in ten or more** children admitted with **severe malaria** in African studies, and many more in those in coma; it is associated with a several-fold increase in mortality. Quinine used to make this worse; IV **artesunate** (now first line in Uganda) causes much less hypoglycaemia.
- **SAM**: most deaths in the first days of admission come from the trio of **hypoglycaemia, hypothermia and infection**. That is why the WHO/UCG protocol feeds every 2–3 hours, day and night.
- **Newborns**: preterm/LBW babies, babies of mothers with diabetes or GDM (rising in Uganda), asphyxiated, cold or septic babies, and babies who are not breastfed early.
- **Children with type 1 diabetes**: hypoglycaemia is the commonest acute complication of insulin therapy; families often lack glucometers, strips or reliable food supply.
- Ugandan studies of critically ill children (e.g. at Fort Portal Regional Referral Hospital) found **dysglycaemia (low or high glucose) in a substantial proportion of emergency admissions, linked to higher mortality**.

> [!UGANDA]
> Practical realities: **glucometers** are available at most HC IVs and hospitals but **strips often run out**; 10% dextrose is usually **made on the ward** from 50% dextrose and water for injection or 5% dextrose; NG tubes and sugar are always available. Neonatal units in Uganda (Mulago, Mengo, RRHs) generally treat newborns at **<2.6 mmol/L**, which is what the Neonatal Resuscitation topic uses; the WHO Pocket Book uses 2.2 mmol/L. When in doubt in a symptomatic baby, treat.

## Physiology & pathophysiology

### How the body keeps glucose up

```
FED (0–4 h)        → glucose from the gut; insulin high → glycogen stored
POST-ABSORPTIVE    → glycogenolysis (liver glycogen) — lasts only ~8–12 h in
(4–12 h)              an infant, much less in a preterm or SGA baby
FASTING (>12 h)    → gluconeogenesis (from lactate, alanine, glycerol)
                     + lipolysis → fatty acids → KETONES (brain fuel)
Counter-regulatory hormones: glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol, growth hormone
```

Insulin is the only hormone that **lowers** glucose. Normally, as glucose falls, insulin switches **off**, fat breakdown switches **on** and ketones rise. So:

- **Low glucose + high ketones** = the body is responding normally to starvation (ketotic hypoglycaemia, SAM, prolonged fasting) or there is a hormone deficiency (cortisol/GH) or glycogen storage disease.
- **Low glucose + no ketones** = insulin is still switched on (**hyperinsulinism**: IDM, quinine, sulphonylurea, insulin) or the child cannot burn fat (**fatty-acid oxidation defect**).

### Why malaria causes hypoglycaemia

1. **Reduced intake**: vomiting, anorexia, fasting.
2. **Increased use**: fever, parasites (which metabolise glucose anaerobically), and hypoxic tissues.
3. **Impaired gluconeogenesis**: the liver is overwhelmed; lactic acidosis.
4. **Quinine** stimulates the pancreatic beta cells to **release insulin** (hyperinsulinaemic hypoglycaemia), especially in pregnancy and in children.

### Why newborns are vulnerable

At birth the continuous glucose supply through the placenta stops. Glucose normally **falls in the first 1–2 hours** and then rises as feeding and glycogenolysis begin ("transitional hypoglycaemia"). Babies with **small glycogen stores** (preterm, SGA), **high use** (cold, septic, asphyxiated, respiratory distress) or **high insulin** (infant of a diabetic mother, because the fetal pancreas has been making extra insulin) cannot cope.

### Why the brain is damaged

Neurons cannot store glucose. Severe or prolonged hypoglycaemia causes **neuronal death**, particularly in the **occipital cortex** (newborns: cortical blindness, epilepsy) and the hippocampus. Recurrent neonatal hypoglycaemia is linked to later developmental delay and learning problems.

## Clinical acumen

### Focused history

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Age, weight, gestation and birth weight (neonate) | Preterm/SGA babies have little glycogen; doses per kg |
| When did the child last eat or breastfeed? Vomiting? | Fasting is the commonest trigger |
| Fever, convulsions, reduced consciousness | Malaria, sepsis, meningitis |
| Medicines: quinine, insulin, any adult diabetes medicines in the home (glibenclamide, gliclazide, metformin), beta-blockers, salicylates, herbal medicines | Drug-induced hyperinsulinism or toxicity |
| Alcohol (local brew, waragi, sachets) | Alcohol blocks gluconeogenesis; small children become hypoglycaemic and hypothermic |
| Mother has diabetes or GDM? Big baby? | Infant of a diabetic mother → hyperinsulinism in the first 24–72 h |
| Previous episodes; relation to fasting or meals; time since last meal at each episode | Recurrent fasting hypoglycaemia → ketotic, endocrine or metabolic cause |
| Growth, weight loss, diarrhoea, oedema | SAM |
| Known diabetes: insulin dose, last injection, meal skipped, exercise | Diabetes-related hypoglycaemia |
| Family history: unexplained infant deaths, consanguinity | Inborn errors of metabolism |
| Prolonged jaundice, micropenis, midline defects (newborn) | Hypopituitarism |
| HIV status, immunisation, feeding | Background risk and sepsis |

### Examination

- **Symptoms and signs by mechanism**:

| Adrenergic (early; glucose falling) | Neuroglycopenic (brain short of fuel) |
|---|---|
| Sweating, tremor, pallor, tachycardia, anxiety, hunger | Irritability, lethargy, confusion, abnormal behaviour, headache, slurred speech, seizures, coma, focal deficits |

- **Newborns** may show: jitteriness, poor suck, floppiness, apnoea, cyanotic spells, hypothermia, high-pitched or weak cry, seizures, or **nothing at all**. Screening at-risk babies is therefore essential.
- **Children with SAM, beta-blockers or recurrent episodes** may lack the adrenergic warning signs.
- Look for the cause: **temperature**, pallor, jaundice, hepatomegaly (glycogen storage disease, heart failure), splenomegaly (malaria), wasting/oedema (SAM), **hyperpigmentation** and **ambiguous genitalia** (CAH/adrenal insufficiency), midline defects/micropenis (hypopituitarism), macrosomia/macroglossia/omphalocele (Beckwith–Wiedemann), smell of alcohol.

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - Coma or convulsions **not improving within 15–30 minutes** of correcting the glucose: look for another cause (cerebral malaria, meningitis, poisoning, head injury).
> - Hypoglycaemia that **recurs** despite feeds and a 10% dextrose infusion: needs a higher glucose infusion rate and senior input (hyperinsulinism, sepsis, adrenal insufficiency).
> - **Hypoglycaemia + hypothermia** in a malnourished or newborn child = sepsis until proven otherwise.
> - Hypoglycaemia with **shock, hyponatraemia and hyperkalaemia** in an infant (± ambiguous genitalia or hyperpigmentation) = **adrenal crisis**: give hydrocortisone.
> - Hypoglycaemia in a child **without** ketones: hyperinsulinism or a fatty-acid oxidation defect.

### Thinking like a clinician

- **"Treat first, think second."** The bolus takes 2 minutes; the work-up can wait.
- **"Why is this child hypoglycaemic?"** is the second question. In Uganda the answer is usually **malaria, sepsis, SAM, fasting, or the newborn period**. If none fits, or it keeps recurring, think of drugs (sulphonylurea, alcohol), hormones (cortisol, GH), the liver, and hyperinsulinism.
- **Unconscious child with malaria whose glucose is now 6 mmol/L but who is still in coma**: the hypoglycaemia was not the whole story (cerebral malaria, post-ictal state, meningitis). Keep checking glucose 2–4 hourly.
- **A baby who needs a lot of glucose** (glucose infusion rate >8–10 mg/kg/min) to stay normal almost certainly has **hyperinsulinism**.
- **Glucagon works only when the liver has glycogen**: useful for insulin overdose in a well-fed diabetic child; useless in SAM, prolonged fasting, alcohol, or liver disease.
- **Common student mistakes**: not checking glucose in a convulsing child; giving 50% dextrose undiluted (vein damage, rebound); giving a bolus but not following it with feeds or a dextrose infusion (rebound hypoglycaemia); forgetting to recheck at 30 minutes; using the adult threshold of 4 mmol/L to decide on a bolus in a non-diabetic sick child (fine to be cautious, but know the numbers); using glucagon in SAM.

> [!EXAM]
> Viva favourite: **"Why does quinine cause hypoglycaemia, and how do you prevent it?"** Quinine stimulates insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells. Give quinine diluted in **5% or 10% dextrose**, feed the child, check glucose every 2–4 hours, and prefer **IV artesunate** (UCG 2023 first line for severe malaria).

## Clinical workup

### Step 0: First 5 minutes

1. **ABC** (ETAT): open the airway, recovery position if unconscious, oxygen if SpO₂ <94% with emergency signs.
2. **Check glucose** (glucometer, heel prick in infants, finger prick in older children).
3. **If <2.5 mmol/L (or <3.0 in SAM, <2.6 in a neonate), or you cannot measure it**:
   - IV access → **10% dextrose 5 mL/kg** (neonate **2 mL/kg**) over 2–5 minutes.
   - No IV → NG 10% dextrose/sugar water 5 mL/kg, sublingual sugar, or IO; glucagon if insulin-induced.
4. **If convulsing**, give the anticonvulsant as well (rectal diazepam 0.5 mg/kg; phenobarbital in neonates).
5. **If the cause is unclear** (no malaria, no SAM, not a newborn, not diabetic), draw a **critical sample** (below) **with the cannula, before the dextrose**, but never let it delay treatment.
6. **Recheck glucose after 30 minutes** (15 minutes in a diabetic child). If still low, repeat the bolus.

> [!REDFLAG]
> Call your senior if: glucose stays low after two boluses; the child remains unconscious after glucose is normal; there is shock or suspected adrenal crisis; a newborn needs repeated boluses.

### Step 1: Focused history

- [ ] **Last feed / meal and duration of fasting** → fasting-related (ketotic, SAM) vs inappropriate (hyperinsulinism if hypoglycaemic soon after feeding).
- [ ] **Fever, malaria test result, antimalarial given (quinine?)** → malaria-related; quinine → hyperinsulinism.
- [ ] **Diabetes and insulin** (dose, timing, missed meal, exercise) → insulin-induced; plan for education.
- [ ] **Possible ingestion**: diabetes tablets in the house, alcohol, herbal medicines → poisoning; sulphonylurea needs prolonged observation (24 h).
- [ ] **Newborn**: gestation, birth weight, maternal diabetes, asphyxia, temperature, feeding → transitional vs persistent.
- [ ] **Previous episodes** → recurrent hypoglycaemia needs investigation.
- [ ] **Growth and nutrition, HIV status, immunisation** → SAM, chronic disease.
- [ ] **Family history** of sudden infant death, consanguinity → inborn errors.

### Step 2: Focused examination

**General**: conscious level (AVPU/Blantyre), temperature (hypothermia), weight, MUAC, oedema, jaundice, pallor, hydration.

**Specific**: liver size (glycogen storage disease, liver failure), spleen (malaria), genitalia and pigmentation (CAH), midline defects and micropenis (hypopituitarism), large baby with large tongue (IDM, Beckwith–Wiedemann), injection sites (insulin), signs of sepsis.

| Document these positives | And these negatives |
|---|---|
| Glucose value, time, and the method (glucometer vs lab) | "No hepatomegaly" |
| AVPU/Blantyre score before and after dextrose | "Genitalia normal; no hyperpigmentation" |
| Temperature, weight, MUAC, oedema | "No signs of fluid overload" |
| Time and volume of each dextrose bolus | "Glucose rechecked at 30 min: x mmol/L" |

### Step 3: Bedside tests

- **Glucometer glucose**: fast and available; less accurate at very low levels (confirm with a lab glucose if possible, but **never wait for it** before treating).
- **Urine or blood ketones** (dipstick, BOHB meter): ketotic (starvation, ketotic hypoglycaemia, hormone deficiency) vs non-ketotic (hyperinsulinism, fatty-acid oxidation defect).
- **mRDT / malaria smear**, **Hb** (HemoCue), **temperature**.
- **MUAC** (6–59 months).

### Step 4: Laboratory & imaging

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management | Uganda availability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Laboratory plasma glucose | To confirm a glucometer reading | <2.5 mmol/L (or local threshold) | Confirms the diagnosis | Hospital labs |
| Malaria smear/mRDT | Every febrile child | Parasitaemia | IV artesunate; check glucose 2–4 hourly | Everywhere |
| Blood culture, FBC, CRP | Fever, hypothermia, SAM, newborn | Sepsis | Antibiotics | RRH/national referral |
| Urine/blood ketones | Unexplained hypoglycaemia | High (ketotic) vs low/absent (hyperinsulinism, FAOD) | Directs further tests | Dipstick at HC IV; BOHB meters rare |
| **Critical sample**: insulin, C-peptide, cortisol, growth hormone, BOHB, free fatty acids, lactate, ammonia; urine organic acids | Unexplained or recurrent hypoglycaemia, taken **at the time of hypoglycaemia** | Insulin detectable when glucose low = hyperinsulinism; low cortisol = adrenal insufficiency; high lactate = gluconeogenesis defect/GSD | Specific treatment (diazoxide, hydrocortisone, diet) | Mulago/private labs or send-out; often unavailable. Freeze serum if possible |
| Electrolytes (Na, K) | Suspected adrenal crisis, vomiting | Low Na, high K | Hydrocortisone, saline | RRH/national referral |
| LFTs, INR | Hepatomegaly, jaundice | Liver failure | Supportive, referral | Hospitals |
| Sulphonylurea screen, alcohol level | Suspected ingestion | Positive | Observe ≥24 h; octreotide | Rarely available |

### Step 5: Putting it together

**Model one-line summary**: "A 3-year-old girl, 12 kg, with severe malaria and **hypoglycaemia (1.8 mmol/L)** causing a convulsion; corrected with 10% dextrose 60 mL, now 5.6 mmol/L on maintenance 10% dextrose-containing fluid and IV artesunate."

**Problem list**: (1) hypoglycaemia, (2) its cause (severe malaria), (3) risk of recurrence, (4) other complications (anaemia, acidosis, seizures).

> [!NOTE]
> **Worked example: calculating a glucose infusion rate (GIR).** A 2.0 kg neonate is on 10% dextrose at 90 mL/kg/day and is still hypoglycaemic. **GIR = % dextrose × mL/kg/day ÷ 144 = 10 × 90 ÷ 144 = 6.25 mg/kg/min.** Normal neonatal glucose production is about 4–6 mg/kg/min. Increase the fluid volume (if the baby tolerates it) or the concentration (12.5% through a peripheral line at most; higher concentrations need a central line). If the baby needs **>8–10 mg/kg/min**, suspect **hyperinsulinism** and discuss with a paediatrician.

> [!NOTE]
> **Worked example: ketones tell you the mechanism.** A 2-year-old has a seizure at 7 a.m. after missing supper because of a cold; glucose 2.1 mmol/L, **urine ketones +++**, thin, no hepatomegaly. → **Ketotic hypoglycaemia** (diagnosis of exclusion). Plan: dextrose, then advise frequent carbohydrate snacks, a bedtime snack, and sugary drinks during illness; check ketones at home if possible. If **ketones were negative**, you would worry about hyperinsulinism or a fatty-acid oxidation defect and refer.

## Differential diagnosis

When a child presents with coma, convulsions or abnormal behaviour, hypoglycaemia is one cause among several, and often coexists with them.

| Condition | Distinguishing features | Key investigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypoglycaemia** | Rapid improvement after dextrose | Glucose |
| Cerebral malaria | Fever, positive mRDT; coma persists after glucose corrected | Smear, glucose |
| Bacterial meningitis | Neck stiffness, bulging fontanelle, purpura | LP (when safe) |
| Febrile convulsion | Age 6 months–5 years, brief generalised seizure, full recovery | Glucose normal; exclude meningitis/malaria |
| Post-ictal state | Recovers within about 1 hour | Observation |
| Poisoning (alcohol, sulphonylurea, OP, sedatives) | History, smell, pupils, secretions | History; glucose (alcohol and sulphonylureas cause hypoglycaemia) |
| DKA / hyperglycaemia | Polyuria, acidotic breathing, high glucose, ketones | Glucose, urine ketones |
| Hypocalcaemia (neonate, rickets) | Tetany, jitteriness, seizures with normal glucose | Calcium |
| Hyponatraemia | Diarrhoea, hypotonic fluids, seizures | Sodium |
| Neonatal jitteriness (benign) | Stops with gentle restraint, baby feeds well, glucose normal | Glucose |
| Adrenal crisis | Shock, low Na, high K, ± ambiguous genitalia/pigmentation | Electrolytes, cortisol |

| Test | What you are looking for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose (bedside then lab) | Level and recovery | Confirms and monitors |

## Management

### A. Emergency treatment algorithm

```
Sick child: coma / convulsing / SAM / shock / not feeding / newborn at risk
                        │
                CHECK GLUCOSE (or cannot check)
                        │
      Low* or unknown? ─┴─ Normal → look for other causes; recheck 4-hourly if at risk
            │
   Conscious & can swallow? ── yes ──► oral sugar/feed (0.3 g/kg; breastfeed; milk; F-75)
            │ no
   IV/IO access? ── yes ──► 10% DEXTROSE 5 mL/kg (neonate 2 mL/kg) over 2–5 min
            │ no
   NG tube: 10% dextrose or sugar water 5 mL/kg, or milk
   + sublingual sugar every 10–20 min; glucagon if insulin-treated
            │
   RECHECK at 30 min ── still low? → repeat bolus; senior review
            │
   Prevent rebound: feed or start dextrose infusion; treat the cause
   Monitor 1–4 hourly until stable

*<2.5 mmol/L child · <3.0 mmol/L SAM · <2.6 mmol/L neonate (Uganda) · <3.9 mmol/L diabetic
```

> [!DRUG]
> **5 mL/kg of 10% dextrose = 0.5 g/kg glucose.** In a 12 kg child: 60 mL of 10% = 12 mL of 50% + 48 mL water for injection. Write the volume of 10% on the chart, not "50% dextrose 1 ampoule".

### C. Hypoglycaemia in severe malaria

1. Check glucose **on admission** in every child with severe malaria, then **every 4 hours** (every 2 hours if unconscious, convulsing or on quinine), and at any deterioration.
2. Treat <2.5 mmol/L (UCG severity criterion <2.2 mmol/L) with **10% dextrose 5 mL/kg over 5–7 minutes**; recheck at 30 min.
3. Give **maintenance fluid containing dextrose** (e.g. 5% or 10% dextrose in 0.45% saline) if not feeding; start feeds (breastfeeding or NG milk) as soon as safe.
4. Give **IV/IM artesunate**: 3 mg/kg if <20 kg, 2.4 mg/kg if ≥20 kg, at 0, 12 and 24 h, then daily until able to take oral ACT.
5. If quinine must be used (artesunate unavailable): **10 mg/kg in 5–10% dextrose** over 4 hours, 8-hourly (UCG gives quinine in dextrose 5%), with 2-hourly glucose checks.

### D. Hypoglycaemia in severe acute malnutrition

1. **All** SAM children are treated as at risk: on admission give **50 mL of 10% glucose or sugar water** (1 rounded teaspoon of sugar in 3 tablespoons of water, UCG 2023) orally or by NG, followed by the first feed as soon as possible.
2. If able to drink: **F-75 every 30 minutes for 2 hours**, then **every 2 hours for 24 hours** (day and night), then every 2–3 hours.
3. If unconscious or convulsing: **10% glucose 5 mL/kg IV**, then 50 mL of 10% glucose or sucrose by NG, then F-75 by NG.
4. Recheck glucose after 30 minutes and every 2–4 hours on day 1; if it falls below 3 mmol/L again, repeat the 50 mL glucose and continue 2-hourly feeds.
5. Keep warm (skin-to-skin, cover the head), and start **antibiotics** (hypoglycaemia in SAM usually means infection).
6. **Do not use glucagon** (no glycogen).

### E. Neonatal hypoglycaemia

1. **Prevent**: breastfeed within 1 hour of birth, then every 2–3 hours (at least 8 times/24 h); keep warm (skin-to-skin, KMC).
2. **Screen at-risk babies** (preterm, LBW/SGA <2.5 kg, infant of a diabetic mother, asphyxia, sepsis, hypothermia, poor feeding): glucose before the second feed (2–4 h of age), then before feeds for at least 24 hours (IDM, SGA) or until stable.
3. **Asymptomatic, 2.0–2.6 mmol/L, able to feed**: breastfeed or give expressed breast milk (cup or NG, about 10 mL/kg), recheck in 30–60 min. If still low → IV dextrose.
4. **Symptomatic, or <2.0–2.2 mmol/L, or not rising with feeds**: **10% dextrose 2 mL/kg IV**, then **10% dextrose infusion 60 mL/kg/day** on day 1 (higher on later days: about 80, 100, 120, 150 mL/kg/day from day 2 to day 5, per unit protocol); continue enteral feeds if tolerated; recheck 30 min after the bolus, then 1–3 hourly until stable.
5. Persistent need for GIR **>8–10 mg/kg/min** or hypoglycaemia beyond 48–72 h → think hyperinsulinism, hypopituitarism, adrenal insufficiency, inborn errors; refer.

### F. Hypoglycaemia in a child with type 1 diabetes (ISPAD 2022)

1. **Conscious**: give **0.3 g/kg** fast-acting glucose (about 10–15 g: 3 glucose tablets, 150 mL juice or soda, 2–3 teaspoons sugar in water). Recheck in **15 minutes**; repeat if still <3.9 mmol/L ("rule of 15").
2. Then a **snack with complex carbohydrate** (bread, posho, banana) if the next meal is more than an hour away.
3. **Unconscious or convulsing**: **glucagon 0.5 mg (<25 kg) or 1 mg (≥25 kg) IM/SC** at home, or **10% dextrose 2–5 mL/kg IV** in hospital (NICE: maximum 5 mL/kg), then a dextrose infusion until able to eat.
4. **Find the cause**: missed meal, extra exercise, insulin error, alcohol, new illness (vomiting). Adjust insulin, educate the family, and make sure there is sugar at home and at school.

### G. Prevention (the most important part of the tutorial)

| Group | Prevention |
|---|---|
| All sick children | Keep feeding (breastfeed, cup feeds, NG milk); avoid prolonged "nil by mouth"; maintenance fluids with dextrose if not feeding; check glucose in at-risk children |
| Severe malaria | 4-hourly glucose (2-hourly if unconscious/quinine); artesunate rather than quinine; quinine in dextrose; early feeding |
| SAM | Feed every 2–3 h **including at night** (night feeds prevent most deaths), warmth, antibiotics |
| Newborns | Early and frequent breastfeeding, warmth, screen at-risk babies |
| Before surgery | Minimal fasting (clear fluids until 1–2 h before; breast milk until 4 h), dextrose-containing fluids |
| Diabetes | Education on symptoms, sugar always available, glucometer, glucagon if possible, regular meals, insulin adjustment for exercise and illness |
| Poisoning | Keep adult medicines (diabetes tablets) and alcohol out of reach |

## Complications

| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Death** | Especially in severe malaria, SAM and neonates |
| **Seizures, status epilepticus** | Hypoglycaemia lowers the seizure threshold |
| **Permanent brain injury** | Occipital lobe damage (cortical visual impairment), epilepsy, developmental delay, learning problems |
| **Rebound hypoglycaemia** | After a bolus without follow-up feeding or infusion |
| **Thrombophlebitis / extravasation injury** | Concentrated dextrose (>12.5% peripherally) |
| **Hyperglycaemia and osmotic diuresis** | Overtreatment, especially in neonates on high GIR |
| **Fluid overload** | Large-volume dextrose infusions in neonates or malnourished children |

## Ward-round & exam pearls

### Model case presentation

> "Baby A.K., a **2-year-6-month-old girl** from Kawempe, **weight 11.5 kg** (weight-for-height Z-score −1, height-for-age Z −1.5, MUAC 13.5 cm, no oedema), admitted at 03:00 with **fever for 2 days, vomiting and a generalised convulsion at home**. On arrival she was **AVPU P**, glucose **1.6 mmol/L**, mRDT positive (smear 3+), Hb 8.2 g/dL. She received **10% dextrose 57 mL IV** (5 mL/kg) and rectal diazepam 5 mg; glucose at 30 minutes was **5.9 mmol/L** and she woke to voice within an hour. She is on **IV artesunate 34 mg (3 mg/kg)** and **maintenance 10% dextrose in 0.45% saline at 43 mL/h**. Glucose has been 4.8–6.2 mmol/L on 4-hourly checks. She is **fully immunised for age** (child health card), **eats family food, breastfed until 18 months**; **mother HIV-negative** in this pregnancy, child's HIV test today negative. **Assessment: severe malaria with hypoglycaemia and a convulsion, now stable**. **Plan**: continue artesunate to 3 doses then oral ACT, start oral feeds and reduce IV fluid, 4-hourly glucose until feeding well for 24 h, Hb tomorrow, counsel on bed nets and early care-seeking."

### Viva questions with answers

1. **Define hypoglycaemia in a sick child.** Glucose <2.5 mmol/L (45 mg/dL) in a well-nourished child, <3.0 mmol/L in SAM (WHO); UCG 2023 notes symptoms usually occur below 3.0 mmol/L.
2. **Dose of dextrose?** 10% dextrose 5 mL/kg IV (2 mL/kg in neonates); recheck in 30 minutes.
3. **Why not 50% dextrose?** It is hyperosmolar (2,500 mOsm/L), sclerosing, can cause tissue necrosis if extravasated and causes rebound hypoglycaemia by a large insulin surge.
4. **How do you treat without IV access?** Sugar water or 10% dextrose by NG tube, sublingual sugar every 10–20 minutes, IO access, glucagon if insulin-induced.
5. **Name five causes of hypoglycaemia in Ugandan children.** Severe malaria (± quinine), sepsis, SAM, prolonged fasting/vomiting, neonatal (preterm/SGA/IDM/asphyxia); also alcohol and sulphonylurea poisoning, insulin in diabetes.
6. **Why does an infant of a diabetic mother become hypoglycaemic?** Fetal hyperinsulinism from chronic exposure to maternal hyperglycaemia continues after the cord is cut.
7. **What is the critical sample?** Blood taken at the time of hypoglycaemia (before dextrose) for glucose, insulin, C-peptide, cortisol, GH, BOHB, free fatty acids, lactate and ammonia, plus the first urine for ketones and organic acids.
8. **When is glucagon useless?** When liver glycogen is depleted: SAM, prolonged fasting, alcohol, liver failure, glycogen storage disease type 1.

### Questions Dr Mawanda is likely to ask (prepare answers)

- **"What threshold do you use in a child with SAM, and why is it higher?"** <3.0 mmol/L; they have no glycogen or fat reserves and lack warning symptoms, so they deteriorate quickly.
- **"How would you give glucose if the IV line fails?"** NG 10% dextrose or sugar water 5 mL/kg; sublingual sugar every 10–20 min; IO line.
- **"Why do children on quinine become hypoglycaemic?"** Quinine stimulates insulin release.
- **"The child's glucose is normal after treatment but he is still unconscious. What next?"** Look for cerebral malaria, meningitis (LP when safe), post-ictal state, poisoning; keep checking glucose.
- **"How do you prevent rebound hypoglycaemia?"** Feed or start a dextrose infusion immediately after the bolus; recheck in 30 minutes and regularly.
- **"What is ketotic hypoglycaemia?"** Fasting hypoglycaemia with high ketones in a thin toddler (18 months–5 years); diagnosis of exclusion; prevented by frequent carbohydrate and sugary drinks in illness; outgrown by school age.
- **"What is the GIR and why does it matter?"** % dextrose × mL/kg/day ÷ 144; >8–10 mg/kg/min suggests hyperinsulinism.

## High-yield summary

### Numbers & doses to know

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Threshold, sick child | **<2.5 mmol/L** (45 mg/dL) |
| Threshold, SAM | **<3.0 mmol/L** (54 mg/dL) |
| Threshold, severe malaria criterion (UCG) | **<2.2 mmol/L** (40 mg/dL) |
| Threshold, neonate (Ugandan units) | **<2.6 mmol/L** (WHO Pocket Book 2.2) |
| Threshold, diabetes (ISPAD 2022) | Level 1 **<3.9**; level 2 **<3.0**; level 3 = needs help |
| Conversion | mmol/L × 18 = mg/dL |
| Bolus, child | **10% dextrose 5 mL/kg** (0.5 g/kg) |
| Bolus, neonate | **10% dextrose 2 mL/kg** |
| Making 10% | **1 part 50% + 4 parts water**; or remove 50 mL from 500 mL of D5 and add 50 mL of D50 |
| Recheck | **30 min** (15 min in diabetes) |
| Oral treatment | **0.3 g/kg** fast sugar (10–20 g) |
| Sugar solution | **20 g (4 level teaspoons) in 200 mL** water |
| SAM admission | **50 mL** 10% glucose or sugar water, then F-75 every 30 min × 2 h, then 2-hourly |
| Glucagon | **0.5 mg (<25 kg); 1 mg (≥25 kg)** IM/SC |
| Neonatal maintenance day 1 | **10% dextrose 60 mL/kg/day** |
| GIR | % × mL/kg/day ÷ 144; normal neonatal need **4–6 mg/kg/min**; **>8–10** = hyperinsulinism |
| Monitoring in severe malaria | Glucose **4-hourly** (2-hourly if unconscious or on quinine) |

### Classic exam traps

- **"Glucose not measurable, so wait for the lab."** Wrong: **treat as hypoglycaemia** in any child with coma or convulsions.
- **"Give 50% dextrose 1 mL/kg as for adults."** Wrong: dilute to **10%** and give 5 mL/kg.
- **"Glucagon for a hypoglycaemic child with kwashiorkor."** Wrong: no glycogen; use IV/NG glucose.
- **"Glucose now normal; stop monitoring."** Wrong: rebound is common; **feed or infuse** and recheck.
- **"Hypoglycaemia in malaria is just from not eating."** Partly: also parasite use, impaired gluconeogenesis and **quinine-induced insulin release**.
- **"SAM child: use the 2.5 mmol/L threshold."** Wrong: **3.0 mmol/L**.
- **"Neonate: give 5 mL/kg like an older child."** Wrong: **2 mL/kg**, then an infusion.
- **"Hypoglycaemia with no ketones is ketotic hypoglycaemia."** Wrong: no ketones → **hyperinsulinism or FAOD**.

### Questions seniors ask

**This child is convulsing. What is your first investigation?**
A bedside glucose. If I can't measure it, I treat with 10% dextrose 5 mL/kg.

**How much 10% dextrose for a 9 kg child, and how will you make it?**
45 mL: 9 mL of 50% dextrose plus 36 mL of water for injection.

**What do you do after the bolus?**
Recheck glucose in 30 minutes, then feed or start a dextrose-containing maintenance infusion, monitor 2–4 hourly, and treat the cause.

**What if the glucose stays low after two boluses?**
Start a 10% dextrose infusion and calculate the GIR; consider sepsis, adrenal insufficiency (hydrocortisone) or hyperinsulinism; call a senior.

**Why is hypoglycaemia so common in SAM?**
No glycogen or fat stores, impaired gluconeogenesis, frequent infection and hypothermia, and long gaps between feeds (especially at night).

**Which newborns do you screen?**
Preterm, LBW/SGA, infants of diabetic mothers, asphyxiated, septic, hypothermic or poorly feeding babies: check before the second feed and before feeds for at least 24 hours.

**How would you counsel the mother of a child with ketotic hypoglycaemia?**
Avoid long fasts, give a bedtime carbohydrate snack, give sugary drinks frequently when the child is ill or not eating, and come to hospital early if the child is drowsy or vomiting; it usually resolves by school age.
