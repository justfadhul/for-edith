## In a nutshell

On the labour ward a small number of drugs do most of the work, and most of the harm. **Magnesium sulphate** prevents eclamptic fits but can stop a woman breathing. **Oxytocin** saves women from haemorrhage but can rupture a uterus or drown a baby in hypoxia. **Ergometrine** contracts the uterus and can also cause a stroke in a hypertensive woman. **Misoprostol** is the most versatile drug in obstetrics, and its dose changes a hundred-fold (25 µg to 800 µg) depending on the gestation and the indication.

This handbook is organised by drug group. Every key drug has a **drug card** covering class, mechanism, indications, dose and route, side effects, contraindications, monitoring, pregnancy and breastfeeding, and antidote. Learn the cards, then test yourself on the flashcards and questions.

> [!PEARL]
> For any drug the examiner asks about, answer in this order: **what it is, why we give it, how much and by which route, what we watch for, when we must not give it, and what reverses it.** That structure alone earns most of the marks.

> [!UGANDA]
> Doses here follow the **Uganda Clinical Guidelines 2023** and WHO recommendations, and match the **Quick reference** page. Where UK practice differs (labetalol, carboprost, anti-D doses) this is flagged. Availability matters: labetalol, carboprost and atosiban are often unavailable in Ugandan public facilities, while MgSO₄, oxytocin, misoprostol, hydralazine, nifedipine and methyldopa are on the essential medicines list.

> [!EXAM]
> Pregnancy changes how drugs behave (bigger volume of distribution, faster renal clearance, lower albumin); the table is in **Drug safety** near the end. The classic viva: "Why can magnesium toxicity develop even when the dose was correct?" Because magnesium is **excreted only by the kidneys**. In severe pre-eclampsia with **oliguria** or rising creatinine, the same maintenance dose accumulates. That is why urine output is checked before every dose.

## Clinical acumen

### Focused history

Before prescribing anything to a pregnant or postpartum woman, ask these questions (each one changes the drug you choose):

- **Gestation, or is she postpartum?** It decides teratogen risk (organogenesis weeks 3–8 after conception), NSAID safety (avoid after ~20 weeks), misoprostol dose and whether ACE inhibitors are allowed (postpartum only).
- **Is she breastfeeding?** Most drugs are fine; check the few that aren't.
- **Allergies**, especially **penicillin**: what exactly happened? True anaphylaxis changes the antibiotic plan; a rash on amoxicillin may not.
- **Asthma** rules out carboprost, labetalol and other beta-blockers, and makes NSAIDs cautious.
- **Hypertension, pre-eclampsia, heart disease or Raynaud's** rule out ergometrine and make oxytocin boluses slow.
- **Kidney function / urine output** affects MgSO₄, gentamicin, NSAIDs and nitrofurantoin.
- **Epilepsy, HIV (which ARVs?), TB (rifampicin?), sickle cell, diabetes, myasthenia gravis** (MgSO₄ is contraindicated).
- **Previous caesarean or uterine surgery**: misoprostol for induction at term is contraindicated, oxytocin needs extra caution.
- **Drugs already given**, and when: how much oxytocin has run in, when the last MgSO₄ dose was, how much lignocaine has already been infiltrated.

### Examination

- **Before MgSO₄**: respiratory rate, patellar reflexes, urine output (catheter), SpO₂, level of consciousness.
- **Before a uterotonic**: blood pressure (ergometrine), chest (asthma, pulmonary oedema), and is the **placenta out** and **is there a second twin?** (ergometrine and syntometrine must never be given with an undelivered twin).
- **Before oxytocin for augmentation**: contraction frequency, fetal heart, **cephalopelvic disproportion** (never augment an obstructed labour), parity and scars.
- **Weight**: for gentamicin (5 mg/kg), lignocaine maximum, enoxaparin and cefazolin (3 g if >120 kg).

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **Loss of patellar reflexes or RR <16/min on MgSO₄**: stop the magnesium; calcium gluconate if breathing is depressed.
> - **More than 5 contractions in 10 minutes or a fetal bradycardia on oxytocin**: stop the drip and resuscitate the fetus.
> - **Headache, confusion, fits or hyponatraemia after hours of oxytocin in dextrose**: water intoxication.
> - **Perioral numbness, tinnitus, metallic taste, then fits** after perineal infiltration: local anaesthetic toxicity.
> - **Wheeze, stridor, facial swelling or hypotension** after an antibiotic: anaphylaxis, give **adrenaline 0.5 mg IM** (0.5 mL of 1:1000).
> - **Crushing headache or BP surge after ergometrine**: hypertensive crisis; treat as severe hypertension.

### Thinking like a clinician

- **"Could the drug be the problem?"** A drowsy woman on MgSO₄, a baby who won't breathe after pethidine, a woman who fits after 15 mL of 2% lignocaine, a hypoglycaemic woman on IV quinine: always think of the drug first.
- **"What is the safest effective option here?"** Hypertensive + PPH → oxytocin, misoprostol, TXA; **not ergometrine**. Asthmatic + PPH → oxytocin, ergometrine (if normotensive), misoprostol; **not carboprost**. Asthmatic + severe hypertension → nifedipine or hydralazine; **not labetalol**.
- **Doses change with purpose.** Oxytocin 10 IU IM (AMTSL) is not the same as 2.5 IU in 500 mL titrated for induction. Misoprostol 25 µg (induction) is not 800 µg (PPH). A decimal error with these drugs kills.
- **Timing is part of the dose.** TXA only works within 3 h of birth; anti-D within 72 h; CS antibiotics within 60 min *before* incision; steroids need 24–48 h to help, which is why tocolysis buys 48 h.
- **Common student mistakes**: calling methyldopa an emergency drug (it takes hours); giving nifedipine sublingually; forgetting to check the undelivered twin before ergometrine; prescribing doxycycline or ciprofloxacin to a pregnant woman; giving 5 mg folic acid with SP (it blocks SP's antimalarial effect).

## Clinical workup

For drugs, the "workup" is the set of checks you do **before** prescribing and the monitoring you do **after**. Use this every time you write a drug chart on the labour ward.

### Step 0: First 5 minutes

In an emergency (eclamptic fit, PPH, anaphylaxis), the drug goes in first and the paperwork follows:

1. **Call for help** and assign someone to draw up drugs and record the time of each dose.
2. **ABC**, left lateral tilt if pregnant, oxygen, IV access with two large-bore cannulae.
3. Give the **first-line drug from memory**: MgSO₄ loading dose (fit), oxytocin 10 IU and TXA 1 g (PPH), adrenaline 0.5 mg IM (anaphylaxis), calcium gluconate 1 g IV (MgSO₄ toxicity with respiratory depression).
4. **Say the dose out loud** and have a second person confirm the drug, strength and route (closed-loop communication). Most labour ward drug errors are ampoule mix-ups (e.g. 50% vs 20% MgSO₄, ergometrine vs oxytocin in the fridge).

### Step 1: Focused history

- [ ] **Allergies** (and the reaction): changes the antibiotic choice.
- [ ] **Gestation or days postpartum**: teratogen window, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, misoprostol dose.
- [ ] **Breastfeeding**: affects a few drugs (methotrexate, cabergoline, some antiepileptics).
- [ ] **Asthma, hypertension, heart disease, myasthenia, epilepsy, renal disease**: each removes specific drugs.
- [ ] **Current medicines**: ARVs (efavirenz/rifampicin interact with hormonal contraception), antiepileptics, anticoagulants.
- [ ] **Doses already given**: especially oxytocin, MgSO₄, lignocaine and opioids.

### Step 2: Focused examination

- **Vital signs**: BP (ergometrine, antihypertensives), pulse (hydralazine tachycardia), RR and SpO₂ (MgSO₄, opioids), temperature (misoprostol fever vs sepsis).
- **Neurological**: patellar reflexes (MgSO₄), level of consciousness.
- **Chest**: wheeze (asthma: avoid carboprost and beta-blockers), crackles (pulmonary oedema: restrict fluids and oxytocin volume).
- **Obstetric**: uterine tone and contractions, fetal heart, placenta delivered, second twin excluded.
- **Weight** for weight-based doses.

### Step 3: Bedside tests

- **Urine output** by catheter (hourly) on MgSO₄ and in PPH.
- **Urine dipstick** for protein and nitrites (pre-eclampsia, UTI before nitrofurantoin).
- **Random blood glucose** on insulin, IV quinine, or after steroids in diabetics.
- **CTG or intermittent fetal heart auscultation** on oxytocin, misoprostol and after antihypertensive boluses (a sudden BP drop can cause fetal distress).
- **Bedside clotting test** before regional anaesthesia or LMWH decisions when a coagulopathy is possible.

### Step 4: Laboratory & imaging

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Serum magnesium** | On MgSO₄ with oliguria, absent reflexes or confusion (regional/national referral; rarely at HC IV) | Therapeutic **2–3.5 mmol/L**; reflexes lost at **4–5**; respiratory depression at **6–7.5** | Withhold or reduce doses; clinical monitoring is the standard where levels are unavailable |
| **Creatinine / urea** | Before and during MgSO₄, gentamicin, NSAIDs | Rising creatinine (>90 µmol/L in pregnancy is abnormal) | Halve or withhold MgSO₄ maintenance; avoid NSAIDs; once-daily gentamicin with care or switch |
| **Serum sodium** | Long oxytocin infusions, especially in dextrose | **<130 mmol/L** = water intoxication | Stop oxytocin, restrict fluids, use saline-based infusions |
| **Blood group and Rh, Coombs** | Every booking; any sensitising event | Rh-negative, non-sensitised | Anti-D within 72 h |
| **Hb / full blood count** | Before iron, after PPH, on hydroxyurea/cotrimoxazole | Low Hb; low platelets (HELLP) | Iron dose; avoid NSAIDs and regional anaesthesia if platelets <75 × 10⁹/L |
| **LFTs** | Methotrexate, pre-eclampsia, TB drugs, nevirapine | ALT/AST rise | Methotrexate contraindicated; review drug choice |
| **Serum β-hCG** | Before and day 4 and 7 after methotrexate | Needs a **≥15% fall day 4 → 7** | Repeat dose or surgery if the fall is <15% |
| **Blood glucose profile** | GDM on insulin or metformin | Fasting >5.3 mmol/L, 1 h >7.8 or 2 h >6.4 | Titrate insulin |
| **G6PD** (where available) | Before primaquine, nitrofurantoin, sulphonamides in known deficiency | Deficient | Avoid oxidant drugs (haemolysis) |

### Step 5: Putting it together

**Model summary before prescribing:** "Mrs K, 24, G2P1 at 36 weeks, 62 kg, with severe pre-eclampsia (BP 168/112, proteinuria 3+). No asthma, no allergies, catheterised with urine 40 mL/h, RR 18, reflexes present. **Plan**: MgSO₄ Pritchard loading dose, nifedipine 10 mg orally now and repeat in 30 min if BP ≥160/110, R-R-U before every dose, hourly BP and fetal heart; calcium gluconate drawn up at the bedside; no ergometrine at delivery."

**Worked example: is it safe to give the next MgSO₄ dose?**
Four hours after loading: RR 14/min, knee jerks absent, urine 60 mL over the last 4 h, drowsy.
- RR <16 and reflexes absent → **withhold the dose**.
- Urine 15 mL/h (<30) explains the accumulation (renal excretion).
- Drowsy with RR 14 → **oxygen**, ask for a senior, **calcium gluconate 1 g IV over 10 min** is indicated if respiratory depression progresses (give it now if RR falls further or SpO₂ drops).
- Send creatinine and magnesium level if available; restart at a lower dose only when reflexes return and urine output improves.

**Worked example: maximum lignocaine**
60 kg woman, 1% lignocaine plain (10 mg/mL). Max 3 mg/kg = **180 mg = 18 mL**. With 2% lignocaine that is only **9 mL**. If more is needed (e.g. a big tear), switch to 1% with adrenaline (max 7 mg/kg) or dilute, or move to theatre.

## Magnesium sulphate: the anticonvulsant

Magnesium sulphate (MgSO₄) is the single most important drug on the Ugandan labour ward after oxytocin. It **prevents** eclampsia in pre-eclampsia with severe features, **treats** eclamptic fits, and **protects the preterm baby's brain** before 32 weeks. It is cheap, on the essential medicines list, and can be given IM at a health centre III. What kills women is not the drug: it is **not giving it**, **giving it without checks**, or **having no calcium gluconate** when toxicity appears.

> [!EXAM]
> MgSO₄ is **not an antihypertensive**. A woman on MgSO₄ with a BP of 170/115 still needs **nifedipine or hydralazine now**. Examiners love this trap.

### Magnesium sulphate (MgSO₄)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Anticonvulsant (electrolyte); calcium antagonist; NMDA-receptor antagonist |
| **Mechanism** | (1) **NMDA (glutamate) receptor antagonist**: blocks the excitatory channel, raising the seizure threshold and limiting excitotoxic neuronal injury. (2) **Physiological calcium antagonist**: competes with Ca²⁺ at voltage-gated channels, relaxing vascular smooth muscle → **cerebral vasodilatation**, reversing the vasospasm and ischaemia behind eclampsia. (3) Reduces **blood-brain-barrier permeability** → less vasogenic cerebral oedema (PRES). (4) At the **neuromuscular junction** it reduces acetylcholine release: this is why **reflexes disappear** and, at higher levels, breathing stops |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Eclampsia** (treatment and prevention of recurrence); **pre-eclampsia with severe features** (prophylaxis, and intrapartum/24 h postpartum per unit protocol); **HELLP**; **fetal neuroprotection** when birth is expected **<32 weeks**. Not recommended as a tocolytic (ineffective) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **Pritchard (standard)**: **4 g of 20% IV over 5–10 min + 10 g of 50% IM** (5 g deep IM each buttock, each with **1 mL of 2% lignocaine**) = **14 g** loading; then **5 g of 50% IM every 4 h**, alternate buttocks. **Zuspan (IV)**: **4 g IV over 5–20 min**, then **1 g/h** infusion. **Recurrent fit**: **2 g of 20% IV over 5 min**. **Neuroprotection**: **4 g IV over 20–30 min**, then **1 g/h** until birth or max 24 h. **Duration** (eclampsia/PE): **24 h after delivery or the last fit, whichever is later** |
| **Onset / key PK** | IV: onset **immediate**. IM: onset about **1 h**, lasts **3–4 h** (hence 4-hourly doses). Distributed in extracellular fluid, crosses the placenta freely (fetal level ≈ maternal). **Excreted almost entirely by the kidneys** (unchanged); half-life about **4 h** with normal renal function, much longer in renal impairment. **Oliguria = accumulation = toxicity** |
| **Side effects** | Warmth and **flushing** during IV loading (expected), nausea/vomiting, headache, sweating, muscle weakness, drowsiness, **diplopia**, slurred speech, **pain/abscess at IM site**, mild hypotension. Toxicity: **areflexia → respiratory depression → cardiac arrest** |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Myasthenia gravis** (can precipitate a myasthenic crisis: absolute). **Heart block** / significant conduction disease. **Renal impairment/oliguria**: reduce or withhold. Caution with **neuromuscular blockers** (potentiates both depolarising and non-depolarising), **aminoglycosides** (gentamicin: additive neuromuscular block), **nifedipine** (theoretical hypotension/neuromuscular block; rare in practice) |
| **Monitoring** | Before **every** IM dose and **hourly** on infusion: **R-R-U**: **RR ≥16/min**, **patellar reflexes present**, **urine ≥30 mL/h (≥100 mL in 4 h)**. Also SpO₂ (keep ≥95%), BP, pulse, level of consciousness; FHR. Serum Mg²⁺ only if available (oliguria, rising creatinine, suspected toxicity) |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Safe and life-saving in pregnancy; compatible with breastfeeding. Neonate may be **hypotonic** or have **respiratory depression**/low Apgar: tell the resuscitator. Prolonged use (>5–7 days, as tocolysis) linked to fetal bone demineralisation: not relevant to a 24-hour course |
| **Antidote/reversal** | **Calcium gluconate 10%, 10 mL (1 g) IV slowly over 10 min**. Keep it **at the bedside** before you start |

**Ward pearls**

- **Label syringes in grams, not mL.** "4 g" of 50% is **8 mL**; "4 g" of 20% is **20 mL**. Mix-ups between grams and millilitres are the classic fatal error.
- The **50% solution is for IM use only**; never push 50% as an IV bolus. IV loading is always given as **20%**, slowly (**not faster than 5 min**: rapid IV injection can cause hypotension, arrhythmia and arrest).
- If she has had a **spinal**, the patellar reflex may be absent from the block: **test the biceps reflex** instead.
- A woman who had her **loading dose at an HC III** 3 hours ago needs her **maintenance** dose, **not a second loading dose**. Always read the referral note for the time and dose given.

#### Preparations and dilution maths

| Preparation | Concentration | What you need to know |
|---|---|---|
| **MgSO₄ 50%** (10 mL ampoule, 5 g) | 50 g per 100 mL = **0.5 g/mL** | **10 mL = 5 g**; 2 mL = 1 g. Use neat for **IM** (with lignocaine) |
| **MgSO₄ 20%** (made on the ward) | 20 g per 100 mL = **0.2 g/mL** | **8 mL of 50% (4 g) + 12 mL water for injection (or NS) = 20 mL of 20%** containing 4 g. Use for **IV** loading and recurrent fits |
| **Recurrent-fit dose** | 2 g of 20% | **4 mL of 50% + 6 mL water = 10 mL of 20%** |

> [!DRUG]
> **Worked example 1: making the IV loading dose.** You need **4 g** as **20%**.
> 1. 50% = 0.5 g/mL, so 4 g ÷ 0.5 g/mL = **8 mL of 50%**.
> 2. 20% = 0.2 g/mL, so 4 g must sit in 4 ÷ 0.2 = **20 mL** total volume.
> 3. Add 20 − 8 = **12 mL of water for injection**. Give the **20 mL over 5–10 min** (about 2–4 mL per minute).
>
> **Worked example 2: Zuspan infusion at 1 g/h.** Put **5 g (10 mL of 50%) in 250 mL NS** → 5 g in about 260 mL ≈ **19 mg/mL**. For 1 g/h you need 1,000 ÷ 19 ≈ **52 mL/h** (most units round to **50 mL/h** on a pump). If urine output falls below 30 mL/h, **halve to 0.5 g/h (≈25 mL/h)**.
>
> **Worked example 3: IM maintenance.** 5 g of 50% = **10 mL**; draw up with **1 mL of 2% lignocaine** (11 mL in total) and give deep IM into the upper outer quadrant of the buttock. Big volume: use a long (21 G, 38 mm) needle and alternate sides.

> [!REDFLAG]
> Never run MgSO₄ by gravity drip "by eye" without a pump or burette: a free-flowing bag can deliver many grams in minutes. If you have no pump, use **Pritchard (IM)**.

#### Regimens at a glance

| Regimen | Loading | Maintenance | Duration | Where |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pritchard (IM)**: Uganda standard | **4 g 20% IV over 5–10 min + 10 g 50% IM** (5 g each buttock) = 14 g | **5 g 50% IM 4-hourly**, alternate buttocks | **24 h after delivery or last fit** (whichever is later) | Any level: needs no pump |
| **Zuspan (IV)** | **4 g IV over 5–20 min** | **1 g/h** IV infusion | Same | Hospitals with pumps and 1:1 monitoring |
| **Recurrent fit** | **2 g of 20% IV over 5 min** (some protocols 4 g if >70 kg) | Continue current regimen | If fits continue, think of another cause (CT head) | All |
| **Fetal neuroprotection (<32 wk)** | **4 g IV over 20–30 min** | **1 g/h** | Until birth or max **24 h** | Hospitals |
| **Pre-referral (HC II/III)** | **Full loading dose** (10 g IM ± 4 g IV if trained) | Send the **next 5 g IM** with her; due **4 h after loading**, even on the road | Continue at the receiving hospital | HC II/III |

> [!UGANDA]
> Uganda (UCG 2023 and the MoH Essential Maternal and Newborn Clinical Care Guidelines) uses **Pritchard** because IM dosing needs no infusion pump and fits the staffing reality: one midwife, many mothers. In practice the commonest failures are **missed 4-hourly doses** and **undocumented R-R-U checks**. As the clerk, own the MgSO₄ chart: write the time the next dose is due in big letters.

#### Monitoring: what to check and what to do

| Check | How and when | Normal | If abnormal: action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Respiratory rate** | Count a full minute; **before each IM dose**, hourly on infusion (every 15–30 min in the first hours after loading in sick women) | **≥16/min** | **RR <16**: **withhold** the dose, call for help, oxygen, check reflexes and SpO₂. **RR <12 or apnoea**: stop MgSO₄, **bag-mask ventilate**, **calcium gluconate 1 g IV over 10 min** |
| **Patellar reflex** | Tap both knees before each dose (biceps if spinal) | **Present** (may be brisk in PE) | **Absent**: **withhold** the dose; this is the **first sign of toxicity** (≈4–5 mmol/L). Recheck hourly; resume only when reflexes return |
| **Urine output** | **Foley catheter + hourly measurement** | **≥30 mL/h** (≥100 mL in 4 h) | **<30 mL/h**: **withhold the next IM dose** (or **halve the infusion to 0.5 g/h**); check creatinine (± serum Mg); strict fluids (80 mL/h); **no big fluid boluses** (pulmonary oedema). Resume when output improves |
| **SpO₂** | Pulse oximeter, continuous if available | **≥95%** | Falling SpO₂: oxygen, check RR/airway, think toxicity **or pulmonary oedema** |
| **Level of consciousness, speech** | With each set of observations | Alert | Drowsiness, slurred speech: early toxicity → check reflexes and RR; also consider stroke/post-ictal state |

#### Toxicity: levels and signs

Toxicity follows a predictable ladder: **reflexes go first, then breathing, then the heart**. Because reflexes disappear well before breathing is affected, a **present reflex is a reliable safety check**.

| Serum Mg²⁺ (mmol/L) | Approx. mg/dL | Clinical picture |
|---|---|---|
| 0.7–1.0 | 1.7–2.4 | Normal (non-pregnant) |
| **2–3.5** | **4.8–8.4** | **Therapeutic** for seizure prophylaxis. Warmth, flushing, nausea common |
| **4–5** | ~10–12 | **Loss of patellar reflexes**; drowsiness, **slurred speech**, **diplopia**, muscle weakness |
| ~5–7.5 | ~12–18 | **ECG changes** (prolonged PR, widened QRS), hypotension, somnolence |
| **6–7.5** | ~15–17 | **Respiratory depression → respiratory paralysis** |
| >7.5 | >18 | **Cardiac conduction defects** (heart block), profound hypotension |
| **>12** | **>30** | **Cardiac arrest** (asystole) |

> [!NOTE]
> Sources quote slightly different bands (RCOG and several UK units give a therapeutic range of about **2–4 mmol/L**; Williams quotes 4.8–8.4 mg/dL, i.e. **2–3.5 mmol/L**). This handbook uses **2–3.5 mmol/L** to match the quick reference. The **order** of signs is what matters clinically, and it is the same in every source. Conversion: **mmol/L × 2.43 = mg/dL**.

**Early symptoms to ask about** (she will tell you before the reflexes go): feeling hot and **flushed**, **nausea**, **slurred speech**, **drowsiness**, **double vision**, muscle weakness ("my legs feel heavy").

#### Step-by-step management of magnesium toxicity

1. **Stop the infusion / withhold the IM dose.** Do not give the next dose.
2. **Call for help** (senior midwife, doctor, anaesthetist).
3. **A-B-C**: open the airway, left lateral tilt if antenatal; **oxygen by face mask**; SpO₂ monitor.
4. If breathing is depressed (RR <12, falling SpO₂) or has stopped: **bag-valve-mask ventilation**, and prepare for intubation.
5. **Calcium gluconate 10%, 10 mL (1 g) IV slowly over 10 min** (it transiently antagonises Mg at the neuromuscular junction and heart). May be repeated if needed. Giving it too fast causes bradycardia and arrhythmia.
6. **Cardiac arrest**: start CPR (with left uterine displacement) and give calcium gluconate as above.
7. **Find the cause**: check **urine output** (oliguria is the usual culprit), **creatinine**, and **serum Mg²⁺** if available. Review the chart for **dosing errors** (double loading dose, 50% given IV, mL/g confusion).
8. **Enhance excretion** (senior decision): with adequate volume status, furosemide can increase renal Mg loss; **dialysis** for toxicity with renal failure at referral centres.
9. **When to restart**: only when **reflexes have returned, RR ≥16 and urine ≥30 mL/h**, usually at a **reduced dose** (e.g. infusion 0.5 g/h, or the next IM dose delayed/halved per unit protocol) with hourly checks.

> [!DRUG]
> **Calcium gluconate 10%**: 10 mL = **1 g** (≈2.2 mmol Ca²⁺). **Dose: 1 g IV over 10 min.** Draw it up and tape it to the MgSO₄ chart or bed before the first dose. Calcium effect is **short-lived**: ventilation, not calcium, is what keeps her alive if she stops breathing.

> [!UGANDA]
> Calcium gluconate is the item most often **missing** when MgSO₄ is on the shelf. At HC III/IV, check stock at the start of every shift and include it in the eclampsia box. If there is none, the life-saver for respiratory depression is still **bag-and-mask ventilation**.

#### Why MgSO₄ beats diazepam and phenytoin

| Trial | Comparison | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Collaborative Eclampsia Trial** (Lancet 1995) | MgSO₄ vs **diazepam** in eclampsia | **52% fewer recurrent fits** with MgSO₄; lower maternal death trend, better neonatal outcomes |
| Same trial | MgSO₄ vs **phenytoin** | **67% fewer recurrent fits** with MgSO₄ |
| **Magpie Trial** (Lancet 2002; ~10,000 women in 33 countries) | MgSO₄ vs placebo in pre-eclampsia | **Halved the risk of eclampsia** (~58% reduction); NNT **~63** in severe pre-eclampsia; no clinically significant harm to the baby |

Diazepam **sedates** and depresses breathing in both mother and neonate, masks the level of consciousness and is a poorer anticonvulsant for eclampsia. Use it **only if MgSO₄ is unavailable** (10 mg IV slowly over 2 min; rectal 20 mg if no IV access).

> [!PEARL]
> **Magnesium for the fetal brain**: given before birth <32 weeks it reduces **cerebral palsy by about one third** (NNT about 63). Give when birth is expected within **24 h**, ideally **within 4 h** of birth, but **never delay an urgent delivery** to give it.

## Antihypertensives in pregnancy

The aim is to **prevent maternal stroke** (the main cause of death in severe pre-eclampsia), not to "cure" pre-eclampsia (only delivery does that). **Treat urgently if BP ≥160/110** (confirm within 15 min; treat within **30–60 min**). **Acute target: 140–150/90–100.** Maintenance target: **~135/85**. **Do not drop the diastolic below 80**: the placenta has no autoregulation, so a rapid fall causes **fetal distress**.

| Situation | First choice in Uganda | Alternatives |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute severe hypertension** | **Nifedipine IR 10 mg oral** or **hydralazine 5 mg IV** | Labetalol IV (rarely available) |
| **Maintenance antenatal** | **Methyldopa** ± **nifedipine MR (retard)** | Labetalol oral |
| **Postpartum** | **Nifedipine MR**, **amlodipine**, **enalapril** | Labetalol; switch methyldopa within 2 days |

### Hydralazine

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Direct arteriolar vasodilator |
| **Mechanism** | Relaxes arteriolar smooth muscle (interferes with intracellular calcium release), lowering peripheral resistance; reflex rise in heart rate and cardiac output |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Acute severe hypertension (≥160/110)** in pregnancy and postpartum |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **5 mg IV slowly over 2–5 min** (10 mg if very high). Check BP every 5 min for 20 min; **repeat 5–10 mg every 20–30 min**. **Max 20 mg IV per episode** (WHO alternative: 12.5 mg IM every 2 h). **Dilution**: 20 mg ampoule (powder) reconstituted and diluted to **20 mL** with water for injection or NS = **1 mg/mL**, so **5 mg = 5 mL** |
| **Onset / key PK** | IV onset **10–20 min**, peak ~20 min, lasts 2–4 h. Hepatic acetylation (slow acetylators get higher levels) |
| **Side effects** | **Reflex tachycardia**, palpitations, **headache** (mimics imminent eclampsia), flushing, nausea, **maternal hypotension → fetal distress** (more often than with nifedipine or labetalol); lupus-like syndrome with long-term use |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Maternal tachycardia >120, SLE; caution with hypovolaemia (e.g. APH) and with other antihypertensives given together (stacking) |
| **Monitoring** | BP and pulse **every 5 min for 20–30 min** after each dose, then every 15–30 min; **FHR** (CTG if available) |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Long safety record in pregnancy; compatible with breastfeeding |
| **Antidote/reversal** | None specific; for overshoot hypotension: left lateral, IV crystalloid 250–500 mL cautiously, stop further doses |

**Ward pearls**

- Always **dilute and give slowly**: bolus injection of the neat ampoule causes precipitous hypotension.
- New **headache after hydralazine** may be the drug, not worsening disease; but still check reflexes, clonus and GCS.
- Don't give the next dose on a timer: **give only if BP is still ≥160/110**.

### Labetalol

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Combined **α₁ and non-selective β** blocker |
| **Mechanism** | α₁ blockade → vasodilatation; β blockade → prevents reflex tachycardia. Preserves uteroplacental flow |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | Acute severe hypertension (IV); maintenance (oral). **First line in the UK (NICE)** |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **IV: 20 mg over 2 min**, then **40, 80, 80 mg every 10 min** if needed; **max 300 mg** (or infusion 20–160 mg/h). **Oral: 100–400 mg 8–12-hourly** (max 2.4 g/day) |
| **Onset / key PK** | IV onset 5 min, peak 10–15 min; oral onset 1–2 h. Hepatic metabolism |
| **Side effects** | Bradycardia, postural hypotension, fatigue, bronchospasm, scalp tingling; neonatal **bradycardia and hypoglycaemia** (tell the paediatrician) |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Asthma** (bronchospasm), **heart failure**, heart block/bradycardia, cocaine use |
| **Monitoring** | BP and pulse every 5–10 min during IV titration; neonatal glucose after birth |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding |
| **Antidote/reversal** | Atropine for bradycardia; (glucagon for severe β-blocker overdose) |

**Ward pearls**

- **Ask about asthma before every labetalol dose**: it is the viva question.
- **Rarely available** in Ugandan public facilities; know it for exams and private hospitals.

### Nifedipine

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Dihydropyridine **calcium-channel blocker** |
| **Mechanism** | Blocks L-type calcium channels in arteriolar smooth muscle (vasodilatation) and **myometrium** (relaxation: hence also a tocolytic) |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Acute severe hypertension** (IR); **maintenance** (MR/retard); **postpartum hypertension**; **tocolysis** (see below) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **Acute: nifedipine IR 10 mg orally (swallowed)**; recheck after **20–30 min**, repeat **10–20 mg** if still ≥160/110; **about 30–40 mg in the first hour**, then MR. **Maintenance: nifedipine MR (retard) 20–40 mg 12-hourly** (max 120 mg/day) |
| **Onset / key PK** | IR onset **10–20 min** orally; MR smooth over 12 h. Hepatic (CYP3A4) |
| **Side effects** | **Headache**, **flushing**, feeling hot, palpitations/tachycardia, dizziness, **ankle oedema**, hypotension |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Hypotension, aortic stenosis, cardiac failure; caution when combined with MgSO₄ (see below) and when it is already being used for BP and someone proposes it for tocolysis |
| **Monitoring** | BP and pulse every 15 min for the first hour after an acute dose; FHR |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding |
| **Antidote/reversal** | Calcium gluconate IV and fluids for severe overdose/hypotension |

**Ward pearls**

- **Never sublingual, never bite or pierce the capsule.** Sublingual/chewed nifedipine gives an **unpredictable, precipitous fall in BP** → placental hypoperfusion, **fetal distress**, maternal MI or stroke. The woman **swallows** the capsule whole.
- The **"sensation"** after a dose (hot, flushed, throbbing headache) is expected: warn her so she does not panic, and do not confuse it with worsening pre-eclampsia (still check reflexes).
- **Nifedipine + MgSO₄**: an old fear of profound hypotension and neuromuscular blockade comes from case reports; a large cohort study (Magee 2005) found no meaningful increase. The combination is **standard in Uganda**: give both, and **monitor**.
- **Most available** acute agent in Uganda (on the shelf in ~93% of HC III+ facilities) and needs **no IV access**: ideal at HC III before referral.

### Methyldopa

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Centrally acting **α₂-agonist** |
| **Mechanism** | Converted to α-methylnoradrenaline, which stimulates central α₂ receptors → reduced sympathetic outflow |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Maintenance** treatment of chronic or gestational hypertension/pre-eclampsia **antenatally** |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **250 mg orally 8-hourly**, increase to **500–750 mg 8-hourly** (**max 3 g/day**) |
| **Onset / key PK** | **Slow: onset 4–6 h**, full effect in 24–48 h. **Useless for acute control** |
| **Side effects** | **Sedation**, tiredness, **depression**, dry mouth, postural hypotension, nasal stuffiness; **positive direct Coombs test** (up to ~20%), rarely **haemolytic anaemia**; drug-induced **hepatitis** |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **History of depression**, active liver disease, phaeochromocytoma; caution in the postpartum period |
| **Monitoring** | BP; mood; LFTs if symptoms; a positive Coombs can complicate blood cross-matching (tell the lab) |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | **Longest safety record in pregnancy** (child follow-up data); passes into breast milk in small amounts but **stop/switch within 2 days postpartum** because of **postnatal depression** |
| **Antidote/reversal** | None |

**Ward pearls**

- **Never give methyldopa for BP ≥160/110.** Give nifedipine IR or hydralazine first, then start methyldopa for maintenance.
- On the postnatal ward, switch to **nifedipine MR, amlodipine or enalapril**. Examiners ask "why?": **depression**.

### Enalapril (postpartum only)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | ACE inhibitor |
| **Mechanism** | Blocks conversion of angiotensin I to II → vasodilatation, less aldosterone |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Postpartum** hypertension (especially with proteinuria, diabetes or cardiomyopathy) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **5–20 mg orally once or twice daily** |
| **Onset / key PK** | Onset ~1 h, peak 4–6 h; renally excreted (active metabolite enalaprilat) |
| **Side effects** | Dry cough, first-dose hypotension, **hyperkalaemia**, **renal impairment**, angio-oedema |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Contraindicated antenatally**; AKI, hyperkalaemia, bilateral renal artery stenosis |
| **Monitoring** | **Creatinine and potassium** within about 1 week; BP |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | **Pregnancy: avoid** (ACE-inhibitor fetopathy). **Breastfeeding: safe** (preferred ACE inhibitor, with captopril) |
| **Antidote/reversal** | None; stop and give fluids for hypotension |

> [!REDFLAG]
> **Drugs to AVOID in pregnancy**
> - **ACE inhibitors and ARBs** (enalapril, captopril, lisinopril, losartan): **2nd/3rd-trimester fetopathy**: fetal renal failure, **oligohydramnios**, **skull hypoplasia**, limb contractures, pulmonary hypoplasia, FGR, stillbirth. If a woman conceives on one, **switch as soon as pregnancy is known** (early exposure is not an indication for termination).
> - **Atenolol**: associated with **fetal growth restriction** (especially when started early).
> - **Diuretics** (furosemide, thiazides): reduce the already contracted plasma volume in pre-eclampsia; use only for **pulmonary oedema**.
> - **Ergometrine** in any hypertensive woman: severe hypertension and stroke. Use oxytocin.

### Low-dose aspirin (prevention)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Antiplatelet (irreversible COX-1 inhibitor) |
| **Mechanism** | At low dose, selectively suppresses platelet **thromboxane A₂** while sparing prostacyclin → improves placentation/reduces placental thrombosis |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Prevention of pre-eclampsia** in women at high risk: **one high-risk factor** (previous pre-eclampsia, chronic hypertension, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, SLE/antiphospholipid syndrome, multiple pregnancy) or **two or more moderate** factors (first pregnancy, age ≥40, BMI ≥35, pregnancy interval >10 years, family history of pre-eclampsia) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **75–150 mg orally at night** (Uganda usually has 75 mg tablets) |
| **Onset / key PK** | Start **from 12 weeks**, ideally **before 16 weeks** (benefit is small if started after 20 weeks); continue to **36 weeks** |
| **Side effects** | Dyspepsia; minor bleeding |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Aspirin allergy/asthma triggered by NSAIDs, active peptic ulcer, bleeding disorders |
| **Monitoring** | Adherence (the most common failure) |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Low dose is safe in pregnancy |
| **Antidote/reversal** | None needed at this dose |

**Ward pearls**

- **Stop at 36 weeks** (to reduce bleeding risk around delivery and neuraxial anaesthesia).
- Every woman who has had pre-eclampsia should leave the postnatal ward knowing: **"aspirin from 12 weeks next time."**

### Calcium supplementation

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Mineral supplement |
| **Mechanism** | Low calcium intake raises parathyroid hormone and intracellular calcium in vascular smooth muscle → vasoconstriction; supplementation reduces this |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Prevention of pre-eclampsia** in populations with **low dietary calcium** (most of Uganda); WHO ANC 2016 recommendation |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **1.5–2 g elemental calcium daily**, orally, in divided doses, **from 20 weeks** until delivery |
| **Onset / key PK** | Absorption reduced by iron and tea |
| **Side effects** | Constipation, bloating |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Hypercalcaemia, renal stones |
| **Monitoring** | Adherence; tablet burden |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Safe |
| **Antidote/reversal** | Not applicable |

**Ward pearl**: take calcium and iron **at least 2 hours apart** (calcium blocks iron absorption); a practical schedule is iron in the morning and calcium at lunch and supper.

## Tocolytics and antenatal corticosteroids

In threatened preterm labour we **cannot reliably stop labour**, and tocolysis on its own does **not** improve newborn outcomes. Its only job is to **buy 48 hours** to complete **antenatal corticosteroids (ACS)** and/or **transfer the mother in utero** to a unit that can care for the baby.

> [!REDFLAG]
> **Contraindications to tocolysis**: **chorioamnionitis**/maternal infection; **antepartum haemorrhage** (abruption); **severe pre-eclampsia/eclampsia**; **fetal distress**; **intrauterine fetal death** or lethal anomaly; **GA ≥34 weeks**; advanced labour (e.g. cervix ≥4–6 cm per protocol); maternal cardiac disease or hypotension; severe FGR. **PPROM** is a relative contraindication (brief use for steroids/transfer only if no infection).

### Nifedipine (as a tocolytic)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Calcium-channel blocker |
| **Mechanism** | Blocks calcium entry into myometrial cells → uterine relaxation |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | Threatened preterm labour **24–34 weeks** to allow ACS or transfer (**WHO 2022**: the only tocolytic recommended, conditionally) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **20 mg orally (loading)**, then **10–20 mg every 6–8 h** for up to **48 h** |
| **Onset / key PK** | Onset 20–30 min; no benefit from maintenance tocolysis beyond 48 h |
| **Side effects** | Headache, flushing, **hypotension**, tachycardia, dizziness |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | As above; **maternal BP <90/50**; cardiac disease; caution if she is **already on nifedipine for hypertension** (senior input) |
| **Monitoring** | **BP and pulse before each dose (withhold if BP <90/50)**; contractions; FHR |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Safe |
| **Antidote/reversal** | Fluids; calcium gluconate for severe overdose |

**Ward pearl**: at HC II/III, give **dexamethasone 6 mg IM + nifedipine 20 mg** and refer **the mother with the baby inside her**: the uterus is the best transport incubator.

### Indomethacin

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | NSAID (COX inhibitor) |
| **Mechanism** | Inhibits prostaglandin synthesis → reduces myometrial contractility |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | Specialist use in preterm labour **<32 weeks** (also polyhydramnios) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **50–100 mg loading** (oral or rectal), then **25 mg 6-hourly**, **max 48 h** |
| **Onset / key PK** | Crosses the placenta |
| **Side effects** | Maternal: dyspepsia, GI bleeding, platelet dysfunction. **Fetal: premature constriction/closure of the ductus arteriosus** (pulmonary hypertension), **oligohydramnios** (reduced fetal urine), neonatal NEC and IVH with prolonged use |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **≥32 weeks**, >48 h use, oligohydramnios, peptic ulcer, asthma, renal impairment, bleeding disorders |
| **Monitoring** | Liquor volume (and ductal Doppler) if used >48 h |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Avoid after 32 weeks |
| **Antidote/reversal** | Stop the drug |

**Ward pearl**: the numbers are **"32 and 48"**: never after **32 weeks**, never beyond **48 hours**.

### Salbutamol / terbutaline (β₂-agonists)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | β₂-adrenergic agonists (betamimetics) |
| **Mechanism** | Raise cAMP in myometrium → relaxation |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **No longer recommended** for tocolysis (WHO 2022). Terbutaline 250 mcg SC is still used in some units for **acute tocolysis during intrauterine resuscitation** (e.g. uterine hyperstimulation with fetal distress) while preparing for CS |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | Not recommended for preterm labour. (Acute uterine relaxation: terbutaline 250 mcg SC once, per unit protocol) |
| **Onset / key PK** | Rapid; systemic β₁/β₂ effects |
| **Side effects** | **Maternal tachycardia**, palpitations, tremor, **hypokalaemia**, **hyperglycaemia**, myocardial ischaemia, **pulmonary oedema** (especially with IV fluids, steroids, twins), maternal deaths reported; fetal tachycardia, neonatal hypoglycaemia |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Cardiac disease**, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, APH, multiple pregnancy |
| **Monitoring** | Pulse (stop if >120), fluid balance, glucose, potassium, chest |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Inhaled salbutamol for **asthma** is completely safe: the problem is tocolytic IV doses |
| **Antidote/reversal** | Stop; β-blocker for severe tachyarrhythmia (specialist) |

**Ward pearl**: why not salbutamol? **More side effects, no better outcomes**: they buy time no better than nifedipine and can cause **pulmonary oedema**.

### Atosiban

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | **Oxytocin-receptor antagonist** |
| **Mechanism** | Competitively blocks myometrial oxytocin (and vasopressin V1a) receptors |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | Preterm labour 24–33+6 weeks (Europe) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **6.75 mg IV bolus over 1 min**, then **18 mg/h for 3 h**, then **6 mg/h** for up to 45 h (max 48 h) |
| **Onset / key PK** | Rapid; IV only |
| **Side effects** | Few: nausea, headache, injection-site reaction |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | As for all tocolytics |
| **Monitoring** | Contractions, FHR |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Safe profile |
| **Antidote/reversal** | None |

> [!UGANDA]
> **Atosiban is expensive and not available in Ugandan public facilities.** WHO 2022 does not recommend it outside research. **Nifedipine is the Ugandan tocolytic.** MgSO₄ is **not** a tocolytic: give it for neuroprotection, not to stop contractions.

### Dexamethasone and betamethasone (antenatal corticosteroids)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Fluorinated glucocorticoids (cross the placenta; not inactivated by placental 11β-HSD2) |
| **Mechanism** | Induce type II pneumocytes to make **surfactant**, mature lung structure, and mature other organs (brain germinal matrix, gut, cardiovascular) |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | Preterm birth likely within **7 days** at **24–34 weeks** (WHO 2022; **Uganda usually 28–34**, lower at NICU centres), with **accurate GA**, **no maternal infection**, and **adequate childbirth and newborn care** available. Also before planned preterm delivery (e.g. severe pre-eclampsia, APH) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **Dexamethasone 6 mg IM 12-hourly × 4 doses** (24 mg; first choice in Uganda) **or betamethasone 12 mg IM 24-hourly × 2 doses** (24 mg) |
| **Onset / key PK** | Benefit begins within hours; **maximal if birth occurs 24 h to 7 days** after the first dose. Start even if birth may occur before the course is complete |
| **Side effects** | Maternal **hyperglycaemia** (up to 5 days), **WBC rise for ~72 h** (confuses sepsis assessment), transient reduced fetal movements/FHR variability (1–3 days); **neonatal hypoglycaemia**; possible harm if the baby is actually term or the mother is infected |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Systemic maternal infection** (including chorioamnionitis, TB), uncertain dates suggesting ≥34 weeks; **diabetes** (monitor and adjust insulin) |
| **Monitoring** | **Capillary glucose 4-hourly** in diabetic women for 3–5 days (increase insulin); temperature (infection); document dose times |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Safe in pregnancy at this dose; compatible with breastfeeding |
| **Antidote/reversal** | Not applicable |

**Benefits** (Cochrane): reduced **neonatal death** (~30%), **respiratory distress syndrome** (~35%), **intraventricular haemorrhage** (~45%) and **necrotising enterocolitis**.

**Ward pearls**

- **One repeat ("rescue") course only**: if birth has not occurred **≥7 days** after the first course and she is again at high risk of birth within 7 days, still **<34 weeks** (WHO 2022). Never give multiple repeated courses (reduced fetal growth).
- **Not routine at ≥34 weeks** in Uganda (the ACT trial showed **harm** where dating and newborn care were poor; ACTION-I showed benefit when the WHO conditions are met).
- A raised **WBC after steroids** is expected for 72 hours: decide chorioamnionitis on **clinical signs** (fever, tachycardia, tender uterus, offensive liquor).

> [!DRUG]
> **Worked example 4: drawing up dexamethasone.** Ugandan dexamethasone ampoules are commonly **4 mg/mL** (check: some are 5 mg/mL). For **6 mg**: 6 ÷ 4 = **1.5 mL IM**. Four doses 12 hours apart: e.g. 08:00, 20:00, 08:00, 20:00. Write the dose times on the referral note so the next unit does not restart the course.

> [!EXAM]
> **Viva triad for threatened preterm labour at 30 weeks**: **"Steroids, magnesium, and move her"**: dexamethasone 6 mg IM 12-hourly × 4; MgSO₄ 4 g IV then 1 g/h if birth expected <32 weeks within 24 h; nifedipine 20 mg to buy 48 h for **in-utero transfer**; plus erythromycin if PPROM and GBS prophylaxis in labour.

## Uterotonics: oxytocin, ergometrine, prostaglandins

Uterotonics make the uterus contract. You use them for three different jobs, and the **dose depends on the job**:

1. **Making labour happen** (induction, augmentation): tiny, carefully titrated doses, because too much kills the baby (hyperstimulation) or ruptures the uterus.
2. **Stopping or preventing bleeding after birth** (AMTSL, PPH): big doses, because you want a hard, sustained contraction and the baby is already out.
3. **Emptying the uterus in early pregnancy** (miscarriage, IUFD, abortion care): prostaglandins, because the early uterus has **few oxytocin receptors**.

> [!PEARL]
> **The uterus becomes more sensitive as pregnancy advances.** Oxytocin receptors in the myometrium rise from about 20 weeks and are **about 100–200-fold higher by early labour** than in the non-pregnant uterus; prostaglandin sensitivity rises too. So: oxytocin is weak in the first trimester, and a misoprostol dose that is fine at 16 weeks (400 µg) can **rupture the uterus at 36 weeks**. Doses go **down** as gestation goes **up**.

### Oxytocin

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Posterior pituitary nonapeptide hormone (synthetic; Syntocinon/Pitocin). Differs from ADH (vasopressin) by only **2 amino acids** |
| **Mechanism** | Binds the **Gq-coupled oxytocin receptor** on myometrium → phospholipase C → IP₃ → **intracellular Ca²⁺ release** → rhythmic contractions. Also makes the decidua release **prostaglandins**. Receptor density **rises steeply near term** (weak drug before ~20 weeks). Also causes milk let-down (myoepithelial cells) |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | AMTSL (first choice); treatment of PPH; induction of labour (after ARM or a ripe cervix); augmentation of slow labour; second twin with poor contractions; after evacuation/expulsion of later pregnancies; at caesarean section after delivery of the baby |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **AMTSL: 10 IU IM** (or IV slowly) **within 1 min of birth**. **PPH: 10 IU IV slowly (or IM), then 20–40 IU in 1 L NS/RL** (e.g. **20 IU in 1 L at 60 drops/min**, maintenance **40 drops/min**). **Augmentation/induction (WHO MCPC): 2.5 IU in 500 mL NS/RL at 10 drops/min, ↑ by 10 drops/min every 30 min to max 60 drops/min**; if still inadequate, 5 IU in 500 mL at 30 drops/min, ↑ to 60 drops/min (see worked maths below) |
| **Onset / PK** | IV: **within 1 min**; IM: **2–3 min**, lasts 30–60 min. **Half-life 3–5 min** → a bolus wears off quickly (hence the infusion) and **stopping the drip reverses hyperstimulation within minutes**. Steady state takes ~30–40 min → **increase no faster than every 30 min** |
| **Side effects** | **Tachysystole / hyperstimulation** → fetal distress; **uterine rupture** (grand multipara, scar, obstruction); **water intoxication / hyponatraemia** (antidiuretic effect at high dose with large volumes); **hypotension, tachycardia, flushing** with rapid IV bolus (can cause myocardial ischaemia); nausea; atony PPH after prolonged augmentation (receptor desensitisation) |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **CI:** obstructed labour / CPD, malpresentation (transverse lie), **fetal distress**, hypertonic uterus, placenta praevia, **previous classical CS** or myomectomy entering the cavity. **Caution:** **grand multipara (≥5)**, **previous lower-segment CS**, overdistended uterus (twins, polyhydramnios), severe heart disease (avoid boluses) |
| **Monitoring** | In labour: **contractions every 30 min** (number in 10 min, duration), **FHR every 15–30 min** (every 5 min in second stage), maternal pulse, BP; record **IU/L and drops/min** on the partograph/LCG every 30 min; fluid balance. In PPH: tone, loss, vitals every 15 min |
| **Pregnancy / breastfeeding** | Physiological hormone; safe in breastfeeding (it *is* the let-down hormone) |
| **Storage / antidote** | **Refrigerate 2–8 °C**, protect from light; loses potency in heat (cold-chain failures → substandard ampoules). No antidote: **stop the infusion** (short half-life); **tocolytic** (terbutaline/salbutamol) for persisting hyperstimulation |

**Ward pearls**
- **Oxytocin is only as safe as the person counting the contractions.** One-to-one care, a partograph/LCG, and a documented rate every 30 min. Never leave an oxytocin drip running unattended at night.
- **Never augment a woman who might be obstructed.** Poor progress in a multipara is obstruction until proven otherwise (moulding 3+, caput, Bandl's ring, haematuria). Oxytocin + obstruction = rupture.
- **Oxytocin before ergometrine or misoprostol** in PPH: first line, cheapest, fewest side effects. But after **many hours of augmentation** the receptors are desensitised, so add a drug from a **different class** early.
- **Uterine inversion: stop oxytocin until the uterus is replaced**, then give it.

#### Oxytocin toxicity: what to recognise and what to do

| Toxicity | Why it happens | Recognise | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tachysystole / hyperstimulation** | Dose too high, increased too fast, very sensitive uterus (multipara, after misoprostol) | **>5 contractions in 10 min**, contractions **lasting >60 s**, poor relaxation between, then **FHR <110, late or prolonged decelerations**, meconium | 1. **Stop the oxytocin immediately** (disconnect, don't just slow). 2. **Left lateral position** (relieves aortocaval compression). 3. **IV fluid** (crystalloid, not dextrose) and **oxygen** if hypoxic/per local protocol. 4. Call a senior; listen to FHR continuously. 5. If contractions/fetal distress persist: **tocolysis: terbutaline 250 µg (0.25 mg) SC** (or IV slowly over 5 min), or **salbutamol 10 mg in 1 L IV fluid at 10 drops/min** (WHO MCPC). 6. If FHR does not recover → **deliver** (CS or assisted delivery if fully dilated). 7. Restart only after senior review, at half the previous rate |
| **Water intoxication / hyponatraemia** | Oxytocin acts on **V2 (ADH) receptors** at high doses (≥20–40 mIU/min) for many hours, especially in **large volumes of 5% dextrose** (free water) | Headache, nausea, vomiting, confusion, drowsiness, **convulsions** (mimics eclampsia!), low urine output, oedema; **Na⁺ <130 mmol/L**; neonatal hyponatraemia too | **Stop oxytocin**, **restrict fluids**, check **U&E**, stop dextrose; treat seizures; senior/ICU for severe hyponatraemia. **Prevention: use NS or RL, not 5% dextrose, and limit oxytocin-containing fluid to about 3 L** |
| **Hypotension** | **Rapid IV bolus** causes vasodilatation, reflex tachycardia (and ECG ischaemia) | Sudden fall in BP, flushing, chest tightness, especially at **caesarean section under spinal** or in a **hypovolaemic** or **cardiac** patient | **Never push IV oxytocin fast**: give **IV slowly over 1–2 min** (or IM). In cardiac disease, use a slow infusion rather than a bolus |
| **Uterine rupture** | Oxytocin against an obstruction or scar; grand multipara | Sudden pain between contractions, contractions stop, scar tenderness, FHR abnormality/loss, fetal parts easily felt, shock, vaginal bleeding | Stop oxytocin, resuscitate, **laparotomy** (see obstructed labour) |

> [!REDFLAG]
> **A labouring woman on oxytocin who fits is not automatically eclamptic.** Check BP and urine protein, but also think **water intoxication** (low Na⁺, many litres of dextrose) and treat accordingly.

#### Worked oxytocin drip-rate calculation (20 drops/mL giving set)

Three formulas you must know:

```
mL/min       = drops/min ÷ 20            (standard adult set = 20 drops/mL)
drops/min    = volume (mL) × 20 ÷ time (min)
mIU/min      = concentration (mIU/mL) × mL/min
concentration (mIU/mL) = IU in bag × 1,000 ÷ bag volume (mL)
```

**Example 1: augmentation starting dose.** 2.5 IU in 500 mL = 2,500 mIU ÷ 500 mL = **5 mIU/mL**. At **10 drops/min** → 10 ÷ 20 = 0.5 mL/min → 5 × 0.5 = **2.5 mIU/min**. Each step up of 10 drops/min adds 2.5 mIU/min, so the maximum **60 drops/min = 3 mL/min = 15 mIU/min**.

**Example 2: second concentration.** 5 IU in 500 mL = **10 mIU/mL**. Why restart at **30 drops/min**? 30 ÷ 20 = 1.5 mL/min × 10 = **15 mIU/min**: exactly the dose she was already on at 60 drops/min of the weaker bag. At **60 drops/min** = 3 mL/min × 10 = **30 mIU/min**.

**Example 3: PPH infusion.** 20 IU in 1 L = **20 mIU/mL**. At **60 drops/min** = 3 mL/min × 20 = **60 mIU/min** (a big dose, deliberately), and 3 mL/min = 180 mL/h, so the litre lasts **about 5.5 hours**.

**Example 4: a nurse asks "the doctor wrote 500 mL over 4 hours; what rate?"** 500 × 20 ÷ 240 min = **42 drops/min**.

**If an infusion pump is available:** mL/h = mIU/min × 60 ÷ mIU/mL. For 2.5 mIU/min using 10 mIU/mL: 2.5 × 60 ÷ 10 = **15 mL/h**.

> [!EXAM]
> Viva favourite: "What dose in mIU/min is 2.5 IU in 500 mL at 10 drops/min?" **2.5 mIU/min.** Then: "and at 60 drops/min?" **15 mIU/min.** Check the drop factor on the giving set: a **paediatric burette is 60 drops/mL**, which changes every answer by a factor of 3.

> [!UGANDA]
> Many Ugandan wards start with **5 IU in 500 mL** rather than 2.5 IU. Know your unit's protocol, **write concentration and drops/min on the partograph**, and remember that the **WHO MCPC 10 IU in 500 mL step is for primigravidae only**. Multiparas and women with a previous CS who fail at the 5 IU concentration go to **caesarean section**, not to a stronger drip.

### Ergometrine and Syntometrine

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Ergot alkaloid (ergonovine); **Syntometrine** = **oxytocin 5 IU + ergometrine 0.5 mg per 1 mL** ampoule |
| **Mechanism** | Partial agonist at **α-adrenergic, serotonin (5-HT₂) and dopamine** receptors → **sustained, tetanic** contraction of the whole uterus including the lower segment (unlike oxytocin's rhythmic contractions); also **vasoconstriction** (the cause of its dangers) |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | Second-line treatment of PPH from atony; AMTSL where oxytocin unavailable (only if normotensive); heavy bleeding from incomplete abortion while awaiting evacuation |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **0.2 mg IM** (or **slow IV** over at least 1 min); **repeat 0.2 mg IM after 15 min**, then **0.2 mg every 4 h** if needed. **Max 5 doses (1 mg) in 24 h**. Syntometrine: **1 mL IM** (not IV) |
| **Onset / PK** | IM **2–7 min**, IV **~1 min**; action lasts **~3 h** (much longer than oxytocin). Syntometrine combines oxytocin's fast onset with ergometrine's long action |
| **Side effects** | **Nausea and vomiting** (common), **hypertension** (can precipitate **stroke, eclampsia**, pulmonary oedema), headache, chest pain/coronary spasm, peripheral vasoconstriction; ergotism with interacting drugs |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Hypertension, pre-eclampsia/eclampsia, cardiac disease** (including rheumatic valve disease), **retained placenta**, **undelivered second twin** (never before delivery of the last baby), peripheral vascular disease/Raynaud's, severe liver or renal disease, sepsis. **Interaction:** strong CYP3A4 inhibitors, especially **ritonavir/cobicistat-boosted protease inhibitors**, clarithromycin/erythromycin, azole antifungals → ergotism (severe vasospasm) |
| **Monitoring** | **BP before every dose** and after; uterine tone; vomiting (aspiration risk if drowsy) |
| **Pregnancy / breastfeeding** | **Never before birth of the (last) baby.** Single doses fine in breastfeeding; repeated doses may reduce prolactin and milk supply |
| **Storage / antidote** | **Refrigerate 2–8 °C, protect from light** (degrades quickly in heat). No antidote; treat hypertension (e.g. hydralazine/nifedipine) and vasospasm |

**Ward pearls**
- **Check the BP before you reach for ergometrine.** "Ergometrine: not for the hypertensive heart."
- **Retained placenta → never ergometrine**: the tetanic contraction closes the cervix and **traps the placenta**. Give oxytocin, empty the bladder, CCT, then manual removal.
- **HIV on a boosted protease inhibitor** (second-line ART such as ATV/r, LPV/r, DRV/r): avoid ergometrine; use oxytocin/misoprostol. On TLD (dolutegravir) there is no such interaction.
- Syntometrine gives slightly less PPH than oxytocin alone but **much more vomiting and hypertension**, which is why oxytocin alone is preferred for AMTSL.

### Carboprost (15-methyl PGF2α)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Prostaglandin F2α analogue (Hemabate) |
| **Mechanism** | Stimulates **FP prostaglandin receptors** → strong myometrial contraction; also smooth muscle of **bronchi** and **gut** (hence the side effects) |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **PPH from atony refractory to oxytocin** (± ergometrine/misoprostol) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **250 µg (0.25 mg) deep IM**, repeat **every 15 min**; **max 8 doses (2 mg)**. Intramyometrial injection only by a specialist |
| **Onset / PK** | Onset within ~15 min, peak effect 15–60 min |
| **Side effects** | **Diarrhoea, vomiting**, fever, flushing, **bronchospasm**, hypertension, **desaturation** (ventilation–perfusion mismatch) |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Asthma** (absolute: bronchospasm), active cardiac, pulmonary, renal or hepatic disease; caution in hypertension, glaucoma |
| **Monitoring** | SpO₂, RR and wheeze; BP; uterine tone |
| **Pregnancy / breastfeeding** | Used only after birth; breastfeeding acceptable |
| **Storage / antidote** | **Refrigerate 2–8 °C**. Bronchospasm: salbutamol nebuliser, oxygen |

**Ward pearls**
- "**Carboprost: not for the wheezy chest.**"
- **Expensive and rarely stocked in Ugandan public facilities**: in practice, second-line after oxytocin is **misoprostol 800 µg SL** (± ergometrine if normotensive), then tamponade, NASG and surgery. Don't waste time looking for carboprost.

### Misoprostol

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Synthetic **prostaglandin E1 (PGE1) analogue** (originally for NSAID ulcers). Tablets of **200 µg**; 25 µg vaginal tablets in some places |
| **Mechanism** | EP-receptor agonist → **myometrial contraction** and **cervical ripening** (collagen breakdown, softening) |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | PPH prevention (where no oxytocin) and treatment; induction of labour (live fetus, **unscarred uterus**); IUFD; missed and incomplete miscarriage; abortion care where legal; cervical priming before MVA/surgery |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **Depends on indication and gestation: see the table below.** Never write "miso 1 tab": write **µg, route, interval** |
| **Onset / PK** | **Sublingual**: fastest and highest peak (~30 min), good for PPH. **Oral**: peak ~30 min, shorter action (hence 2-hourly for IOL). **Vaginal**: slower peak (1–2 h) but **most sustained** (hence 6-hourly). Buccal: between |
| **Side effects** | **Shivering and fever** (up to 40 °C, commonest with 800 µg SL; not sepsis), **diarrhoea**, nausea, vomiting, cramps; **tachysystole** and **uterine rupture** (dose-related, worse with a scar); teratogenic if pregnancy continues (Möbius syndrome, limb defects) |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Previous CS / uterine scar: avoid for induction in the third trimester**; lower doses and caution in the second trimester. Allergy to prostaglandins. Suspected ectopic (misoprostol does not treat it). Caution in severe asthma is **not** needed (PGE1 is not a bronchoconstrictor) |
| **Monitoring** | Contractions and FHR (IOL); temperature (fever expected, but look for sepsis if >24 h); bleeding and expulsion; **wait ≥4 h after the last dose before starting oxytocin** |
| **Pregnancy / breastfeeding** | Abortifacient: never in a wanted ongoing pregnancy. Compatible with breastfeeding |
| **Storage / antidote** | **Heat-stable** at room temperature (in blister pack, keep dry): ideal for Uganda. Hyperstimulation → remove vaginal tablets if possible, left lateral, **terbutaline 250 µg SC** |

#### Misoprostol regimens by indication (agree with the Quick Reference)

| Indication | Gestation | Regimen |
|---|---|---|
| **PPH prevention** (no oxytocin, e.g. home birth) | After birth | **600 µg orally** once |
| **PPH treatment** | After birth | **800 µg sublingually** once |
| **Induction of labour, live fetus** | Term (≥37 weeks), **no uterine scar** | **25 µg vaginally every 6 h** or **25 µg orally every 2 h** (oral solution: **1 × 200 µg tablet in 200 mL water, give 25 mL**; discard after 24 h) |
| **Incomplete miscarriage/abortion** | <13 weeks (uterus ≤12–13 weeks) | **600 µg orally** once or **400 µg sublingually** once |
| **Missed miscarriage** | <14 weeks | **Mifepristone 200 mg orally, then misoprostol 800 µg vaginally/SL/buccally 1–2 days later**; misoprostol alone: **800 µg vaginally** (or 600 µg SL), repeat after 3 h if needed (max 2 doses) |
| **Cervical priming before MVA / surgical evacuation** | <13 weeks | **400 µg sublingually 1 h** or **vaginally 3 h** before |
| **Incomplete abortion / termination (legal indication)** | 13–24 weeks | **400 µg vaginally, SL or buccally every 3 h** until expulsion |
| **IUFD** | **14–28 weeks** | **400 µg vaginally/SL every 4–6 h** (WHO 2022, ideally after mifepristone 200 mg) or **200 µg every 4–6 h** (FIGO); **100 µg every 4 h at 27–28 weeks** |
| **IUFD** | **>28 weeks** | **25 µg vaginally every 6 h** or **25 µg orally every 2 h**; if no response after 2 doses, 50 µg every 6 h; **never more than 50 µg per dose** |

> [!DRUG]
> **The dose ladder to memorise:** first trimester **400–800 µg** → mid-trimester **200–400 µg** → 27–28 weeks **100 µg** → third trimester/term **25 µg** (max 50 µg). **After birth it jumps back up** to 600 µg (prevention) and 800 µg (treatment), because now you *want* a maximal contraction. **Never 200 µg to a woman in the third trimester with a baby still inside.**

**Ward pearls**
- **Post-misoprostol fever** is expected within hours. A fever **persisting beyond 24 h**, with foul discharge or tenderness, is **sepsis** (septic abortion, endometritis) until proven otherwise.
- Misoprostol is the **Ugandan workhorse**: cheap, heat-stable, on the shelf at HC III. That is exactly why dosing errors are common: check the gestation every time.
- In PPH, misoprostol is **slower** than IV oxytocin; give it **with** the oxytocin infusion, not instead of it (unless no oxytocin). If she already had misoprostol 600 µg for prophylaxis, be wary of high-dose repeats (hyperpyrexia) and prefer another class.

> [!REDFLAG]
> **Previous caesarean at term: no misoprostol for induction** (WHO; rupture risk several-fold higher). Use a **Foley catheter** then **oxytocin** with senior supervision, in a hospital with theatre, or plan a repeat CS.

### Carbetocin (heat-stable)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Long-acting synthetic **oxytocin analogue** |
| **Mechanism** | Oxytocin-receptor agonist; **half-life ~40 min** (about 10× longer than oxytocin) → sustained contraction from one dose |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Prevention of PPH only** (AMTSL, including at CS) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **100 µg IM or slow IV, single dose**, after delivery of the baby |
| **Onset / PK** | Onset ~2 min; effect lasts ~1–2 h |
| **Side effects** | As oxytocin: nausea, flushing, headache, hypotension with fast IV, tachycardia |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Not for treatment of PPH, not for induction or augmentation, never before birth of the baby.** Caution in cardiac disease, epilepsy, hyponatraemia risk |
| **Monitoring** | Uterine tone every 15 min for 2 h; BP |
| **Pregnancy / breastfeeding** | Postpartum use only; compatible with breastfeeding |
| **Storage / antidote** | **Heat-stable formulation: no cold chain needed** (the whole point). No antidote |

**Ward pearls**
- **CHAMPION trial** (WHO, 2018; ~30,000 women in 10 countries including Uganda): heat-stable carbetocin was **non-inferior** to oxytocin for preventing PPH ≥500 mL.
- WHO recommends it where **oxytocin quality cannot be guaranteed** (cold chain). Available through some programmes in Uganda; oxytocin 10 IU IM remains the standard.

### Dinoprostone (PGE2)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Natural **prostaglandin E2** |
| **Mechanism** | Cervical ripening (collagen remodelling) and myometrial contraction |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | Cervical ripening / induction of labour with an unfavourable cervix |
| **Dose & route** | **Vaginal gel 1–2 mg**, repeat after 6 h (max per product), or **controlled-release pessary 10 mg** left for up to 24 h (removable) |
| **Onset / PK** | Onset within 1 h; pessary releases slowly and **can be pulled out** if hyperstimulation occurs (advantage over misoprostol) |
| **Side effects** | Tachysystole, fetal distress, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, fever |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Previous CS (UK allows with caution; generally avoided in Uganda), grand multiparity, placenta praevia, malpresentation, fetal distress, active asthma (caution) |
| **Monitoring** | CTG/FHR and contractions; wait ~6 h after gel (30 min after pessary removal) before oxytocin |
| **Pregnancy / breastfeeding** | Used only to induce labour |
| **Storage / antidote** | **Refrigerate**. Remove pessary; terbutaline 250 µg SC for hyperstimulation |

> [!UGANDA]
> **Dinoprostone is expensive, needs a fridge, and is rarely available in Ugandan public hospitals** (mainly private units). In Uganda, cervical ripening means **misoprostol** or a **Foley catheter**. Know dinoprostone for exams comparing UK (NICE) and WHO practice.

### Comparison of uterotonics

| Drug | Class | Prevention (AMTSL) | PPH treatment | IOL / ripening | Early pregnancy | Key contraindication | Storage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Oxytocin** | Peptide hormone | **10 IU IM** | **10 IU IV slow + 20–40 IU/L infusion** | Yes (after ripening/ARM), titrated | Weak (<20 weeks) | Obstruction, fetal distress, classical scar | **2–8 °C** |
| **Carbetocin** | Long-acting oxytocin analogue | **100 µg IM/IV** | **No** | **No** | No | Before birth of baby | **Heat-stable** |
| **Ergometrine** | Ergot alkaloid | 0.2 mg IM (if normotensive) | **0.2 mg IM**, max **1 mg/24 h** | **Never** | Bleeding incomplete abortion | **HTN, PE, heart disease, retained placenta, 2nd twin** | **2–8 °C** |
| **Syntometrine** | Oxytocin 5 IU + ergometrine 0.5 mg | 1 mL IM | Occasionally | Never | No | As ergometrine | 2–8 °C |

| **Carboprost** | 15-methyl PGF2α | No | **250 µg IM q15 min, max 2 mg** | No | No | **Asthma** | 2–8 °C |

> [!EXAM]
> **Classic traps:** carbetocin for treatment (no, prevention only); ergometrine for retained placenta (no, traps it); oxytocin for a 9-week incomplete miscarriage (no, few receptors: misoprostol); carboprost in an asthmatic (no); ergometrine in pre-eclampsia (no: stroke/eclampsia); misoprostol IOL with a previous CS at term (no: Foley + oxytocin or CS).

## Tranexamic acid

### Tranexamic acid (TXA)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | **Antifibrinolytic**: synthetic **lysine analogue** |
| **Mechanism** | Blocks the **lysine-binding sites on plasminogen** → plasminogen cannot bind fibrin or become plasmin → the **clot is not broken down**. It does **not** make new clot; it protects the clot you have. Fibrinolysis is **activated within the first hours after birth** (placental separation releases tPA), which is why it works early |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Treatment of PPH of any cause** (tone, trauma, tissue, thrombin), with uterotonics; heavy menstrual bleeding; bleeding from cervical cancer; some units at CS for high-risk women (not routine) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **PPH: 1 g (10 mL of 100 mg/mL) IV over 10 min** (1 mL/min) **within 3 h of birth**; **second 1 g** if bleeding continues after **30 min** or restarts within **24 h**. **Heavy menstrual bleeding: 1 g orally 8-hourly** (1–1.5 g 8–12-hourly) for **up to 4–5 days** from the start of each period. **Cervical cancer bleeding:** 1 g IV 8-hourly, then 1 g orally 8-hourly |
| **Onset / PK** | IV onset minutes; half-life ~2 h; **renally excreted** (reduce dose in renal impairment) |
| **Side effects** | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea; **hypotension/dizziness if injected too fast**; rarely visual disturbance, seizures (very high doses) |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Active thromboembolic disease** (DVT, PE, stroke); caution with a history of VTE or thrombophilia; **severe renal impairment** (dose reduction); history of seizures; established DIC with predominant clotting |
| **Monitoring** | Time of birth vs time of dose (**document both**); continued bleeding; signs of VTE |
| **Pregnancy / breastfeeding** | Safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding |
| **Storage / antidote** | Room temperature. No antidote |

**Ward pearls**
- **WOMAN trial (Lancet 2017, 20,060 women, 21 countries incl. Uganda):** TXA **reduced death from bleeding** (1.5% vs 1.9%), with the **biggest benefit when given within 3 h** (about a one-third reduction); **each 15-minute delay reduces the benefit by ~10%**; **no benefit after 3 h**; **no increase in thromboembolism**.
- Mnemonic **"1-10-3-30"**: **1 g**, over **10 min**, within **3 h**, repeat after **30 min**.
- Give TXA **as soon as PPH is diagnosed, together with oxytocin** (E-MOTIVE bundle): don't wait for the uterotonics to fail.
- It is **not a uterotonic**: TXA plus a floppy uterus is still a floppy uterus. Massage and oxytocin first-line, all at once.

> [!DRUG]
> For heavy menstrual bleeding, TXA is **non-hormonal** and taken **only on heavy days**, so it suits women trying to conceive. It reduces loss by about **40–50%**. Mefenamic acid 500 mg 8-hourly (an NSAID) can be combined with it. Exclude pregnancy, fibroids, polyps and cancer first.

## Drugs for miscarriage, abortion care, IUFD and ectopic pregnancy

> [!UGANDA]
> **Post-abortion care (PAC) is always legal and always required** in Uganda, whatever the cause of the abortion. Induced abortion is legally restricted, so use abortion-care regimens only for **legal indications** (e.g. to save the woman's life) under senior/unit policy. **Mifepristone is not consistently stocked** in public facilities; misoprostol-alone regimens are therefore common.

### Mifepristone

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Synthetic **anti-progestogen** (19-norsteroid; RU-486) |
| **Mechanism** | **Progesterone receptor antagonist** → decidual necrosis and detachment, **cervical softening**, and a myometrium **far more sensitive to prostaglandins** (a "primer"). High doses also block glucocorticoid receptors |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | Missed miscarriage <14 weeks; IUFD (before misoprostol); medical abortion where legal |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **200 mg orally once**, then **misoprostol 24–48 h (1–2 days) later** at the dose for the gestation |
| **Onset / PK** | Long half-life (~18–30 h); effect builds over 24–48 h |
| **Side effects** | Bleeding, cramps, nausea, headache; bleeding for up to 2 weeks after expulsion |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Suspected ectopic** (it does not treat ectopic), **chronic adrenal failure / long-term steroids**, severe uncontrolled asthma, inherited porphyria, bleeding disorder or anticoagulation, **IUD in situ** (remove first) |
| **Monitoring** | Bleeding, pain, completion (history ± scan or hCG after 2 weeks) |
| **Pregnancy / breastfeeding** | Teratogenic risk unclear, but counsel that a continuing pregnancy after failed treatment needs evacuation; breastfeeding acceptable |
| **Storage / antidote** | Room temperature. No antidote |

**Ward pearls**
- **Mifepristone + misoprostol beats misoprostol alone**: higher complete-evacuation rates and a **shorter induction-to-delivery time** in IUFD.
- Where there is no mifepristone, **don't wait**: start the misoprostol-alone regimen.

### Methotrexate (for ectopic pregnancy)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | **Antimetabolite / folic acid antagonist** (cytotoxic) |
| **Mechanism** | Inhibits **dihydrofolate reductase** → no tetrahydrofolate → no purine/thymidine synthesis → kills rapidly dividing **trophoblast** |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Unruptured tubal ectopic** meeting criteria; pregnancy of unknown location with rising hCG (referral centres); persistent trophoblast after salpingotomy; low-risk **gestational trophoblastic neoplasia** (different regimen) |
| **Criteria (NICE, all needed)** | **Haemodynamically stable, no significant pain**; unruptured, **adnexal mass <35 mm**; **no fetal heartbeat**; **hCG <1,500 IU/L** (offer choice of methotrexate or surgery for **1,500–5,000 IU/L**); **no intrauterine pregnancy**; **able to return** for follow-up |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **50 mg/m² IM single dose** (BSA = √(height cm × weight kg ÷ 3,600); e.g. 1.6 m² → **80 mg IM**). Baseline **FBC, LFTs, creatinine, hCG, blood group** |
| **Monitoring** | **hCG on day 4 and day 7**: a **fall of ≥15% from day 4 to day 7** = success, then **weekly until negative** (4–6 weeks). hCG often **rises between day 1 and day 4**: expected. **<15% fall → second dose 50 mg/m²** (about 1 in 7 need it) or surgery |
| **Side effects** | "Separation pain" days 2–7; nausea, vomiting, **stomatitis**, conjunctivitis, **photosensitivity**, transient LFT rise; rarely **marrow suppression**, pneumonitis, alopecia; **tubal rupture can still occur** |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Haemodynamic instability/rupture**; **intrauterine or heterotopic pregnancy**; **breastfeeding**; **renal or hepatic disease** (raised creatinine/LFTs); **blood dyscrasias** (anaemia, low WBC/platelets); **immunodeficiency**; active peptic ulcer; active lung disease; cannot follow up |
| **Patient advice** | **Avoid folic acid supplements** (antidote effect: treatment fails), **NSAIDs** (reduce methotrexate clearance → toxicity; use paracetamol), **alcohol** (hepatotoxicity), **sun exposure** (photosensitivity) and **sex** until hCG is negative. **Effective contraception for ~3 months** (teratogenic) |
| **Pregnancy / breastfeeding** | **Teratogenic**; contraindicated in breastfeeding |
| **Storage / antidote** | Cytotoxic handling and disposal. Antidote for toxicity: **folinic acid (calcium leucovorin)** |

**Ward pearls**
- Memory aid (from the ectopic topic): **"No PAIN, No BEAT, Under 35 mm, Under 1,500, Can come back."** Avoid-list: **SAFE-N** (Sun, Alcohol, Folate, Expectancy: no pregnancy for 3 months, NSAIDs).
- **Severe pain, dizziness or shoulder-tip pain after methotrexate = come back now**: rupture is still possible.
- **In most Ugandan hospitals the safer option is surgery** (salpingectomy): methotrexate and serial quantitative hCG are mainly at referral and private centres, and many women cannot return within hours.

> [!NOTE]
> **Anti-D**: give to **Rh-negative, non-sensitised** women after ectopic pregnancy (with surgery), surgical evacuation, miscarriage ≥12 weeks, molar pregnancy, IUFD and delivery of an Rh-positive baby, **within 72 h**. Doses and Uganda vial sizes are in the **Quick Reference (section 7)**.

## Cervical ripening and induction of labour: the drug summary

**Step 1: score the cervix** with the **Bishop score** (Quick Reference, section 9). **≥8 favourable** → ARM + oxytocin. **≤6 unfavourable** → ripen first.

| Option | Type | How | Pros | Cons / cautions | Previous CS? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Membrane sweep** | Mechanical | Finger sweeps membranes off the lower segment at ≥39–41 weeks | Cheap, reduces need for formal IOL | Discomfort, spotting | Yes |
| **Foley catheter** | **Mechanical** | **16–18 F** through the cervix past the internal os, balloon **30–60 mL** saline, taped to the thigh with gentle traction; remove at **12–24 h** or when it falls out | Cheap, available, **less tachysystole**, **no drug**, safe with a live fetus | Slightly slower; infection risk if left long; not with low-lying placenta or ruptured membranes (relative) | **Yes: preferred ripening method** |
| **Misoprostol** | Pharmacological (PGE1) | **25 µg vaginal 6-hourly or 25 µg oral 2-hourly** | Cheap, heat-stable, effective, fewer CS for failed IOL | Tachysystole; **can't be switched off quickly** | **No (third trimester)** |
| **Dinoprostone** | Pharmacological (PGE2) | Gel 1–2 mg or 10 mg pessary | Pessary removable | Expensive, fridge, rarely in Uganda | UK: with caution; Uganda: avoid |
| **ARM + oxytocin** | Mechanical + pharmacological | When Bishop ≥8 (or ≥6 after ripening) | Controllable, short half-life | Needs one-to-one monitoring | **With caution** (senior, theatre ready); never with a **classical scar** |

**Rules of thumb**
1. **Start oxytocin at least 4 h after the last misoprostol dose** (combined effect → hyperstimulation).
2. **Previous CS: avoid misoprostol and (in Uganda) prostaglandins for IOL at term.** Use **Foley + oxytocin** at a hospital with theatre, with low-dose, slowly titrated oxytocin, and **stop at the first sign of scar pain, bleeding, maternal tachycardia or FHR abnormality**. **Classical scar or ≥2 CS → elective CS, no labour.**
3. **Grand multipara**: lower doses, slower titration, and no oxytocin if there is any hint of obstruction.
4. Contraindications to any IOL: **CPD/obstruction, transverse lie, placenta praevia, vasa praevia, cord presentation, previous classical CS, active genital herpes**.

> [!PEARL]
> Why is misoprostol dangerous with a scar but oxytocin tolerated? **Oxytocin can be switched off** (half-life 3–5 min), whereas misoprostol, once absorbed, keeps working for hours and prostaglandins also soften the scar tissue. **"If you can't switch it off, don't put it into a scarred uterus."**

## Antibiotics in pregnancy and gynaecology

Infection kills Ugandan mothers in three ways: **puerperal sepsis**, **septic (usually unsafe) abortion** and **pyelonephritis or chorioamnionitis that is treated too late**. Two questions decide almost every prescription on the ward: **"Is this drug safe for the fetus (and the breastfed baby)?"** and **"Does it cover the likely organisms?"** Pelvic infections are **polymicrobial**: Gram-positives (streptococci, including GBS; staphylococci), **Gram-negatives** (*E. coli*) and **anaerobes** (*Bacteroides*, *Clostridium*). That is why the classic regimen has **three drugs**, one for each group.

### Safe versus avoid in pregnancy

| Generally safe (use freely when indicated) | Why it is safe / the caveat |
|---|---|
| **Penicillins** (benzylpenicillin, ampicillin, amoxicillin, cloxacillin/flucloxacillin, benzathine penicillin) | Decades of use; no teratogenicity. Only issue is allergy |
| **Cephalosporins** (cefazolin, cefalexin, ceftriaxone, cefixime) | Safe in all trimesters. Avoid only after **penicillin anaphylaxis** |
| **Macrolides**: erythromycin, azithromycin | Safe. Erythromycin is **the** PPROM antibiotic |
| **Metronidazole** | Large meta-analyses show no teratogenic effect, including in the 1st trimester. Warn about alcohol (disulfiram-like reaction) |
| **Nitrofurantoin** | Good for cystitis/ASB. **Avoid at term (from ~36–38 weeks), in labour and in G6PD deficiency** (neonatal haemolysis); useless for pyelonephritis |
| **Clindamycin** | Safe. Used for GBS prophylaxis in penicillin allergy, BV and with quinine. Watch for *C. difficile* diarrhoea |

| Avoid or use with caution | Fetal/neonatal risk | Exam hook |
|---|---|---|
| **Tetracyclines** (doxycycline, tetracycline) | **Yellow-brown teeth staining**, impaired bone growth (after ~15 weeks); maternal liver toxicity | "**T**etracycline = **T**eeth" |
| **Fluoroquinolones** (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) | Cartilage/arthropathy in animal studies | Avoid unless no alternative (e.g. MDR infection) |
| **Aminoglycosides** (gentamicin, **streptomycin**) | **Fetal 8th-nerve damage (deafness)**, clearly shown for **streptomycin**; nephrotoxicity. Gentamicin: accepted as a **short course** in serious sepsis | Benefit beats risk in sepsis; keep courses short and monitored |
| **Trimethoprim** | **Folate antagonist**: neural tube and cardiac defects in the **1st trimester** | Avoid in the 1st trimester |
| **Sulfonamides / co-trimoxazole** (as *treatment*) | **Near term**: displace bilirubin from albumin → **kernicterus**; haemolysis in G6PD deficiency | HIV-positive women **continue** co-trimoxazole prophylaxis |
| **Chloramphenicol** | **Grey baby syndrome** near term (the neonatal liver cannot glucuronidate it → vomiting, grey skin, circulatory collapse) | Avoid in late pregnancy and breastfeeding |
| **Co-amoxiclav in PPROM** | **Necrotising enterocolitis (NEC)** in the preterm baby (ORACLE I trial) | Fine for other indications (e.g. UTI) |

> [!EXAM]
> The favourite trio: **tetracyclines → teeth**, **chloramphenicol → grey baby**, **aminoglycosides (streptomycin) → deafness**. Add **sulfonamides near term → kernicterus** and **co-amoxiclav in PPROM → NEC**, and you have the whole viva.

### Drug card: Gentamicin

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Aminoglycoside |
| **Mechanism** | Binds the **30S ribosomal subunit** → misreading of mRNA; **concentration-dependent** killing with a post-antibiotic effect (why once-daily dosing works) |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | Gram-negative cover in **puerperal sepsis, septic abortion, chorioamnionitis**, pyelonephritis (second line), PID (inpatient) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **5 mg/kg IV (or IM) once daily** (e.g. **300 mg for 60 kg**). Older regimen still seen: **80 mg IV/IM 8-hourly** (~1.5 mg/kg for a 55 kg woman). Obese: use adjusted body weight. Give IV over 3–5 min |
| **Side effects** | **Nephrotoxicity** (acute tubular necrosis, usually reversible), **ototoxicity** (vestibular and cochlear; often permanent), **neuromuscular blockade** |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **AKI** or rising creatinine, myasthenia gravis. Caution with **MgSO₄** (additive neuromuscular block), furosemide, other nephrotoxins |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Crosses the placenta: theoretical fetal ototoxicity, so use **short courses (≤5–7 days)** when clearly needed. **Compatible with breastfeeding** (hardly absorbed from the baby's gut) |
| **Monitoring** | **Urine output** (catheter in sepsis), **creatinine at baseline and every 1–2 days**, trough level before the next dose where available (**<1 mg/L** for once-daily), ask about tinnitus/vertigo |

> [!DRUG]
> **Once daily beats 8-hourly**: the same or better killing (high peak ÷ MIC), **less kidney toxicity** (the kidney gets a drug-free interval), and fewer doses to miss on a busy ward. **80 mg 8-hourly under-doses** most women (240 mg/day vs 300 mg for 60 kg). If creatinine rises or urine output falls: **hold gentamicin** and switch to **ceftriaxone + metronidazole**.

### Drug card: Metronidazole

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Nitroimidazole |
| **Mechanism** | Reduced inside anaerobes to toxic radicals that break DNA |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Anaerobic** cover in sepsis/septic abortion/post-CS infection; BV; trichomoniasis; PID; *C. difficile* |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **500 mg IV 8-hourly** (sepsis); **400–500 mg orally 8–12-hourly**; trichomonas 2 g once or 500 mg 12-hourly × 7 days |
| **Side effects** | Metallic taste, nausea, dark urine; **disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol** (avoid during and 48 h after); peripheral neuropathy with long courses |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Alcohol; severe liver disease (reduce dose) |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Safe in all trimesters. Standard doses compatible with breastfeeding; after a **2 g** single dose, pause breastfeeding **12–24 h** |
| **Monitoring** | Clinical response; LFTs if prolonged in liver disease |

### Drug card: Benzathine penicillin G

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Long-acting (depot) penicillin |
| **Mechanism** | Blocks bacterial cell-wall synthesis; the depot releases penicillin slowly for **2–4 weeks**, long enough for slow-dividing *Treponema pallidum* |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Syphilis** (mother and partner); rheumatic fever prophylaxis |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **2.4 MU deep IM** (1.2 MU into each buttock). **Early: once. Late latent/unknown: weekly × 3** (if a dose is >2 weeks late in pregnancy, restart the course) |
| **Side effects** | Painful injection; allergy/anaphylaxis (have adrenaline ready); **Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction** |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Penicillin anaphylaxis (see below). **Never IV** (depot can cause embolic/cardiorespiratory arrest) |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | **The only drug that reliably treats the fetus.** Safe |
| **Monitoring** | Observe 30 min after injection; RPR titre at 3 and 6 months (a **fourfold fall** = response); examine and treat the baby at birth |

> [!REDFLAG]
> **Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction**: fever, chills, myalgia, headache and hypotension **2–24 h after the first penicillin dose**, from killed spirochaetes releasing endotoxin-like products. In pregnancy it can trigger **contractions, preterm labour and fetal distress**. It is **not an allergy**: give **paracetamol**, fluids, monitor the fetus, and **do not stop** the treatment.

> [!NOTE]
> **Penicillin allergy in pregnancy and syphilis**: erythromycin (500 mg 6-hourly × 14 days) **does not cross the placenta reliably**, so the baby is untreated and must be treated at birth. Azithromycin has widespread resistance. Options are **penicillin desensitisation** (the gold standard, at a referral hospital) or **ceftriaxone 1 g IM/IV daily × 10–14 days** per protocol. Many "allergies" are not true allergies: take a careful history.

### Drug card: Erythromycin

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Macrolide |
| **Mechanism** | Binds the **50S** ribosomal subunit; bacteriostatic |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **PPROM** (prolongs latency, reduces chorioamnionitis and neonatal infection, ORACLE I); chlamydia; penicillin allergy (mastitis, syphilis, penicillin V replacement in SCD) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **PPROM: 250 mg orally 6-hourly × 10 days** (or until labour). Chlamydia: 500 mg 6-hourly × 7 days |
| **Side effects** | Nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps (motilin agonist), QT prolongation, cholestasis (estolate salt) |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | QT-prolonging drugs (quinine); liver disease |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Safe. Small infantile pyloric stenosis signal with neonatal exposure |
| **Monitoring** | Signs of chorioamnionitis (temperature, pulse, FHR, uterine tenderness, liquor smell) every 4 h |

### Ward pearls: antibiotics

- **Timing saves lives**: in sepsis, give the **first dose within 1 hour**, after blood cultures if they can be drawn without delay. Do not wait for the lab.
- **Antibiotics never replace source control.** Retained products, an infected haematoma, a pelvic abscess or dead uterine tissue will keep her febrile whatever you prescribe.
- **Fever not settling after 48–72 h** → look for pus (ultrasound), retained products, wound, breasts, legs (DVT), **malaria** and septic pelvic thrombophlebitis; then consider resistant organisms (ESBL: ask for culture).
- Always ask **"What kind of reaction?"** to a penicillin allergy: a rash vs anaphylaxis changes whether a cephalosporin is allowed.
- **Doxycycline is fine for PID** in a non-pregnant woman, which is why a **pregnancy test comes before every PID prescription**.

## Antimalarials in pregnancy

Malaria in pregnancy causes **maternal anaemia, hypoglycaemia, severe malaria (pregnant women are more likely to develop it), miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm birth and low birth weight**. The placenta hides parasites (VAR2CSA binding to chondroitin sulphate A), so a woman can have **placental malaria with a negative blood smear**. Prevention (**IPTp-SP + ITN**) is as important as treatment.

### Drug card: Sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine (SP) for IPTp

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Antifolate combination (sulfonamide + dihydrofolate reductase inhibitor) |
| **Mechanism** | Sulfadoxine blocks **dihydropteroate synthase**, pyrimethamine blocks **dihydrofolate reductase**: sequential block of parasite folate synthesis |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp)** for HIV-negative women (or HIV-positive women **not** on co-trimoxazole) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **3 tablets** (each sulfadoxine 500 mg / pyrimethamine 25 mg = **1,500/75 mg**) orally, **directly observed** at the clinic. **From 13 weeks**, then **monthly** (at least **4 weeks apart**) **until delivery**; aim for **≥3 doses** |
| **Side effects** | Usually none; rash; rarely **Stevens–Johnson syndrome**, marrow suppression |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **1st trimester**; **sulfa allergy**; **on co-trimoxazole** (overlapping toxicity, no added benefit); **folic acid ≥5 mg** (antagonises the effect) |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Safe in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters, **including at term** (WHO 2012 update). Compatible with breastfeeding |
| **Monitoring** | Record each dose on the ANC card; ask about rash |

> [!UGANDA]
> Give IPTp with the routine **iron + folic acid 400 mcg** (the low dose is fine). The problem is **folic acid 5 mg**: women with **SCD, epilepsy or a previous NTD** need 5 mg, so they get **daily proguanil 200 mg** instead, or SP with the folic acid **withheld for 1–2 weeks after each dose** (follow your unit protocol; see the sickle cell topic). An **HIV-positive** woman on **co-trimoxazole 960 mg daily** gets **no SP**, only an **ITN**.

### Treating uncomplicated malaria by trimester

| Trimester | First line | Alternative | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1st** | **Artemether–lumefantrine (AL)**: **WHO 2022** now recommends AL in the 1st trimester (large cohort data showed no higher risk of miscarriage, stillbirth or anomalies than quinine) | **Oral quinine 600 mg (10 mg/kg salt) 8-hourly × 7 days + clindamycin 300 mg 8-hourly × 7 days** | Many Ugandan wards and older UCG editions still teach **quinine + clindamycin** here. Check your unit's current UCG copy |
| **2nd and 3rd** | **AL** | Other ACT (e.g. artesunate–amodiaquine, dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine) | ACTs are safe; quinine is second choice |

**AL dose (adult ≥35 kg): 4 tablets (20/120 mg each) at 0 h, 8 h, then 12-hourly for 2 more days = 6 doses, 24 tablets**, taken **with fatty food or milk** (lumefantrine absorption rises many-fold with fat). Repeat the dose if she vomits within 30 min.

> [!REDFLAG]
> **Avoid in pregnancy**: **primaquine** (haemolysis of a possibly G6PD-deficient fetus; also avoid when breastfeeding an infant of unknown G6PD status), **doxycycline** (teeth/bone), **tafenoquine**. Doxycycline is therefore not an option for prophylaxis in pregnant travellers.

### Drug card: Artesunate (severe malaria)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Artemisinin derivative |
| **Mechanism** | Endoperoxide bridge activated by haem iron → free radicals that kill parasites fast, including young ring forms (before they sequester) |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Severe malaria in any trimester** (impaired consciousness, convulsions, prostration, severe anaemia, hypoglycaemia, respiratory distress, jaundice, shock, AKI, hyperparasitaemia) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **2.4 mg/kg IV (or IM) at 0, 12 and 24 h, then once daily** until she can take oral drugs (**minimum 3 doses / 24 h**), then a **full 3-day AL course**. 60 mg vial: dissolve in 1 mL 5% sodium bicarbonate, then dilute (IV: 5 mL → 10 mg/mL; IM: 2 mL → 20 mg/mL) |
| **Side effects** | Well tolerated; **post-artesunate delayed haemolysis** 1–3 weeks later |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Known hypersensitivity. **No trimester restriction for severe malaria** |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | **Drug of choice in all trimesters**: the risk of severe malaria to mother and fetus far outweighs any theoretical drug risk |
| **Monitoring** | GCS, **RBS 4-hourly** (malaria itself causes hypoglycaemia), Hb (and at **7, 14 and 28 days** for delayed haemolysis), urine output, fetal heart |

If artesunate is unavailable: **IM artemether 3.2 mg/kg loading, then 1.6 mg/kg daily**, or **IV quinine 20 mg/kg (salt) loading in 5% dextrose over 4 h, then 10 mg/kg 8-hourly** (each over 4 h). **Never give quinine as an IV bolus.**

### Quinine: the hypoglycaemia drug

| Quinine | |
|---|---|
| Uses | 1st-trimester uncomplicated malaria (with clindamycin, where AL is not used); severe malaria when artesunate is unavailable |
| Key toxicity | **Hypoglycaemia** (quinine stimulates **pancreatic insulin release**; pregnancy and parasites also consume glucose), **cinchonism** (tinnitus, deafness, nausea, blurred vision), QT prolongation, hypotension with fast infusion |
| What to do | **Check RBS every 4 h** (hourly if unconscious), infuse in **5% or 10% dextrose**, treat glucose <3 mmol/L with **25–50 mL of 50% dextrose IV** (or 10% dextrose 5 mL/kg) |

> [!EXAM]
> "A pregnant woman on IV quinine becomes confused and sweaty" = **hypoglycaemia until proven otherwise**. Check the glucose **before** you think of cerebral malaria worsening or eclampsia.

### Ward pearls: malaria

- **Test every febrile pregnant or postnatal woman** (mRDT or smear), but a **negative test does not exclude placental malaria**; treat the clinical picture.
- Severe malaria at an **HC III**: give the **first dose of IM artesunate** (2.4 mg/kg) and **refer**.
- Malaria causes **uterine contractions and fetal distress**: treat the fever (paracetamol) and monitor the fetus.
- Before discharge, check the **ITN** and the IPTp schedule on the ANC card.

## HIV and eMTCT drugs

### Drug card: TLD (tenofovir DF / lamivudine / dolutegravir)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Two NRTIs (**TDF**, **3TC**) + an **integrase strand transfer inhibitor** (**DTG**) |
| **Mechanism** | NRTIs are chain terminators of **reverse transcriptase**; DTG blocks **integration** of viral DNA into the host genome |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Lifelong ART for every HIV-positive pregnant or breastfeeding woman** (test-and-treat, same day); PEP for 28 days; HBV co-infection (TDF + 3TC treat HBV) |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **One tablet (TDF 300 mg / 3TC 300 mg / DTG 50 mg) orally once daily**. With **rifampicin**: add an **extra DTG 50 mg** (DTG **twice daily**) until 2 weeks after rifampicin stops |
| **Side effects** | **TDF**: **renal tubular toxicity** (Fanconi-type), falling eGFR, reduced bone density. **DTG**: **weight gain**, insomnia, headache; small creatinine rise from blocked tubular secretion (not true kidney damage). **3TC**: well tolerated |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **CrCl <50 mL/min** → no TDF (substitute AZT or ABC per guidelines). **Polyvalent cations** (iron, calcium, antacids, zinc) chelate DTG. Carbamazepine/phenytoin lower DTG. DTG raises **metformin** levels |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | **Preferred regimen in pregnancy and breastfeeding** (WHO 2019 onwards, Uganda). Fast viral suppression, high barrier to resistance |
| **Monitoring** | **Viral load**: first ANC visit if already on ART, **3 months after starting**, then **every 3 months until breastfeeding ends**, and one available at **34–36 weeks**; baseline **creatinine/CrCl**; weight, BP, glucose; adherence |

> [!NOTE]
> **The dolutegravir neural tube defect story**: in **2018**, Botswana's **Tsepamo** study found NTDs in about **0.9%** of babies of women on DTG **at conception** (4 of 426), several times the rate on other ART. With many more exposures the rate fell to about **0.1%**, **statistically the same as other ART**. WHO (2019) and Uganda therefore recommend **DTG for everyone**, including women who are pregnant or may become pregnant. Give **folic acid** as for any pregnancy. **Never stop or delay TLD** because of the NTD question.

> [!DRUG]
> **DTG + iron/calcium**: take DTG **2 h before or 6 h after** iron, calcium or antacids, **or take them together with a meal**. A woman who takes her **TLD, FeFo and calcium together on an empty stomach** absorbs less DTG and is at risk of **non-suppression**. Ask how and when she takes her tablets.

### Infant prophylaxis (Uganda consolidated guidelines)

Start **within 6 hours of birth**. Risk is set by the **mother's most recent viral load**.

| Infant risk | Who | Regimen | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low risk** | Mother on ART with a **suppressed VL** (below 1,000 copies/mL) near delivery | **NVP once daily** | **6 weeks** |
| **High risk** | Mother **non-suppressed or VL unknown**, **ART <4 weeks** before delivery, **diagnosed in labour or while breastfeeding** | **AZT twice daily + NVP once daily for 6 weeks**, then **NVP alone for 6 weeks** | **12 weeks**; continue NVP if mother still not suppressed |

| Age / weight | NVP (10 mg/mL), once daily | AZT (10 mg/mL), twice daily |
|---|---|---|
| Birth–6 weeks, **2.0–2.49 kg** | **10 mg (1 mL)** | **10 mg (1 mL)** |
| Birth–6 weeks, **≥2.5 kg** | **15 mg (1.5 mL)** | **15 mg (1.5 mL)** |
| 6–12 weeks | **20 mg (2 mL)** | Stopped |

Side effects: **AZT** → anaemia and neutropenia; **NVP** → rash and (rarely) hepatotoxicity. The HIV-exposed infant starts **co-trimoxazole from 6 weeks** (120 mg daily under 6 months) until HIV infection is excluded.

### Drug card: Co-trimoxazole prophylaxis (CPT)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Sulfonamide + trimethoprim (sulfamethoxazole 800 mg / trimethoprim 160 mg = **960 mg**) |
| **Mechanism** | Sequential block of folate synthesis (like SP) |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **All HIV-positive pregnant and breastfeeding women in Uganda**: prevents PCP, toxoplasmosis, **malaria**, bacterial pneumonia and diarrhoea |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **960 mg orally once daily** |
| **Side effects** | Rash, **Stevens–Johnson syndrome**, marrow suppression, hepatitis |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Sulfa allergy; **never with IPTp-SP**; severe renal or liver disease |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | **Continue throughout**, including the 1st trimester and near term: the benefit outweighs the theoretical folate and kernicterus risks. Compatible with breastfeeding |
| **Monitoring** | Rash (stop at once if mucosal involvement), FBC if pale |

### Ward pearls: HIV

- On the **day of CS** she still takes her **TLD**. Never omit ART in labour.
- **New positive in labour** → **TLD now**, baby **high-risk** (AZT + NVP), DNA PCR per schedule.
- HIV-positive + HBsAg-positive: **TLD already covers HBV**. Stopping TDF can cause a hepatitis flare.
- **Efavirenz** is no longer considered a reason to avoid pregnancy; old "teratogen" teaching is outdated.

## Anti-D, haematinics, supplements and vaccines

### Drug card: Anti-D immunoglobulin

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Polyclonal human IgG against the RhD antigen |
| **Mechanism** | Coats RhD-positive fetal red cells in the maternal circulation so they are **cleared before her immune system makes its own anti-D** (prevents sensitisation and haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn in the **next** pregnancy) |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **RhD-negative, non-sensitised** women after birth of an Rh-positive baby, after sensitising events, and routine antenatal prophylaxis where available |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | **1,500 IU (300 mcg) IM** (the vial usually stocked) **within 72 h** (some benefit up to 10 days). UK doses: **250 IU** before 20 weeks; **500 IU + Kleihauer** from 20 weeks and after delivery. Antenatal prophylaxis: **1,500 IU at 28 weeks** or **500 IU at 28 and 34 weeks** |
| **Side effects** | Injection-site pain; rarely allergy; blood product (consent) |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Already sensitised** (anti-D antibodies present: no benefit); **RhD-positive** woman; **never give to the baby** |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Safe |
| **Monitoring** | Group the mother (booking) and the **cord blood** (baby's group, DAT); **Kleihauer** to size a large feto-maternal haemorrhage (1,500 IU covers about **15 mL of fetal red cells**, 500 IU about 4 mL) |

| Event | Give anti-D? |
|---|---|
| Delivery of an Rh-positive (or ungrouped) baby | **Yes**, within 72 h |
| Miscarriage **≥12 weeks**; any **surgical evacuation**, **ectopic**, **molar pregnancy** | **Yes** |
| Threatened miscarriage <12 weeks with light bleeding | Not needed (UK); give if heavy/repeated bleeding |
| APH, abdominal trauma, ECV, amniocentesis, IUFD, **after 20 weeks** | **Yes** (+ Kleihauer where available) |

> [!UGANDA]
> Anti-D is **expensive and often out of stock** in public units. Find the **Rh-negative** women at booking, counsel them early and **plan the purchase before delivery**, so the 72-hour window is not missed.

### Drug card: Iron + folic acid (FeFo)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Haematinic (ferrous salt) + B vitamin |
| **Mechanism** | Replaces iron for haemoglobin synthesis (pregnancy needs **~1 g** extra iron); folate for DNA synthesis in dividing cells and neural tube closure |
| **Indications (Obs/Gyn)** | **Every pregnant woman** (prevention); iron-deficiency anaemia (treatment); postpartum |
| **Dose & route (Uganda)** | Prevention: **ferrous sulphate 200 mg (60 mg elemental iron) + folic acid 400 mcg once daily** throughout pregnancy and **3 months postpartum**. Treatment of anaemia: **120 mg elemental iron daily** (e.g. ferrous sulphate 200 mg 12-hourly) |
| **Side effects** | Nausea, epigastric pain, **constipation**, **black stools** (warn her: not melaena), diarrhoea |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **SCD or thalassaemia without proven iron deficiency** (iron overload); haemochromatosis. Absorption falls with **tea, calcium, antacids**; raise with vitamin C. Separate from **DTG** |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Safe |
| **Monitoring** | Hb at booking, **26–28** and **34–36 weeks**; expected **reticulocyte peak at 7–10 days** and Hb rise of about **1 g/dL every 2 weeks** on treatment. No rise → poor adherence, malaria, worms, HIV, folate/B12 deficiency, blood loss, SCD |

**Parenteral iron** (hospital level): for women who **cannot tolerate or will not take oral iron**, malabsorption, or **iron-deficiency anaemia late in pregnancy (>34 weeks)** when time is short. Give only in the **2nd/3rd trimester**, with resuscitation equipment at hand. Options: **iron sucrose 200 mg IV** per dose (repeat to the total deficit), **ferric carboxymaltose up to 1,000 mg (20 mg/kg) IV** in one infusion, or iron dextran (total-dose infusion; highest anaphylaxis risk). Total deficit (Ganzoni) ≈ **weight (kg) × (target Hb − actual Hb, g/dL) × 2.4 + 500 mg** for stores. It raises Hb **no faster** than full-dose oral iron that is actually taken, but it is **certain**. **Transfuse** for severe anaemia with decompensation or Hb <7 g/dL near term.

| **Epilepsy on antiepileptics** (valproate, carbamazepine, phenytoin) | |

| **Diabetes** (pre-existing) | |
| **Obesity (BMI ≥30)** | |
| Malabsorption (coeliac disease), drugs that are folate antagonists (e.g. methotrexate, long-term trimethoprim) | |

### Other supplements, deworming and vaccines (comparison)

| Intervention | Regimen | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Calcium** | **1.5–2 g elemental daily** (divided) **from 20 weeks** | Reduces pre-eclampsia where dietary calcium is low. Take **several hours apart from iron** (and DTG) |
| **Aspirin** (not a supplement, but part of the ANC pack) | **75–150 mg at night from 12 to 36 weeks** | High risk of pre-eclampsia; start before 20 weeks |
| **Deworming** | **Mebendazole 500 mg single dose** or **albendazole 400 mg single dose** | **After the 1st trimester only** (teratogenic in animals). Hookworm is a major cause of iron-deficiency anaemia |
| **Td vaccine** | **0.5 mL IM**: **Td1** first contact → **Td2 ≥4 weeks later** → **Td3 ≥6 months** → **Td4 ≥1 year** → **Td5 ≥1 year** | **Td2 at least 2 weeks before delivery** protects the newborn against **neonatal tetanus**. A woman with 5 documented doses needs no more |

### Sickle cell disease: the drug changes in pregnancy

| Drug | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide)** | **Stop at least 3 months before conception**; if she conceives on it, **stop at once**, counsel and arrange a detailed anomaly scan. Restart after delivery only if not breastfeeding (or per haematologist) | Inhibits DNA synthesis: **teratogenic in animals** |
| **Folic acid** | **5 mg daily throughout** | Chronic haemolysis consumes folate |
| **Iron** | **Not routine**: only if ferritin shows deficiency | Transfusion and haemolysis load iron |
| **Penicillin V** | **250 mg orally twice daily** (some protocols 500 mg); erythromycin 250 mg twice daily if allergic | **Functional asplenia** → pneumococcal sepsis |
| **Malaria chemoprevention** | **Proguanil 200 mg daily** (safe with folic acid 5 mg), or IPTp-SP with folic acid withheld 1–2 weeks after each dose (unit protocol) + **ITN** | Malaria is a major crisis trigger; folic acid 5 mg blunts SP |
| **Aspirin** | **75 mg daily from 12 weeks** | Placental disease, pre-eclampsia |
| ACE inhibitors, iron chelators | **Stop** before conception | Teratogenic / fetotoxic |

### Ward pearls: supplements

- The routine ANC pack is **FeFo + IPTp-SP + mebendazole + Td + ITN (+ calcium, aspirin if high risk)**. When a woman books late, **catch up everything at one visit**.
- **Black stools on iron** are expected; ask about **dizziness, haematemesis or abdominal pain** before blaming the iron.
- An **HIV-positive woman on TLD**: tell her to take **FeFo and calcium at a different time from TLD**, or all together **with food**.
- Always check the **Rhesus group before a woman leaves after any evacuation, ectopic or delivery**.

> [!PEARL]
> **Senior's favourite question: "Why can't she have SP?"** Four answers: **first trimester**, **sulfa allergy**, **on co-trimoxazole**, and **on folic acid 5 mg** (relative: give proguanil or withhold the folic acid around each dose).

## Contraception and reproductive hormones

Hormonal contraceptives work in three ways. They **stop ovulation** by negative feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary. They **thicken cervical mucus** so sperm cannot get through. They **thin the endometrium**. Combined methods (oestrogen + progestogen) rely mainly on stopping ovulation. Progestogen-only methods rely more on the mucus, and how often they stop ovulation depends on the method: DMPA and implants almost always do, while the older POPs do so only about half the time.

### Combined oral contraceptive (COC)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Ethinylestradiol (usually **30 mcg**) + a progestogen (levonorgestrel 150 mcg in Microgynon-type pills, the common Ugandan brand; norgestrel in Lo-Femenal) |
| **Mechanism** | Oestrogen + progestogen suppress FSH and LH, so **no follicle and no LH surge**. Also thickens mucus and thins the endometrium |
| **Indications** | Contraception; heavy or painful periods; PCOS (cycle control, acne, hirsutism); functional ovarian cysts |
| **Dose & route** | 1 tablet orally daily for 21 days, then a 7-day break (or 7 iron "reminder" tablets in 28-day packs). Start **day 1–5 of the cycle** with no back-up needed. Starting later ("quick start") needs **7 days of condoms** |
| **Side effects** | Nausea, breast tenderness, breakthrough bleeding (usually settles within 3 months), headache, mood change, **VTE** (about 2–3 times baseline; still lower than in pregnancy), a small rise in BP, a small rise in breast and cervical cancer risk. **Protects** against ovarian and endometrial cancer |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | See the MEC category 4 table below |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Not needed in pregnancy (not teratogenic if taken by mistake). Breastfeeding: **MEC 4 under 6 weeks** and **MEC 3 from 6 weeks to 6 months** because oestrogen **reduces milk supply**. Not breastfeeding: **MEC 4 under 21 days** (VTE) |
| **Monitoring/antidote** | **BP before starting and at each refill**, BMI, smoking, migraine type. No antidote; stop the pill if there are VTE symptoms |

**WHO MEC category 4 for COCs (unacceptable health risk: do not use):**

| Area | Category 4 condition |
|---|---|
| **Postpartum** | Breastfeeding **<6 weeks**; not breastfeeding **<21 days** |
| **Age and smoking** | **Age ≥35 and smokes ≥15 cigarettes/day** (fewer than 15/day = MEC 3) |
| **Blood pressure** | **Systolic ≥160 or diastolic ≥100**, or hypertension with vascular disease (140–159/90–99 = MEC 3) |
| **Thrombosis** | Current or past **DVT/PE**; **major surgery with prolonged immobilisation**; known **thrombogenic mutations**; **SLE with positive antiphospholipid antibodies** |
| **Vascular / heart** | **Ischaemic heart disease**, **stroke**, **complicated valvular heart disease** (pulmonary hypertension, AF, endocarditis history), peripartum cardiomyopathy |
| **Neurological** | **Migraine with aura** (at any age) |
| **Breast** | **Current breast cancer** |
| **Liver** | Severe (decompensated) cirrhosis, hepatocellular adenoma or hepatoma, acute viral hepatitis (MEC 3/4 when starting) |
| **Diabetes** | With nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy or other vascular disease, or duration >20 years (MEC 3/4) |
| **Multiple risk factors** | Several arterial risk factors together (older age, smoking, diabetes, hypertension) (MEC 3/4) |

> [!EXAM]
> The MEC scale: **1** = use freely; **2** = benefits generally outweigh risks; **3** = risks usually outweigh benefits (use only if nothing else is acceptable, with specialist follow-up); **4** = do not use. In a viva, say "**MEC 4, so absolutely contraindicated**", not just "not advised".

**Missed pill rules (WHO, 30–35 mcg pills):**

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| **1–2 pills missed** (up to 48 h late) | Take the missed pill **now** (even if that means 2 today), carry on. **No back-up needed** |
| **≥3 pills missed** (≥48 h late) | Take one now, carry on, and use **condoms for 7 days** |
| … and missed in **week 3** (pills 15–21) | **Skip the pill-free break**: finish the active pills and start a new pack straight away |
| … and missed in **week 1** with unprotected sex | Consider **emergency contraception** |
| Vomiting within 2 h, or severe diarrhoea | Treat as a missed pill |

> [!PEARL]
> The riskiest pills to miss are those **next to the pill-free week** (the start or end of the pack), because they stretch the hormone-free interval beyond 7 days, which is long enough for a follicle to grow.

**Drug interactions: enzyme inducers.** Drugs that induce liver **CYP3A4** speed up metabolism of oestrogen and progestogen, so contraceptive failure follows:

| Drug | Effect on contraception | Advice |
|---|---|---|
| **Rifampicin** (TB), rifabutin | Very strong inducer. MEC **3** for COC, POP and implants | Use **DMPA**, a **copper IUD** or an **LNG-IUS** (all MEC 1). Continue for **28 days after** stopping rifampicin |
| **Efavirenz** (older ART) | Lowers levonorgestrel and etonogestrel levels. Pregnancies reported with implants | DMPA or IUD preferred. Implant only with condoms and counselling |
| Nevirapine, ritonavir-boosted PIs | Change hormone levels (MEC 2) | Add condoms or choose DMPA or an IUD |
| Carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, topiramate | Inducers (MEC 3) | DMPA or an IUD |
| **Dolutegravir (TLD)** | **No clinically important interaction** | **All methods can be used** |
| Broad-spectrum antibiotics (amoxicillin, doxycycline) | No proven effect (except rifamycins) | No extra precautions needed |

> [!UGANDA]
> Most women living with HIV in Uganda are now on **TLD (tenofovir/lamivudine/dolutegravir)**, which does **not** affect any contraceptive. The trap is the woman still on an **efavirenz-based regimen** or on **TB treatment**. Ugandan studies (Scarsi et al.) found that efavirenz lowers levonorgestrel levels from implants enough for pregnancies to occur. Note too that dolutegravir with rifampicin needs **50 mg twice daily**; check the ART clinic's plan.

### Progestogen-only methods

| Method | Drug & dose | Duration | Key facts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **POP** ("mini-pill") | Levonorgestrel 30 mcg or norethisterone 350 mcg daily, no break; **desogestrel 75 mcg** stops ovulation | Daily | Traditional POP: **3-hour window** (desogestrel: 12 h). If late: take it now, use **condoms for 48 h**. Safe from **day 0 postpartum** |
| **DMPA IM** (Depo-Provera) | **150 mg deep IM** | **Every 12 weeks** (13 weeks). WHO allows reinjection up to **4 weeks late** (2 weeks early) without back-up | The most-used method in Uganda. Amenorrhoea in about 50% by 1 year |
| **DMPA-SC** (Sayana Press) | **104 mg SC** (Uniject, can be **self-injected**) | Every 13 weeks | Uganda promotes **self-injection** at home, VHTs and drug shops |
| **Implant: Jadelle** | 2 rods, levonorgestrel 75 mg each | **5 years** | Subdermal, inner upper arm; fertility returns within days of removal |
| **Implant: Implanon NXT** | 1 rod, etonogestrel 68 mg | **3 years** (evidence for up to 5) | The most effective reversible method (failure <0.1%) |
| **LNG-IUS** (Mirena) | Levonorgestrel 52 mg, releasing ~20 mcg/day | **5 years** for HMB; licensed up to 8 years for contraception | First line for **heavy menstrual bleeding**; endometrial protection in HRT |

**DMPA specifics**: side effects include **irregular bleeding** at first, then amenorrhoea, **weight gain** (about 1–2 kg/year), and **reduced bone mineral density**, which is **reversible** after stopping (caution in teenagers under 18 and after 2 years, but it is not a reason to deny it). **Return of fertility is delayed**: on average **about 10 months after the last injection** (median 9–10 months), which is not infertility. Warn women who want a baby within the next year.

> [!PEARL]
> Implants, the LNG-IUS and the copper IUD are **LARCs** (long-acting reversible contraception). Their typical-use failure rate is under 1%, versus about 7% for pills and 4% for DMPA, because they **do not depend on the user remembering**.

### Copper IUD

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Non-hormonal intrauterine device (**TCu380A**) |
| **Mechanism** | Copper ions are **toxic to sperm and ova** (prevents fertilisation) and cause a sterile endometrial inflammation (prevents implantation) |
| **Indications** | Contraception for **10–12 years**; **the most effective emergency contraception** (>99%) |
| **Dose & route** | Inserted through the cervix. Postpartum: **within 48 h** (PPIUD, including at CS) or **from 4 weeks** |
| **Side effects** | **Heavier, more painful periods**, expulsion, perforation (about 1 in 1,000), infection in the first 20 days (from insertion) |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | MEC 4: **pregnancy**, **current PID/purulent cervicitis/gonorrhoea/chlamydia**, **puerperal sepsis or post-septic abortion**, unexplained vaginal bleeding (until assessed), **cervical or endometrial cancer awaiting treatment**, distorted cavity, pelvic TB, **GTD with persistently raised hCG** |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Fully compatible with breastfeeding. If pregnancy occurs with an IUD in place: exclude **ectopic**; remove it if the strings are visible (lower miscarriage risk) |
| **Monitoring/antidote** | Check the strings after each period; review at 3–6 weeks |

### Emergency contraception (EC)

| Method | Dose / timing | Effectiveness | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Copper IUD** | Insert **up to 5 days (120 h)** after unprotected sex, or up to 5 days after the earliest expected ovulation | **>99%**, best | Also gives ongoing contraception |
| **Levonorgestrel** (Postinor-2, "P2") | **1.5 mg orally once**, **within 72 h** (WHO: up to 120 h, less effective) | ~85% of expected pregnancies prevented; less effective with each day | **Double dose (3 mg)** if on **enzyme inducers** (rifampicin, efavirenz, carbamazepine) and in women with BMI >26 or weight >70 kg (FSRH). Safe in breastfeeding |
| **Ulipristal acetate** (ellaOne) | **30 mg orally once**, **within 120 h** | More effective than LNG on days 3–5 | Not reliable with enzyme inducers. Do not start a hormonal method for **5 days** after it (it blunts it). Express and discard breast milk for 1 week. Rarely available in Uganda |

> [!NOTE]
> Oral EC **delays ovulation**. It does **not** cause an abortion and does not harm an established pregnancy. Always pair EC with a pregnancy test at 3 weeks, STI/HIV risk assessment (PEP within 72 h if indicated) and a start of ongoing contraception.

### Progestogens for pregnancy support and bleeding

| Drug | Indication | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vaginal progesterone** (micronised) | **Short cervix ≤25 mm** on scan at 16–24 weeks (singleton), or previous spontaneous preterm birth | **200 mg vaginally at night** until **34 weeks** | Reduces preterm birth before 34 weeks (NICE NG25). Not effective in twins |
| Vaginal progesterone | **Threatened miscarriage with a previous miscarriage** (PRISM trial) | **400 mg vaginally twice daily** until 16 weeks | NICE 2021; modest gain in live births |
| **17-OHPC** (hydroxyprogesterone caproate, Makena) | Previously weekly IM for prior preterm birth | — | **Withdrawn by the US FDA in 2023** (no benefit in PROLONG trial). Do not learn it as a current treatment |
| **Norethisterone** | Acute heavy bleeding; postponing a period | **5 mg orally 8-hourly** for 7–10 days (bleeding usually stops in 48 h); cyclical 5 mg 8-hourly days 5–26 | Not a contraceptive at this dose. Avoid if VTE history (metabolised partly to ethinylestradiol) |
| **Medroxyprogesterone acetate (oral)** | Acute HMB; endometrial hyperplasia; withdrawal bleed before ovulation induction | **10 mg orally 8-hourly** for acute bleeding; 10 mg daily for 10 days for a withdrawal bleed | |

### Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB): the drug ladder

First exclude pregnancy, anaemia, fibroids and polyps, and cancer (biopsy if ≥45 years or with risk factors).

| Order | Drug | Dose | How it helps |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1st line** | **LNG-IUS** | 52 mg device, 5 years | Reduces loss by **~90%**; contraception too |
| Non-hormonal (take **only during the period**) | **Tranexamic acid** | **1 g orally 8-hourly for up to 4 days** (can be 1.5 g) | Antifibrinolytic: reduces loss by **~50%**. Avoid with **active VTE** |
| | **Mefenamic acid** | **500 mg orally 8-hourly** with food during menses | NSAID: reduces loss by **~25%** and relieves pain |
| Hormonal | COC, cyclical norethisterone, DMPA | As above | Useful if contraception is wanted |
| Surgical | Endometrial ablation, myomectomy, hysterectomy | | When drugs fail or with large fibroids |

> [!PEARL]
> Tranexamic acid + mefenamic acid can be combined, and neither is a contraceptive, so they suit a woman trying to conceive.

### Fertility drugs

| Drug | Mechanism | Dose | Key risks & monitoring |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Letrozole** (aromatase inhibitor), **first line in PCOS** | Less oestrogen, so less negative feedback, so **more FSH** | **2.5 mg PO daily, days 3–7** (or 2–6); up by 2.5 mg per cycle to max **7.5 mg** | Mostly **mono-follicular**; endometrium spared. Higher live birth rate than clomiphene. Off-label for fertility |
| **Clomiphene citrate** (SERM) | Blocks hypothalamic oestrogen receptors, so the brain senses low oestrogen and releases more GnRH and FSH | **50 mg PO daily, days 2–6**; up by 50 mg per cycle to max **150 mg** | **Multiple pregnancy ~5–10%**, **OHSS** (rare), hot flushes, **visual disturbance (stop the drug)**, thin endometrium. **Ultrasound follicle tracking at least in the first cycle**; **no more than 6 cycles** |
| **Metformin** | Improves insulin resistance, lowers insulin-driven ovarian androgens | Build up to **500 mg 8-hourly** (1.5 g/day) | Add in clomiphene resistance or obesity. GI upset, so start low |
| **Gonadotrophins** (hMG, recombinant FSH) + **hCG trigger** | Direct follicle stimulation | Daily SC injections under specialist care | Needs **close ultrasound ± oestradiol monitoring**; highest OHSS and multiples risk. For WHO group I (hypothalamic) anovulation, clomiphene failure, IVF |
| **Cabergoline** (dopamine agonist) | D2 agonist, so prolactin falls | Hyperprolactinaemia: **0.25 mg PO twice weekly**, titrate monthly | Nausea, dizziness, postural hypotension; valvulopathy at high doses. Also lowers OHSS risk in IVF |
| **Bromocriptine** | D2 agonist (older) | **1.25 mg PO at night** with food, up to 2.5 mg 8–12-hourly | More nausea than cabergoline. **Preferred if pregnancy is planned** (longest safety record) |

> [!REDFLAG]
> **OHSS** (ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome): abdominal distension, pain, vomiting, ascites, **haematocrit >45%**, oliguria and **VTE**, usually 3–10 days after the hCG trigger. Admit if severe: fluid balance, **enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily**, paracentesis for tense ascites, and **avoid NSAIDs**.

**Lactation suppression** (after stillbirth or neonatal death, or when the mother is advised not to breastfeed): **cabergoline 1 mg orally as a single dose within 24 h of birth** (if lactation is established: 0.25 mg 12-hourly for 2 days). **Avoid in hypertension and pre-eclampsia**. **Bromocriptine is no longer recommended postpartum** because of reports of **stroke, MI, seizures and psychosis**, especially in hypertensive women.

### HRT (brief)

- **Why:** vasomotor symptoms, and **premature ovarian insufficiency or surgical menopause** (for example after a TAH-BSO for benign disease), where HRT should continue **until about age 51** to protect bone and heart.
- **What:** oestrogen **plus a progestogen if she has a uterus** (unopposed oestrogen causes endometrial hyperplasia and cancer); **oestrogen alone after hysterectomy**. **Transdermal** oestrogen carries **no extra VTE risk**, while oral does.
- **Contraindications:** oestrogen-dependent cancer (breast, endometrial), **undiagnosed vaginal bleeding**, active or recent VTE or arterial disease, active liver disease, pregnancy. **Risks:** a small, duration-dependent rise in breast cancer with combined HRT; VTE and stroke with oral oestrogen. **HRT is not a contraceptive.**

**Ward pearls: contraception**
- A woman on **rifampicin** needs **DMPA or an IUD**, not a pill or implant; continue the alternative for **28 days** after rifampicin ends.
- "Can I get pregnant soon after stopping the injection?" Tell her the truth: **about 10 months on average** after the last dose. For fast return, choose an **implant or IUD**.
- A breastfeeding mother at the 6-week visit: implant, IUD, POP or DMPA, **not the COC** (MEC 3 until 6 months).
- A woman with **BP 160/100 or migraine with aura** can use **any progestogen-only method or an IUD**.
- For EC, the **copper IUD beats every pill** and works **up to 5 days**.

## Analgesia and anaesthesia in obstetrics

### Paracetamol

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Analgesic and antipyretic (not an NSAID) |
| **Mechanism** | Central COX inhibition; no anti-inflammatory or antiplatelet effect |
| **Indications** | First-line pain and fever at any gestation; postpartum and post-CS baseline analgesia |
| **Dose & route** | **1 g orally or IV every 6 h** (**max 4 g/day**); **15 mg/kg** per dose if weight **<50 kg** |
| **Side effects** | Rare at therapeutic doses. Overdose causes **hepatic necrosis** |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Severe liver disease, malnutrition, low body weight |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | **Safe in all trimesters and in breastfeeding**: the analgesic of choice. Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest time |
| **Monitoring/antidote** | Overdose: **N-acetylcysteine** |

### NSAIDs (ibuprofen, diclofenac, mefenamic acid, indomethacin)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Non-selective COX inhibitors |
| **Mechanism** | Block prostaglandin synthesis: analgesic, anti-inflammatory, antiplatelet |
| **Indications** | **Postpartum and post-CS pain** (with paracetamol), dysmenorrhoea, HMB (mefenamic acid). Indomethacin is used as a specialist tocolytic before 32 weeks |
| **Dose & route** | **Ibuprofen 400 mg orally 8-hourly** with food; **diclofenac 50 mg 8-hourly** orally, 75 mg IM 12-hourly, or **100 mg PR** at the end of a CS |
| **Side effects** | Dyspepsia and GI bleeding, renal impairment, bronchospasm, fluid retention |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Asthma** (NSAID-sensitive), peptic ulcer, **severe pre-eclampsia with renal impairment or oliguria**, **PPH with AKI or coagulopathy**, **dehydration/sepsis with oliguria**, thrombocytopenia |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | **Avoid from 20 weeks**: fetal **renal impairment and oligohydramnios** (US FDA 2020). **Contraindicated from 30 weeks**: **premature closure of the ductus arteriosus** (fetal pulmonary hypertension). Also linked to miscarriage around conception. **Safe in breastfeeding** (ibuprofen is preferred) |
| **Monitoring/antidote** | Urine output and creatinine in sick women; no antidote |

### Opioids in labour and after CS

| Drug | Dose | Onset / duration | Key points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pethidine** (still standard in Uganda) | **50–100 mg IM** (about 1 mg/kg), repeat **4-hourly**, max 2 doses in labour usually | 15–20 min / 2–4 h | Give with an **anti-emetic** (e.g. promethazine 25 mg IM or metoclopramide 10 mg IV). Metabolite **norpethidine** accumulates: neonatal respiratory depression and poor suckling, worst when born **2–4 h after the dose**. Avoid in renal failure and **seizure risk** (norpethidine is pro-convulsant) |
| **Morphine** | **5–10 mg IM** (0.1 mg/kg) 4-hourly, or titrated IV 1–2 mg | 20 min / 3–4 h | Better analgesia; post-CS first 24 h. Nausea, itch, sedation |
| **Tramadol** | **50–100 mg** orally/IM/IV 6–8-hourly (max 400 mg/day) | | Moderate pain. Lowers the seizure threshold; less respiratory depression |
| **Naloxone (mother)** | **0.4 mg IV**, repeat every 2–3 min | Short acting: re-dosing may be needed | For opioid respiratory depression (RR <10) or sedation |
| **Naloxone (baby)** | **0.1 mg/kg IM** (ventilate first) | | Only after airway and ventilation are secured. **Avoid if the mother is opioid-dependent** (neonatal withdrawal seizures) |

> [!REDFLAG]
> A baby born limp and apnoeic after maternal pethidine needs **ventilation with bag and mask first** (Helping Babies Breathe: within the Golden Minute). Naloxone is an adjunct after effective ventilation, not the first step.

> [!UGANDA]
> Epidurals are rarely available outside a few private and national referral units, so pethidine, **non-drug support** (a birth companion, mobility, massage, upright positions) and **TENS** (rare) are the realistic options. Offer analgesia: pain relief in labour is part of respectful maternity care.

### Lignocaine (lidocaine)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Amide local anaesthetic (also a class Ib anti-arrhythmic) |
| **Mechanism** | Blocks **voltage-gated sodium channels** in nerve membranes, so no action potential |
| **Indications** | **Episiotomy and perineal repair** (infiltration), **pudendal block** (for instrumental delivery or repair), cervical/paracervical block (MVA), diluting IM MgSO₄ and ceftriaxone |
| **Dose & route** | **1% = 10 mg/mL** (2% = 20 mg/mL). **Max plain: 3 mg/kg** (about **18–20 mL of 1% for 60 kg**). **Max with adrenaline: 7 mg/kg**. Episiotomy/repair: about **10 mL of 1%**. **Pudendal block: 10 mL of 1% each side** just below and behind the **ischial spine**, through the sacrospinous ligament |
| **Side effects** | Local pain; **local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)** if given intravascularly or in overdose |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | True amide allergy (rare); heart block. **Never use adrenaline-containing solution on end-arteries** (digits) or when IV injection is likely |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding |
| **Monitoring/antidote** | **Aspirate before every injection**; add up the total dose (including the lignocaine in MgSO₄ and any re-infiltration). **Antidote for LAST: 20% lipid emulsion** |

**LAST: recognise it early.**

| Stage | Features |
|---|---|
| Early CNS (warning) | **Circumoral numbness or tingling, metallic taste**, tinnitus, light-headedness, visual blurring, slurred speech, anxiety |
| Late CNS | Muscle twitching, **seizures**, reduced consciousness, coma |
| Cardiovascular | Hypertension and tachycardia at first, then **bradycardia, arrhythmias (VT/VF), hypotension, asystole** |

**Management of LAST (AAGBI, stepwise):**
1. **Stop injecting.** **Call for help** (anaesthetist).
2. **Airway**: 100% oxygen; ventilate if needed (hyperventilation helps: acidosis worsens toxicity). Left lateral tilt if still pregnant.
3. **Seizures**: **diazepam 10 mg IV** slowly (or midazolam). In Uganda MgSO₄ may also be to hand, but a benzodiazepine is first line.
4. **IV access**, monitor ECG and BP.
5. **Cardiac arrest**: start CPR (ACLS). Arrhythmias may be refractory; **prolonged CPR is justified** (lipid can take >1 h to work). Use smaller adrenaline doses (≤1 mcg/kg); **avoid lignocaine as an anti-arrhythmic** (use amiodarone).
6. **Give 20% lipid emulsion** (Intralipid) for cardiovascular instability or arrest: **1.5 mL/kg IV bolus over 1 min** (about 100 mL for 70 kg), then an **infusion of 15 mL/kg/h**. Repeat the bolus **up to twice at 5-min intervals** and double the infusion to **30 mL/kg/h** if still unstable. **Maximum cumulative 12 mL/kg**.

> [!PEARL]
> **Bupivacaine is far more cardiotoxic than lignocaine** (VF that is hard to reverse). Its max dose is **2 mg/kg**. Never use bupivacaine for IV regional block.

### Spinal anaesthesia and post-spinal hypotension

| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Hyperbaric ("heavy") bupivacaine 0.5%** | **2–2.5 mL (10–12.5 mg)** intrathecal at L3/4 or L4/5 ± fentanyl 10–25 mcg | Dextrose makes it heavier than CSF, so the block follows gravity (tilt changes the level) |
| **Phenylephrine** (first choice) | **50–100 mcg IV bolus**, or infusion | Pure α-agonist: reverses vasodilatation; less fetal acidosis than ephedrine. Causes reflex **bradycardia** |
| **Ephedrine** | **6 mg IV bolus**, repeat | α + β: use when **HR is low**. Crosses the placenta: fetal acidosis with large doses |

Post-spinal hypotension (the **commonest** complication) comes from **sympathetic block** (vasodilatation) plus **aortocaval compression**. Prevent and treat it with **left lateral tilt 15°**, a crystalloid **co-load of 500–1,000 mL**, and a vasopressor early (aim for SBP near baseline). Untreated, it causes maternal nausea, fainting and **fetal bradycardia/acidosis**.

### Ketamine

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Dissociative anaesthetic (NMDA antagonist) |
| **Mechanism** | Blocks NMDA receptors: analgesia and "dissociation"; sympathetic stimulation |
| **Indications** | Induction in **haemorrhagic shock** (supports BP); short procedures (**MVA, manual removal of placenta, repair**) where no anaesthetist is present; analgesia |
| **Dose & route** | **Induction 1–2 mg/kg IV**; analgesic doses 0.25–0.5 mg/kg IV |
| **Side effects** | **Raises BP and HR**, raised ICP, **emergence hallucinations** (reduce with diazepam), hypersalivation, laryngospasm |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | **Severe pre-eclampsia/eclampsia and hypertension** (BP surge, raised ICP), cardiac ischaemia, psychosis. Protective airway reflexes are **not guaranteed**: aspiration can still happen |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | Crosses the placenta; high doses increase uterine tone |
| **Monitoring/antidote** | SpO₂, BP, airway; no antidote |

### General anaesthesia (GA) in obstetrics

Pregnancy makes GA dangerous: **aspiration** (progesterone relaxes the lower oesophageal sphincter, delayed gastric emptying in labour, raised intra-abdominal pressure), **difficult intubation** (oedema, large breasts, weight), **rapid desaturation** (lower FRC, higher O₂ use), **awareness**, and **uterine atony** (volatile agents relax the uterus).

**Aspiration prophylaxis** before CS (especially under GA):

| Drug | Dose | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Omeprazole** | **40 mg IV** (or 40 mg orally the night before and morning of an elective CS) | Raises gastric pH |
| **Ranitidine** (H2 blocker) | 50 mg IV or 150 mg orally | Same; **withdrawn in many countries since 2020** (NDMA contamination), so use omeprazole or famotidine |
| **Metoclopramide** | **10 mg IV** | Speeds gastric emptying and raises lower oesophageal sphincter tone |
| **Sodium citrate 0.3 M** | **30 mL orally** just before induction | Immediately neutralises acid (non-particulate) |

Then **rapid sequence induction** (pre-oxygenation, thiopental or propofol, **suxamethonium**, cricoid pressure, cuffed tube). Remember **MgSO₄ potentiates non-depolarising muscle relaxants**.

**Ward pearls: analgesia and anaesthesia**
- Before any perineal repair, calculate the **maximum lignocaine volume** for her weight and say it aloud.
- A drowsy woman after pethidine with RR 8 → **naloxone 0.4 mg IV** and oxygen.
- The first sign of LAST is often the patient saying her **lips tingle** or she has a **metallic taste**.

## Other medical drugs in pregnancy

### Insulin

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Human insulin: **soluble** (Actrapid), **isophane/NPH** (Insulatard), **premixed 30/70** (Mixtard) |
| **Mechanism** | Replaces or supplements endogenous insulin. **Does not cross the placenta** |
| **Indications** | Pre-existing type 1 and type 2 diabetes; GDM not controlled on diet ± metformin; **start at once if fasting glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L** at diagnosis; DKA; labour (IV infusion) |
| **Dose & route** | SC. Start about **0.3–0.5 units/kg/day** (0.2–0.3 for mild GDM). **High fasting glucose → bedtime isophane 0.1–0.2 units/kg at 22:00**; high post-meal → pre-meal soluble 2–4 units. Titrate by **2 units every 2–3 days**. Labour: variable-rate IV insulin with dextrose, target **4–7 mmol/L** |
| **Side effects** | **Hypoglycaemia** (worst in the **first trimester** in type 1), weight gain, lipohypertrophy |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Requirements **rise sharply after 20–24 weeks**; **steroids** raise glucose for 3–5 days; a **fall of >15–20%** in late pregnancy may mean **placental insufficiency** |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | **The gold standard** in pregnancy and safe in breastfeeding. **Stop it at delivery in GDM**; in type 1 cut to pre-pregnancy doses at once |
| **Monitoring/antidote** | Capillary glucose (fasting, 1 h post-meal). **Hypo <4.0 mmol/L: 15–20 g fast sugar**, recheck in 15 min; unable to swallow: **IV 20% dextrose 75–100 mL** or **glucagon 1 mg IM** |

**Oral agents in GDM**:

| Drug | Dose | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Metformin** | **500 mg** with the evening meal, titrate to **2 g/day** | Oral, cheap, no hypoglycaemia, less maternal weight gain | GI upset; **crosses the placenta** (long-term child data reassuring but incomplete); stop if eGFR <30, sepsis, dehydration. **Dolutegravir raises metformin levels** (use a lower total dose and titrate carefully, e.g. many clinicians keep to about 1 g/day) |
| **Glibenclamide** | 2.5–5 mg daily, max 15–20 mg | Oral | **Crosses the placenta**: more **macrosomia and neonatal hypoglycaemia**. Second line only |

### Enoxaparin and heparins (thromboprophylaxis)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Class** | **LMWH** (enoxaparin); **unfractionated heparin** (UFH) |
| **Mechanism** | Activates **antithrombin**: LMWH mainly inhibits **factor Xa**; UFH inhibits Xa and thrombin (IIa) |
| **Indications** | Antenatal and postnatal VTE prophylaxis by risk score; after CS; OHSS; treatment of DVT/PE; antiphospholipid syndrome (with aspirin); mechanical valves (specialist) |
| **Dose & route** | Prophylaxis (by **booking weight**): **<50 kg 20 mg**; **50–90 kg 40 mg**; **91–130 kg 60 mg**; 131–170 kg 80 mg — **SC once daily**. **Treatment: 1 mg/kg SC 12-hourly**. **UFH prophylaxis: 5,000 IU SC 12-hourly**; treatment by IV infusion with APTT monitoring |
| **Side effects** | Bruising, bleeding, **heparin-induced thrombocytopenia** (rare with LMWH), osteoporosis (long-term UFH) |
| **Contraindications / cautions** | Active bleeding, **platelets <75 × 10⁹/L**, severe renal impairment (use UFH), uncontrolled hypertension |
| **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** | **Does not cross the placenta**: the anticoagulant of choice in pregnancy. **Safe in breastfeeding** |
| **Monitoring/antidote** | Platelets at baseline; anti-Xa in extremes of weight or renal disease. **Protamine** fully reverses UFH, only partly reverses LMWH |

**LMWH and regional anaesthesia (spinal/epidural):**

| Timing | Prophylactic dose | Treatment dose |
|---|---|---|
| Last dose **before** a spinal/epidural | **≥12 h** | **≥24 h** |
| First dose **after** a spinal or catheter removal | **≥4 h** | ≥4 h (often longer after surgery; senior decision) |
| Remove the epidural catheter | ≥12 h after the last dose | ≥24 h after the last dose |

> [!DRUG]
> Tell women on LMWH: "**If you think you are in labour or you bleed, do not inject; come in.**" That keeps the spinal option open.

**Warfarin**: crosses the placenta. **Warfarin embryopathy** with exposure at **6–12 weeks** (**nasal hypoplasia**, **stippled epiphyses** / chondrodysplasia punctata), later **CNS abnormalities** and **fetal intracranial haemorrhage** (especially at delivery). Switch to LMWH **before 6 weeks**. The exception is some women with **mechanical heart valves**, where a specialist may continue warfarin (fewer valve thromboses; dose ≤5 mg/day). **Warfarin is safe in breastfeeding**. **DOACs** (rivaroxaban, apixaban) are **avoided in pregnancy and breastfeeding**.

### Anti-emetics for nausea, vomiting and hyperemesis gravidarum

| Drug | Dose | Line | Key issues |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cyclizine** (antihistamine) | **50 mg PO/IM/IV 8-hourly** | 1st | Drowsiness; long safety record |
| **Promethazine** (antihistamine) | **25 mg PO/IM 8-hourly** | 1st | Widely available in Uganda; sedation |
| Prochlorperazine / chlorpromazine (phenothiazines) | 12.5 mg IM/IV 8-hourly; 10–25 mg PO 8-hourly | 1st | Extrapyramidal effects, sedation |
| **Metoclopramide** (D2 antagonist) | **10 mg PO/IM/IV 8-hourly**, max **5 days** | 2nd | **Extrapyramidal reactions / oculogyric crisis** (young women especially): stop and give **procyclidine 5–10 mg IV/IM** (or an anticholinergic available locally, e.g. biperiden) |
| **Ondansetron** (5-HT3 antagonist) | **4–8 mg PO/IV 8–12-hourly** | 2nd | Small **cleft lip/palate signal** with **first-trimester** use (about 3 extra per 10,000): counsel, but **do not withhold** if needed. Constipation, **QT prolongation** |
| Hydrocortisone → prednisolone | 100 mg IV 12-hourly, then **40–50 mg PO daily**, tapered | 3rd | Refractory cases only, specialist decision |

**Hyperemesis essentials (RCOG GTG 69):**
1. IV fluids: **0.9% sodium chloride with potassium chloride** (guided by daily U&E). **Avoid dextrose-only fluids at first.**
2. **Thiamine before any dextrose** (100 mg IV, as Pabrinex or plain thiamine, then **oral thiamine 25–50 mg 8-hourly** or 100 mg daily). Dextrose in a thiamine-depleted woman can precipitate **Wernicke's encephalopathy**: confusion, **ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus**, ataxia.
3. **Thromboprophylaxis with LMWH** while admitted (dehydration + immobility).
4. Check for **molar pregnancy and multiple pregnancy** (scan), UTI, thyroid (transient gestational thyrotoxicosis needs **no** antithyroid drugs).
5. Give an **H2 blocker or PPI** if there is reflux or gastritis.

### Antiepileptic drugs (AEDs)

| Drug | Pregnancy risk | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Sodium valproate** | **Highest**: major malformations about **10%** (**neural tube defects** 1–2%, cardiac, cleft, hypospadias); **neurodevelopmental problems in 30–40%** (lower IQ, autism) | **Avoid in girls and women who could become pregnant** unless nothing else works, under a pregnancy-prevention programme. Never stop suddenly in pregnancy: switch under specialist care |
| **Phenytoin** | **Fetal hydantoin syndrome**: craniofacial features, **nail and distal phalanx hypoplasia**, growth and developmental delay | Enzyme inducer (contraception failure) |
| **Carbamazepine** | NTD about **0.5–1%**, cleft, cardiac | Enzyme inducer |
| **Phenobarbital** | Cardiac defects, cleft; neonatal sedation/withdrawal | Still used in Uganda; enzyme inducer |
| **Lamotrigine** | **Among the safest** | **Levels fall by up to 50%** in pregnancy (more glucuronidation): check levels or clinically adjust; **reduce back after delivery** (toxicity) |
| **Levetiracetam** | **Among the safest** | Clearance rises in pregnancy; renal excretion |

> [!DRUG]
> Every woman with epilepsy on AEDs who might conceive needs **folic acid 5 mg daily**, ideally **3 months before conception** and through the first trimester. Also: **monotherapy at the lowest effective dose**, a detailed **anomaly scan**, and **vitamin K 1 mg IM for the baby** at birth (as for every newborn). A fit in pregnancy after 20 weeks is **eclampsia until proven otherwise**: give MgSO₄.

AEDs and **breastfeeding**: encourage it (most are compatible); watch the baby for **sedation and poor feeding** (phenobarbital, benzodiazepines, lamotrigine at high levels).

### Thyroid drugs

| Drug | Use | Dose | Pregnancy points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Levothyroxine** | Hypothyroidism | Usual dose, e.g. 50–150 mcg daily | **Increase by 25–30% as soon as pregnancy is confirmed** (e.g. take **2 extra doses per week**); the fetus depends on maternal T4 in the first trimester (brain development). Check TSH every **4–6 weeks**; target TSH **<2.5 mU/L**. Take on an empty stomach, apart from **iron and calcium**. Return to the pre-pregnancy dose after delivery |
| **Propylthiouracil (PTU)** | Hyperthyroidism **in the first trimester** | 50–150 mg 8-hourly (lowest effective dose) | Less teratogenic than carbimazole but **hepatotoxic**, so switch in the second trimester |
| **Carbimazole** / methimazole | Hyperthyroidism **from the second trimester** | 5–40 mg daily | **First-trimester embryopathy**: **aplasia cutis** (scalp defects), **choanal and oesophageal atresia**, omphalocele |

Both PTU and carbimazole cross the placenta (fetal goitre, hypothyroidism if over-treated): aim for **free T4 at the upper end of normal**. **Graves' disease with high TRAb** → watch for **fetal and neonatal thyrotoxicosis**. Both drugs are compatible with breastfeeding at modest doses.

### Galactagogues

**Domperidone** (D2 antagonist, raises prolactin) **10 mg orally 8-hourly** for about **7 days** (off-label) is used when supply stays low **after correcting attachment and feeding frequency**. It can prolong the **QT interval**: avoid in cardiac disease, with QT-prolonging drugs, or at high doses. Metoclopramide works similarly but causes depression and extrapyramidal effects. **Fix the latch before prescribing.**

**Ward pearls: medical drugs**
- GDM: diet first, then **metformin**, then **insulin**; stop them all at delivery and **check glucose** 6–13 weeks postpartum.
- A woman on **enoxaparin** who wants a spinal: when was the **last dose**? Prophylactic 12 h, treatment 24 h.
- **Thiamine before dextrose** in every vomiting, dehydrated pregnant woman.
- **Never stop an AED abruptly** because a woman is pregnant: uncontrolled seizures (especially SUDEP) are more dangerous than most drugs.

## Drug safety: teratogens, breastfeeding and pregnancy pharmacokinetics

### How timing matters

| Period (from conception) | Weeks from LMP | Effect of a harmful drug |
|---|---|---|
| **0–2 weeks** (pre-implantation) | 2–4 | "**All or nothing**": miscarriage or complete recovery |
| **3–8 weeks** (organogenesis) | 5–10 | **Structural malformations**: the critical window |
| **After 8 weeks** (fetal period) | >10 | **Growth, function** (renal, brain), and neonatal effects near term |

The **baseline** risk of a major congenital anomaly in any pregnancy is **2–3%**; quote risks as an increase over this.

### Teratogen table

| Drug | Effect | Critical window |
|---|---|---|
| **Warfarin** | **Embryopathy**: nasal hypoplasia, **stippled epiphyses**; later CNS defects, fetal haemorrhage | **6–12 weeks**; bleeding throughout, especially at delivery |
| **Sodium valproate** | **Neural tube defects** (spina bifida), cardiac, cleft, hypospadias, **neurodevelopmental delay/autism** | First trimester (NTD at 3–4 weeks post-conception); neurodevelopment throughout |
| **Phenytoin** | **Fetal hydantoin syndrome**: craniofacial, **hypoplastic nails/distal phalanges**, growth and mental delay | First trimester |
| **Carbamazepine** | **NTD**, craniofacial, cardiac | First trimester |
| **Isotretinoin** (and other retinoids) | **Retinoid embryopathy**: ear anomalies (microtia), craniofacial, **cardiac outflow**, CNS, thymus; high miscarriage | First trimester; stop **1 month before** conception (acitretin: 3 years) |
| **ACE inhibitors / ARBs** | **Fetal renal failure, oligohydramnios**, skull ossification defects (hypocalvaria), limb contractures, lung hypoplasia | **Second and third trimesters** (switch before or as soon as pregnancy is known) |
| **Tetracyclines** (doxycycline) | **Yellow-brown tooth discolouration**, enamel hypoplasia, inhibited bone growth | **After about 15 weeks** (teeth calcify) |
| **Methotrexate** | **Aminopterin syndrome**: skull ossification defects, craniofacial, limb; miscarriage | First trimester; stop **3 months before** conception (per older guidance; ask the specialist) |
| **Misoprostol** (failed abortion) | **Möbius syndrome** (facial and 6th/7th nerve palsy), **limb (transverse) defects** | First trimester (vascular disruption) |
| **Lithium** | **Ebstein's anomaly** (tricuspid valve displaced; absolute risk small); neonatal toxicity, **polyhydramnios**, diabetes insipidus | First trimester; toxicity at term |
| **Thalidomide** | **Phocomelia** (limb reduction), ear, cardiac, gut | **Days 20–36 after conception** |
| **Diethylstilbestrol (DES)** | Daughters: **clear-cell adenocarcinoma of the vagina**, vaginal adenosis, **T-shaped uterus**, cervical insufficiency | Before 18 weeks (historical, 1940s–1971) |
| **Alcohol** | **Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder**: smooth philtrum, thin upper lip, short palpebral fissures, microcephaly, FGR, learning problems | **Throughout**: no safe amount |
| **Statins** | Previously classed as teratogenic (weak evidence); FDA removed the contraindication in 2021 | Usually **stopped** before or in pregnancy (cholesterol is needed for fetal development; little benefit in 9 months) |
| **Carbimazole** | Aplasia cutis, choanal and oesophageal atresia | First trimester |
| **Aminoglycosides** (streptomycin, kanamycin) | **Fetal ototoxicity** (8th nerve deafness) | Throughout (single-dose or short gentamicin courses for sepsis are acceptable) |
| **Folate antagonists** (trimethoprim, high-dose SP) | NTD, cardiac | First trimester (cotrimoxazole for HIV prophylaxis is **continued**: the benefit outweighs the risk) |

> [!EXAM]
> Classic pairings: **Ebstein – lithium**; **Möbius – misoprostol**; **phocomelia – thalidomide**; **clear-cell vaginal carcinoma – DES**; **grey baby – chloramphenicol** (neonate); **kernicterus – sulphonamides** (near term, displace bilirubin); **stippled epiphyses – warfarin**; **aplasia cutis – carbimazole**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Dolutegravir**: an early (2018) Botswana signal of NTDs faded with more data (Tsepamo). WHO recommends **TLD for all, including women who may become pregnant**. Efavirenz is no longer considered teratogenic either. Keep women on effective ART; stopping it is the real danger to the baby.

### Breastfeeding: safe or not?

Most drugs pass into milk in tiny amounts. Worry more when the drug is **toxic in small doses**, the baby is **preterm or jaundiced**, or the drug **suppresses lactation**. **LactMed** (free online) is the reference.

| Generally compatible | Avoid or use with caution (why) |
|---|---|
| Paracetamol, **ibuprofen**, diclofenac | **Codeine** and **tramadol**: ultra-rapid metabolisers → infant opioid toxicity (codeine deaths reported; contraindicated in breastfeeding in the UK) |
| Penicillins, cephalosporins, **macrolides**, **metronidazole** (single 2 g dose: pause feeding for 12–24 h), **gentamicin**, nitrofurantoin (not if baby G6PD-deficient or <1 month jaundiced) | **Chloramphenicol** (grey baby syndrome, marrow suppression); **tetracyclines** long courses (short courses acceptable); **sulphonamides** in jaundiced, preterm or G6PD-deficient babies |
| Nifedipine, **methyldopa**, labetalol, amlodipine, **enalapril** | **Amiodarone** (iodine: neonatal hypothyroidism) |
| Heparin, **LMWH**, **warfarin** | **DOACs** (limited data) |
| Insulin, **metformin**, glibenclamide | **Lithium** (infant toxicity; specialist only), high-dose **aspirin** (Reye's syndrome risk) |
| **Levothyroxine**, PTU, carbimazole (modest doses) | **Radioactive iodine**; **cytotoxic chemotherapy**, methotrexate in cancer doses |
| **Sertraline**, most AEDs (lamotrigine, levetiracetam, carbamazepine) | **Benzodiazepines** repeated, phenobarbital (infant sedation, poor feeding) |
| **ARVs (TLD)**, cotrimoxazole, antimalarials (ACTs, quinine) | **Oestrogen-containing COCs** (early), **ergometrine**, **cabergoline**, **bromocriptine**: **reduce milk supply** |
| Progestogen-only contraception, IUDs, **LNG emergency contraception** | **Ulipristal** (express and discard for 1 week) |

### Pharmacokinetic changes in pregnancy

| Change | Mechanism | Effect on drugs | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **↑ Volume of distribution** | Plasma volume **+40–50%**, total body water +8 L, more fat | Lower peak levels of water-soluble drugs; may need bigger loading doses | Gentamicin, MgSO₄ (hence the big loading dose) |
| **↑ Renal clearance** | **GFR rises ~50%** from early pregnancy | Faster elimination of renally cleared drugs | **Levetiracetam**, lithium, penicillins, LMWH (weight-based dosing), digoxin |
| **↓ Albumin** (to ~28–37 g/L) | Haemodilution | More **free (active) drug** of highly protein-bound drugs; total levels mislead | **Phenytoin** (interpret free levels), valproate |
| **↑ Hepatic metabolism** of some drugs | Oestrogen/progesterone induce **CYP3A4, CYP2D6** and **UGT (glucuronidation)**; CYP1A2 falls | Lower levels | **Lamotrigine** (UGT: levels fall up to 50%), nifedipine, methadone. Caffeine and theophylline levels rise (CYP1A2 ↓) |
| **Delayed gastric emptying** and ↓ GI motility (progesterone), especially **in labour** | Smooth muscle relaxation; opioids | Slower, less predictable **oral absorption**; **aspiration risk** under GA | Give **IV/IM** routes in labour emergencies; aspiration prophylaxis |
| Placenta and fetus | Lipid-soluble, small (<500 Da), un-ionised, unbound drugs cross | Fetal exposure; **fetal ion trapping** in an acidotic fetus | Pethidine, local anaesthetics, benzodiazepines cross; **heparin and insulin do not** (large molecules) |

**After delivery** these changes reverse within days to weeks: doses that were **increased** in pregnancy (lamotrigine, levothyroxine, insulin) must be **reduced** again, or toxicity or hypoglycaemia follows.

### How to counsel a woman about a drug in pregnancy

1. **Ask first**: every woman of reproductive age, "Could you be pregnant? Are you planning a pregnancy? Are you breastfeeding?" Check LMP and do a urine hCG if unsure.
2. **Weigh both sides**: the risk of the drug **versus the risk of the untreated disease** (seizures, HIV transmission, depression, malaria, VTE, eclampsia). Untreated illness often harms the baby more than the drug.
3. **Use numbers**: "Every pregnancy has a **2–3%** chance of a major anomaly; this drug raises it to about X%." Absolute risks, not "doubles the risk".
4. **Choose well**: an older drug with a good safety record, **monotherapy**, the **lowest effective dose**, for the **shortest time**; avoid the critical window where possible.
5. **Plan ahead** (pre-conception): switch teratogens (valproate, warfarin, ACE inhibitors, isotretinoin, methotrexate) **before** conception; **folic acid** (400 mcg, or **5 mg** if on AEDs, previous NTD, diabetes, sickle cell); effective contraception until then.
6. **Never tell her to stop suddenly** without a plan; if she was **already exposed**, reassure where appropriate, arrange a **detailed anomaly scan** and document the counselling.
7. **Breastfeeding**: usually continue both breastfeeding and the drug; watch the baby for sedation, poor feeding or jaundice.

> [!UGANDA]
> Many women in Uganda take **herbal medicines** in pregnancy (including local preparations taken to "open the way" or speed labour) and buy drugs over the counter at drug shops. Ask about them specifically: uterotonic herbs cause **tachysystole, uterine rupture and fetal distress**, and some herbal products are hepatotoxic.

**Ward pearls: drug safety**
- The dangerous time is **weeks 3–8 after conception**: often **before she knows she is pregnant**. So ask every woman before you prescribe.
- **Heparin and insulin are big molecules and do not cross the placenta**, which is exactly why they are the drugs of choice in pregnancy.
- In breastfeeding, the drugs to remember are the ones that **dry up milk** (oestrogen, ergometrine, dopamine agonists) and the ones that **sedate or poison the baby** (codeine, lithium, chloramphenicol, chemotherapy).

## Management of drug emergencies on the labour ward

These are the drug-induced emergencies you must be able to recognise and start treating before the senior arrives.

### 1. Magnesium toxicity

1. **Stop** the infusion or withhold the next IM dose.
2. **ABC**: open the airway, **oxygen**, left lateral position; call the anaesthetist if RR <12 or SpO₂ is falling.
3. **Calcium gluconate 10%, 10 mL (1 g) IV slowly over 10 min** for respiratory depression, cardiac arrhythmia or severe drowsiness.
4. **Ventilate** (bag-valve-mask, then intubation) if apnoeic; calcium works only briefly.
5. Check **urine output, creatinine and magnesium level**; restart at a lower dose only once reflexes return and urine output is ≥30 mL/h.

### 2. Oxytocin hyperstimulation (tachysystole) with fetal distress

1. **Stop the oxytocin** (or remove any remaining vaginal misoprostol/dinoprostone).
2. Turn her onto her **left side**; give **oxygen** only if she is hypoxic; give an IV fluid bolus if hypotensive.
3. If the fetal heart does not recover: acute tocolysis with **terbutaline 0.25 mg SC** (or salbutamol per local protocol), and prepare for urgent delivery.
4. Restart oxytocin (at half the previous rate) only after the fetal heart is normal and contractions have settled, and after a senior review.

### 3. Water intoxication (oxytocin's antidiuretic effect)

Headache, vomiting, confusion, fits; sodium <130 mmol/L. **Stop oxytocin**, restrict fluids, use saline-based fluids for any further infusion, check sodium; fits → treat and seek senior/ICU help. Prevent it by running oxytocin in **normal saline or Ringer's**, not large volumes of 5% dextrose.

### 4. Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)

1. **Stop injecting**; call for help.
2. **ABC**, oxygen; treat fits with **diazepam 10 mg IV** (or MgSO₄ if eclampsia is possible).
3. **20% lipid emulsion**: **1.5 mL/kg IV bolus** over 1 min, then **15 mL/kg/h**; repeat the bolus up to twice at 5-min intervals if unstable (where available).
4. Cardiac arrest: CPR with left uterine displacement; consider perimortem caesarean after 4 min if ≥20 weeks.

### 5. Anaphylaxis (antibiotics, oxytocin, iron sucrose, blood)

**Adrenaline 0.5 mg IM** (0.5 mL of 1:1000) into the anterolateral thigh, repeat every 5 min if needed; lie her flat with **left lateral tilt**; high-flow oxygen; **1 L crystalloid bolus**; then chlorphenamine 10 mg IV and hydrocortisone 200 mg IV as adjuncts. Record the reaction and never re-challenge.

### 6. Neonatal respiratory depression after maternal opioids

Start **neonatal resuscitation first** (warm, dry, position the airway, ventilate with bag and mask). Naloxone **0.1 mg/kg IM** is only considered if the baby is breathing poorly **after** effective ventilation has restored heart rate and colour, and never if the mother is opioid-dependent.

### 7. Post-spinal hypotension

Left lateral tilt, fluid co-load, **phenylephrine** 50–100 µg IV boluses (or infusion) or **ephedrine 6 mg IV** boluses; treat a fall of >20% from baseline or SBP <90 mmHg to protect placental perfusion.

### 8. Hypoglycaemia (insulin, IV quinine, after steroids stopped)

Glucose <4 mmol/L (some units use <3.0): conscious → oral glucose; unconscious or unable to swallow → **50 mL of 50% dextrose IV** (or 100–150 mL of 10% dextrose), then a dextrose infusion and hourly glucose.

## Ward-round & exam pearls

**Model presentation:** "Mrs A, 30, P3, day 1 after caesarean for severe pre-eclampsia. She is on **MgSO₄ maintenance**, last dose at 06:00, due at 10:00: RR 18, reflexes present, urine 45 mL/h, so the dose can be given. BP is controlled on **nifedipine MR 20 mg 12-hourly**; methyldopa has been stopped because of the risk of postnatal depression. She had **ergometrine avoided** at delivery and received **oxytocin and TXA**. She is on **enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily** from 12 h after surgery. Plan: stop MgSO₄ at 24 h after delivery, continue BP checks, review in the postnatal clinic."

**Viva questions with answers**

1. *"What is the antidote to magnesium sulphate and how do you give it?"* Calcium gluconate 10%, 10 mL (1 g) IV slowly over 10 min, with airway support.
2. *"Why is diazepam no longer first line for eclampsia?"* The Collaborative Eclampsia Trial (1995) showed MgSO₄ reduced recurrent fits by about half versus diazepam and by about two-thirds versus phenytoin, with a trend towards fewer maternal deaths; Magpie (2002) showed prophylactic MgSO₄ halves the risk of eclampsia.
3. *"Which uterotonic would you avoid in a woman with asthma? And in pre-eclampsia?"* Carboprost (bronchospasm); ergometrine (vasoconstriction, hypertensive crisis, stroke).
4. *"Why not sublingual nifedipine?"* Rapid, uncontrolled BP fall → placental hypoperfusion and fetal distress, maternal myocardial or cerebral ischaemia.
5. *"Which antibiotic is avoided in PPROM and why?"* Co-amoxiclav: ORACLE trial showed increased necrotising enterocolitis in the newborn.
6. *"Name three drugs that interact with hormonal contraception."* Rifampicin, efavirenz (implants lose efficacy) and enzyme-inducing antiepileptics (carbamazepine, phenytoin). Dolutegravir does **not** interact.
7. *"What is the safest antimalarial in severe malaria in the first trimester?"* IV artesunate (WHO recommends it in all trimesters).
8. *"How long do antenatal steroids take to work?"* Maximum benefit from 24 h after the first dose to 7 days.

**OSCE tips**

- In a prescribing station, write **drug name (generic), dose, route, frequency, duration, date/time, your signature**, and state the monitoring.
- In a MgSO₄ station, **say the dilution out loud**: "8 mL of 50% (4 g) plus 12 mL water = 20 mL of 20%."
- Always ask about **allergies and asthma** first; examiners watch for it.

## Mnemonics & memory aids

- **MgSO₄ checks: "R-R-U"**: Respirations ≥16, Reflexes present, Urine ≥30 mL/h.
- **Magnesium levels climb "4-6-12"**: reflexes go at about **4–5**, breathing at about **6–7.5**, the heart at **>12 mmol/L**. (The knee goes before the lungs, the lungs before the heart.)
- **Ergometrine contraindications: "HHH + T"**: **H**ypertension/pre-eclampsia, **H**eart disease, **H**ands (Raynaud's/peripheral vascular disease), undelivered **T**win (and retained placenta).
- **Carboprost: "Carbo-PROST closes the chest"**: never in asthma.
- **Teratogens: "Warfarin Voices ACE TOM's Leaving"**: **W**arfarin, **V**alproate, **ACE** inhibitors/ARBs, **T**etracyclines, isotretin**O**in, **M**ethotrexate/misoprostol, **L**ithium.
- **Safe antibiotics in pregnancy: "Pregnant Cows Eat Many Nice Carrots"**: **P**enicillins, **C**ephalosporins, **E**rythromycin/azithromycin, **M**etronidazole, **N**itrofurantoin (not at term), **C**lindamycin.
- **Steroid numbers: "6-12-4, 12-24-2"**: dexamethasone **6 mg 12-hourly × 4**; betamethasone **12 mg 24-hourly × 2**.

### Classic exam traps

- **Trap:** "Give ergometrine for PPH in a woman with pre-eclampsia." **Right:** oxytocin, misoprostol and TXA; ergometrine is contraindicated.
- **Trap:** "Methyldopa for acute severe hypertension." **Right:** it takes hours to work; use nifedipine IR, hydralazine or labetalol.
- **Trap:** "Give the next MgSO₄ dose because it is due." **Right:** check R-R-U first; withhold if reflexes are absent, RR <16 or urine <30 mL/h.
- **Trap:** "Diazepam is an acceptable first-line anticonvulsant in eclampsia." **Right:** MgSO₄ is first line; diazepam only if MgSO₄ is unavailable.
- **Trap:** "Co-amoxiclav for PPROM." **Right:** erythromycin (co-amoxiclav increases NEC).
- **Trap:** "Folic acid 5 mg with SP." **Right:** high-dose folic acid antagonises SP; give 0.4 mg.
- **Trap:** "Misoprostol 800 µg to induce labour at term." **Right:** 25 µg; high doses cause tachysystole and uterine rupture.
- **Trap:** "Carboprost is the next step in an asthmatic with atonic PPH." **Right:** avoid carboprost (bronchospasm); use oxytocin, ergometrine (if normotensive), misoprostol, TXA, then mechanical/surgical measures.

### Questions seniors ask

1. **"What do you check before giving magnesium?"** Respiratory rate ≥16, patellar reflexes present, urine output ≥30 mL/h; and have calcium gluconate at the bedside.
2. **"She has lost her knee jerks. What now?"** Withhold MgSO₄, check RR, SpO₂ and urine output; if breathing is depressed, give calcium gluconate 1 g IV over 10 min and support ventilation.
3. **"Which uterotonic is first line and why?"** Oxytocin: effective, few contraindications, and short half-life so it can be titrated; ergometrine and prostaglandins are second line.
4. **"Why run oxytocin in saline rather than dextrose?"** Oxytocin has an antidiuretic (ADH-like) effect; large volumes of dextrose cause water intoxication and hyponatraemia.
5. **"When do you give antenatal steroids and when not?"** 24–34 weeks, birth likely within 7 days, no maternal infection, and adequate newborn care; not routinely ≥34 weeks.
6. **"What antihypertensive would you use after delivery in a breastfeeding mother?"** Nifedipine MR, amlodipine, labetalol or enalapril; stop methyldopa (postnatal depression).
7. **"Which contraception for a woman on rifampicin?"** Copper IUD, LNG-IUS or DMPA; avoid relying on implants, COC or POP (enzyme induction).
8. **"What is the maximum dose of lignocaine you can infiltrate?"** 3 mg/kg plain (about 18 mL of 1% for a 60 kg woman), 7 mg/kg with adrenaline.
