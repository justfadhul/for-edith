Knowing the pelvic diameters, the fetal skull landmarks and diameters, the supports and blood supply of the uterus, and the course of the ureter lets you diagnose position and CPD on vaginal examination, perform episiotomy, pudendal block and CS safely, and control haemorrhage.
highYield:
  - The pelvic inlet is widest transversely (about 13 cm) and the outlet is widest anteroposteriorly (about 13 cm), so the head enters transverse and rotates to occipito-anterior. The interspinous diameter (about 10 cm) is the narrowest and is the reference for station (0).
  - Obstetric conjugate (about 10.5–11 cm) ≈ diagonal conjugate (about 12.5 cm) minus 1.5 cm. If you can reach the sacral promontory on VE, the inlet is contracted.
  - A well-flexed vertex presents the suboccipitobregmatic diameter (9.5 cm). Deflexed or OP gives occipitofrontal (11.5 cm), brow gives mentovertical (13–13.5 cm, cannot deliver vaginally) and face gives submentobregmatic (9.5 cm).
  - Anterior fontanelle (bregma) is diamond-shaped with 4 sutures and closes at about 18 months. Posterior fontanelle (lambda) is Y-shaped with 3 sutures and closes at about 6–8 weeks. On VE, the posterior fontanelle near the symphysis means OA.
  - The uterine artery (from the anterior division of the internal iliac) crosses ABOVE the ureter about 1.5–2 cm lateral to the cervix ('water under the bridge'), which is the commonest site of ureteric injury at hysterectomy and CS.
  - The main uterine supports are the cardinal (transverse cervical/Mackenrodt's) and uterosacral ligaments plus levator ani. The round and broad ligaments are not significant supports.
  - The pudendal nerve (S2–S4) passes behind the ischial spine and supplies the perineum. Uterine pain in the first stage travels via T10–L1.
  - The ovarian artery comes from the aorta (L2). The right ovarian vein drains into the IVC and the left into the left renal vein. Ovarian lymph drains to the para-aortic nodes.
  - Engagement means the biparietal diameter (9.5 cm) has passed the brim, which is 2/5 or less of the head palpable abdominally. Abdominal fifths cannot be fooled by caput, so believe the abdomen over the VE station.
  - Moulding is graded 0 to +3. Moulding +3 (bones overlapping and irreducible) with a high head (3/5 or more palpable) means CPD or obstruction, so do a CS, not oxytocin or ventouse.
  - Clinical pelvimetry is adequate if the sacral promontory is not reached, the sacrum is hollow, the spines are not prominent, the subpubic angle admits 2 fingers and the intertuberous diameter admits 4 knuckles.
  - The best evidence of an adequate pelvis is a previous vaginal delivery of a good-sized baby. X-ray pelvimetry is not recommended routinely.

## In a nutshell

Obstetrics is applied anatomy. Every labour is a meeting between the **passenger** (the fetal head: its diameters, sutures, fontanelles and ability to mould) and the **passage** (the bony pelvis and soft tissues), driven by the **powers** (the uterus). When you do a vaginal examination in labour you are reading anatomy with your fingers: which suture, which fontanelle, how much moulding, where the ischial spines are.

In Uganda, where **obstructed labour** still causes uterine rupture, fistula and maternal death, and where many CS and repairs are done by junior doctors at HC IVs, this anatomy is practical, not academic. It tells you **when a head will not fit**, **where the ureter is** when you clamp the uterine artery, and **where to inject** a pudendal block.

The one thing never to forget: **the head enters the pelvis transverse, rotates at the level of the spines, and must present its smallest diameter (9.5 cm, a well-flexed head) to get through.** Deflexion or a brow means trouble.

## Definitions & classification

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **True pelvis** | The bony canal below the pelvic brim (linea terminalis). It is the birth canal |
| **False pelvis** | The part above the brim, between the iliac blades. It supports the gravid uterus but has no obstetric importance |
| **Pelvic inlet (brim)** | Bounded by the sacral promontory, alae of the sacrum, arcuate/iliopectineal lines, pectineal lines and upper border of the symphysis |
| **Mid-pelvis (plane of least dimensions)** | At the level of the **ischial spines**. It is the narrowest part |
| **Pelvic outlet** | A diamond made of two triangles: the anterior (pubic arch and ischial tuberosities) and posterior (tuberosities, sacrotuberous ligaments and tip of the coccyx/sacrum) |
| **Station** | Level of the lowest bony part of the fetal head relative to the **ischial spines** (0 = at the spines; −1 to −3 above; +1 to +3 below, in cm) |
| **Engagement** | The **widest transverse diameter** (biparietal, 9.5 cm) has passed through the pelvic brim. Abdominally, the head is **2/5 or less palpable**; vaginally, the leading part is at or near the spines |
| **Moulding** | Overlapping of the skull bones at the sutures under pressure. Graded 0 to +3 |
| **Caput succedaneum** | Oedema of the scalp over the presenting part. It crosses suture lines |
| **Attitude** | Relationship of fetal parts to each other (flexion or deflexion of the head) |
| **Denominator** | The part of the presentation used to name position: **occiput** (vertex), **sacrum** (breech), **mentum** (face), **acromion** (shoulder) |

## Epidemiology & Uganda context

- **Obstructed labour and CPD** remain major causes of maternal death, **uterine rupture** and **obstetric fistula** in Uganda. Contributing factors are adolescent pregnancy (an immature pelvis), short stature from childhood undernutrition, late referral and unsupervised home births.
- **Obstetric fistula**: Uganda has an estimated tens of thousands of women living with unrepaired fistula. Fistula follows prolonged pressure of the fetal head on the bladder or rectum against the pubic bones, which causes ischaemic necrosis. This is anatomy at its most tragic.
- **Rickets** and pelvic fractures (road traffic accidents, boda boda injuries) cause occasional contracted pelvis.
- **Female genital mutilation** is practised in a few communities in eastern and north-eastern Uganda (e.g. among the Sebei and Pokot) and scars the vulva and perineum.
- Many Ugandan HC IVs rely on medical officers to perform CS, so **safe surgical anatomy** (lower segment, bladder, uterine vessels, ureters) is a core competence.

## Anatomy

### 1. The bony pelvis

**Bones:** two innominate (hip) bones (ilium, ischium and pubis fused at the acetabulum), the **sacrum** (5 fused vertebrae) and the **coccyx**. **Joints:** the symphysis pubis, two sacroiliac joints and the sacrococcygeal joint. These all relax slightly in pregnancy (relaxin), and the coccyx moves backwards in the second stage.

**Obstetric diameters (average female pelvis):**

| Plane | Anteroposterior | Oblique | Transverse | Widest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Inlet (brim)** | **11 cm** (obstetric/true conjugate) | 12 cm | **13 cm** | **Transverse** |
| **Mid-cavity** | 12 cm | 12 cm | 12 cm | Round |
| **Mid-pelvis (spines)** | about 11.5–12 cm | – | **Interspinous about 10 cm** | Narrowest part |
| **Outlet** | **13 cm** (with the coccyx pushed back) | 12 cm | **11 cm** (intertuberous) | **Anteroposterior** |

**The conjugates of the inlet:**

| Conjugate | From | To | Length | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Anatomical (true) conjugate** | Sacral promontory | Upper border of the symphysis | about 11 cm | – |
| **Obstetric conjugate** | Sacral promontory | **Nearest point on the posterior surface of the symphysis** | about **10.5–11 cm** | The **smallest AP diameter the head must pass**; cannot be measured clinically |
| **Diagonal conjugate** | Sacral promontory | **Lower border** of the symphysis | about **12.5 cm** | **Measured on VE.** Obstetric conjugate ≈ diagonal conjugate − 1.5 cm |

```
            INLET viewed from above (gynaecoid)
                   Symphysis (front)
                ______________________
              /                        \
             /     AP (true conj.) 11   \
            |  <------ transverse 13 --> |
             \          ^               /
              \_________|______________/
                  Sacral promontory (back)

 The inlet is widest side-to-side, so the head ENTERS TRANSVERSE (OT).
 The outlet is widest front-to-back, so the head EXITS OA (after rotation).
```

**Axis of the birth canal (curve of Carus):** the canal is J-shaped. It runs **downwards and backwards** in the upper part, then curves **forwards** around the pubic symphysis. This is why you pull **downwards first, then forwards/upwards** during a vacuum or forceps delivery.

**Pelvic types (Caldwell–Moloy):**

| Type | Frequency (approximate) | Inlet shape | Features | Labour implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Gynaecoid** | about 50% | Round | Wide subpubic angle (**about 90°** or more), blunt spines, wide sacrosciatic notch | Ideal |
| **Android** | about 20–30% | Heart-shaped | Narrow subpubic angle, prominent spines, narrow fore-pelvis | **Deep transverse arrest**, OP positions, outlet problems |
| **Anthropoid** | about 20–25% (commoner in African women in some studies) | Long AP oval | Long AP, narrow transverse | **Direct OP** delivery ("face to pubes") is possible |
| **Platypelloid** | about 3–5% | Flat, wide oval | Short AP | Head engages transverse and may fail to engage; **transverse arrest at the inlet** |

### 2. The pelvic floor and perineum

- **Pelvic diaphragm:** **levator ani** (pubococcygeus, puborectalis, iliococcygeus) plus **coccygeus**. It supports the pelvic organs, and in labour its gutter-shaped slope **encourages the leading part to rotate anteriorly** (the occiput to the front).
- **Nerve supply:** the **pudendal nerve (S2–S4)** and direct branches of S3–S4.
- **Urogenital diaphragm (perineal membrane)** with the superficial perineal muscles: bulbospongiosus, ischiocavernosus and superficial transverse perineal.
- **Perineal body (central tendon):** a fibromuscular node between the vagina and anus where bulbospongiosus, the superficial and deep transverse perineal muscles, the external anal sphincter and fibres of levator ani meet. It is **torn or cut in episiotomy** and must be carefully repaired to prevent prolapse and dyspareunia.
- **Anal sphincter complex:** the **external anal sphincter** (striated, voluntary) and **internal anal sphincter** (smooth, involuntary). Tears involving them are **3rd degree** (3a: less than 50% of the EAS; 3b: more than 50% of the EAS; 3c: EAS and IAS). A **4th degree** tear includes the anorectal mucosa.

**Episiotomy:** a **mediolateral** incision at **about 60° from the midline** at crowning (it avoids extension into the anal sphincter). It cuts the vaginal epithelium, perineal skin, bulbospongiosus, superficial transverse perineal muscle and sometimes fibres of levator ani.

### 3. The external genitalia (vulva)

Mons pubis, labia majora and minora, clitoris, vestibule (urethral and vaginal openings), **Bartholin's glands** (at 4 and 8 o'clock, where abscesses form), and the hymen. **Blood supply:** the internal pudendal artery (and external pudendal branches of the femoral artery). **Lymph drainage: superficial inguinal nodes**, which is why vulval cancer spreads to the groin.

### 4. The vagina

- A fibromuscular tube of about **7–8 cm on the anterior wall and 9–10 cm on the posterior wall**, directed upwards and backwards.
- The cervix projects into its vault, creating the **anterior, posterior and two lateral fornices**. The **posterior fornix is related to the pouch of Douglas** (rectouterine pouch), the lowest point of the peritoneal cavity, where blood and pus collect (culdocentesis, colpotomy).
- **Relations:** anteriorly the bladder and urethra (vesicovaginal and urethrovaginal fistula); posteriorly the pouch of Douglas, rectum and perineal body (rectovaginal fistula); laterally the ureters near the lateral fornices, and levator ani.
- **Epithelium:** non-keratinised stratified squamous with no glands. It is kept acidic (pH about 4.5) by **lactobacilli** (oestrogen-dependent glycogen).
- **Blood supply:** the vaginal artery (internal iliac) and branches of the uterine, middle rectal and internal pudendal arteries.
- **Lymph:** the upper third drains to the internal and external iliac nodes, the middle third to the internal iliac nodes and the lower third to the superficial inguinal nodes.

### 5. The uterus

- A pear-shaped, thick-walled muscular organ: about **7.5 × 5 × 2.5 cm**, **50–70 g** in the non-pregnant nulliparous woman.
- **Parts:** fundus, body (corpus), isthmus (the **lower uterine segment** in late pregnancy) and cervix (about 2.5–3 cm, with **internal and external os**). The body-to-cervix ratio is 2:1 in adults and 1:2 in prepubertal girls.
- **Position:** usually **anteverted** (the long axis of the cervix is tilted forwards relative to the vagina, about 90°) and **anteflexed** (the body is bent forwards on the cervix, about 170°). About 15–20% of women have a retroverted uterus.
- **Layers:** endometrium (functional and basal layers), **myometrium** (three layers; the **middle layer has interlacing "figure-of-eight" fibres around blood vessels, the "living ligatures"** that stop bleeding after placental separation), and perimetrium (peritoneum).
- **Peritoneal relations:** in front, the **uterovesical pouch** (opened at CS to push the bladder down). Behind, the **rectouterine pouch (of Douglas)**.

**Lower uterine segment (LUS) vs upper segment:**

| Feature | Upper segment | Lower segment |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Body of the uterus | Isthmus (forms from about 28 weeks; fully formed in labour) |
| Muscle | Thick, active, **contracts and retracts** | Thin, passive, stretches |
| Peritoneum | Firmly attached | **Loosely attached** (so the bladder is easy to reflect) |
| Clinical | Classical CS incision; bleeds more, heals worse, higher rupture risk (about 4–9%) | **Transverse lower segment CS**: less bleeding, stronger scar (rupture risk about 0.5–1%) |

> [!PEARL]
> In **obstructed labour**, the upper segment keeps retracting and the lower segment thins out. The junction rises as a visible groove, the **Bandl's ring (pathological retraction ring)**, which is a sign of **impending uterine rupture**. Do not confuse it with a full bladder.

### 6. Uterine supports

| Structure | Anatomy | Importance |
|---|---|---|
| **Cardinal (transverse cervical, Mackenrodt's) ligaments** | From the cervix and upper vagina to the lateral pelvic wall. Contain the uterine vessels | **Principal support** of the uterus |
| **Uterosacral ligaments** | From the posterior cervix, around the rectum, to the sacrum | Hold the cervix back (keep the uterus anteverted). Main support |
| **Pubocervical ligaments/fascia** | Cervix to the pubis beneath the bladder | Support; damage causes cystocele |
| **Levator ani / pelvic floor** | See above | **Dynamic support**. Damaged in childbirth, causing prolapse |
| **Round ligaments** | Uterine cornu, through the inguinal canal, to the labia majora | Help keep anteversion. **Not a true support**. Stretching causes "round ligament pain" in pregnancy |
| **Broad ligaments** | Double fold of peritoneum from the uterus to the lateral pelvic wall | Contain the tubes, round and ovarian ligaments, vessels and ureter at the base. **Not a support**. Site of broad ligament haematoma |

### 7. Fallopian tubes

About **10 cm** long, running in the upper free border of the broad ligament. The parts from medial to lateral are:

1. **Interstitial (intramural)**: within the uterine wall. An ectopic here ruptures late and bleeds heavily.
2. **Isthmus**: narrow. An ectopic here ruptures early. It is the site of tubal ligation.
3. **Ampulla**: the widest and longest part, the **site of fertilisation** and the **commonest site of ectopic pregnancy (about 70–80%)**.
4. **Infundibulum with fimbriae**: opens into the peritoneal cavity (so PID spreads to the peritoneum, and hydrosalpinx forms when it is blocked).

### 8. The ovaries

- Almond-sized (about 3 × 2 × 1 cm), in the **ovarian fossa** on the lateral pelvic wall, attached to the back of the broad ligament by the **mesovarium**, to the uterus by the **ovarian ligament**, and to the pelvic wall by the **infundibulopelvic (suspensory) ligament**, which carries the ovarian vessels.
- **Relations:** the obturator nerve lies in the lateral pelvic wall behind the ovary, so **ovarian pathology can refer pain to the medial thigh**. The ureter runs behind the ovary.
- **Blood supply:** the **ovarian artery from the abdominal aorta at L2**. Venous drainage: the **right ovarian vein to the IVC** and the **left ovarian vein to the left renal vein** (the same left–right asymmetry as the testicular veins in men).
- **Lymph: para-aortic nodes** at L2 (following the vessels), not the pelvic nodes.

### 9. Blood supply of the pelvis: the essentials

- **Internal iliac artery, anterior division:** **uterine artery**, vaginal, obturator, internal pudendal, inferior gluteal, umbilical/superior vesical, and middle rectal arteries.
- **Uterine artery:** runs medially in the base of the broad ligament (within the cardinal ligament), **crosses ABOVE (anterior to) the ureter about 1.5–2 cm lateral to the cervix at the level of the internal os**, then ascends tortuously along the side of the uterus to **anastomose with the ovarian artery**. Its branches are the **arcuate, radial and spiral arteries**. The spiral arteries are remodelled by trophoblast in pregnancy; this remodelling fails in pre-eclampsia.
- **Internal iliac artery ligation** (anterior division), done for intractable PPH, reduces pulse pressure (it does not fully stop flow, because of collaterals).

> [!REDFLAG]
> **"Water under the bridge"**: the ureter (water) passes **under** the uterine artery (bridge) just lateral to the cervix. When clamping the uterine vessels at CS hysterectomy, or placing haemostatic sutures for lateral extensions of a CS incision, **stay close to the uterus and push the bladder down** to avoid ureteric ligation or injury. Also watch the ureter at the **pelvic brim** (crossing the bifurcation of the common iliac artery) and near the **infundibulopelvic ligament** when clamping ovarian vessels.

### 10. Nerve supply and pain pathways of labour

| Stage | Pain source | Nerves | Analgesia target |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First stage** | Uterine contractions and cervical dilatation | Visceral afferents with sympathetic fibres to **T10–L1** | Epidural or spinal (T10–L1), paracervical block |
| **Second stage** | Stretching of the vagina, perineum and pelvic floor | **Pudendal nerve (S2–S4)** | Pudendal block, perineal infiltration, epidural/spinal extending to the sacral segments |

**Pudendal nerve course:** it leaves the pelvis through the **greater sciatic foramen**, passes **behind the ischial spine and sacrospinous ligament**, re-enters through the **lesser sciatic foramen** into the **pudendal (Alcock's) canal**, and gives the inferior rectal nerve, perineal nerve and dorsal nerve of the clitoris. For a **transvaginal pudendal block**, palpate the ischial spine and inject just **medial and posterior to the tip of the spine**, through the sacrospinous ligament, after aspirating (the internal pudendal vessels are adjacent).

### 11. Lymphatic drainage: a summary for oncology

| Organ | Drains to |
|---|---|
| Vulva and lower third of the vagina | **Superficial inguinal** nodes, then deep inguinal and external iliac |
| Cervix and upper vagina | **Parametrial, obturator, internal and external iliac**, then common iliac and para-aortic |
| Uterine body | Iliac and **para-aortic** nodes (the fundus drains along the ovarian vessels; some drainage via the round ligament to the inguinal nodes) |
| Ovary and tube | **Para-aortic** nodes |

### 12. The fetal skull

**Bones of the vault:** two **frontal**, two **parietal**, two temporal (squamous parts) and one **occipital** bone. At birth they are not fused, which allows **moulding**.

**Sutures:**

| Suture | Between | Clinical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Sagittal** | The two parietal bones | **The main landmark on VE**: its direction shows whether the head is transverse, oblique or AP |
| **Frontal (metopic)** | The two frontal bones | Leads to the anterior fontanelle |
| **Coronal** | Frontal and parietal bones | Forms the lateral arms of the anterior fontanelle |
| **Lambdoid** | Parietal and occipital bones | Forms the arms of the posterior fontanelle |

**Fontanelles:**

| Feature | **Anterior fontanelle (bregma)** | **Posterior fontanelle (lambda)** |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | **Diamond (kite)** | **Triangle ("Y")** |
| Number of sutures | **4** (frontal, sagittal, 2 coronal) | **3** (sagittal, 2 lambdoid) |
| Size | Large (about 3 × 2 cm) | Small |
| Closes | About **18 months** | About **6–8 weeks** |
| On VE | Felt when the head is **deflexed** (OP, brow) | Felt when the head is **well flexed** |

**Regions of the skull:** **vertex** (the area between the two fontanelles and the parietal eminences), **sinciput/brow** (in front of the anterior fontanelle, up to the root of the nose), **face** (root of the nose to the chin), **occiput** (behind the posterior fontanelle).

**Fetal skull diameters: the heart of this lecture**

| Diameter | From → to | Length | Presentation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Suboccipitobregmatic** | Below the occipital protuberance → centre of the anterior fontanelle | **9.5 cm** | **Vertex, well flexed** (normal) |
| **Suboccipitofrontal** | Below the occipital protuberance → centre of the forehead (sinciput) | **10 cm** | Vertex, partially flexed |
| **Occipitofrontal** | Root of the nose (glabella) → most prominent point of the occiput | **11.5 cm** | **Deflexed vertex / OP** |
| **Mentovertical** | Point of the chin → highest point of the vertex | **13–13.5 cm** (some books give 14) | **Brow**: the largest; **no vaginal delivery at term** |
| **Submentovertical** | Junction of the chin and neck → highest point of the vertex | **11.5 cm** | Face, partially extended |
| **Submentobregmatic** | Junction of the chin and neck → centre of the anterior fontanelle | **9.5 cm** | **Face, fully extended** (mento-anterior can deliver) |

| Transverse diameter | Between | Length |
|---|---|---|
| **Biparietal** | The two parietal eminences | **9.5 cm** (the widest transverse diameter; engagement means it has passed the brim) |
| **Bitemporal** | The furthest points of the coronal suture | **8 cm** |
| Bimastoid | Tips of the mastoid processes | 7.5 cm |

```
  ATTITUDE vs PRESENTING DIAMETER

  Well flexed vertex  ── suboccipitobregmatic ── 9.5 cm   ✔ easy
  Partly flexed       ── suboccipitofrontal ──── 10  cm
  Deflexed / OP       ── occipitofrontal ─────── 11.5 cm  (long labour)
  Brow                ── mentovertical ───────── 13–13.5 cm ✘ obstructs
  Face (extended)     ── submentobregmatic ──── 9.5 cm   ✔ if mento-anterior

 (The more the head extends, the bigger the diameter, until full
  extension (face) makes it small again.)
```

**Moulding and caput:**

- **Moulding** means the parietal bones overlap the occipital and frontal bones, and one parietal bone overlaps the other at the sagittal suture. It **reduces the engaging diameter by up to about 1 cm**. Grades on the partograph:

| Grade | Finding |
|---|---|
| 0 | Bones separated; sutures easily felt |
| +1 | Bones just touching |
| +2 | Bones overlapping but can be separated with finger pressure |
| +3 | Bones overlapping and **cannot be separated**, which suggests **CPD** |

- **Caput succedaneum**: diffuse oedema of the scalp, **crosses suture lines**, present at birth, resolves in days. Large caput plus +3 moulding in a poorly progressing labour means **obstruction**.
- **Cephalhaematoma**: subperiosteal bleed, **does not cross sutures** (limited by the periosteum), appears hours after birth, resolves over weeks, and can cause jaundice.
- **Subgaleal haemorrhage**: a bleed under the aponeurosis that crosses sutures and can cause **hypovolaemic shock** in the newborn. It is associated with vacuum delivery.

**Intracranial membranes:** the falx cerebri and tentorium cerebelli. Excessive or abnormal moulding (especially rapid, as in precipitate breech delivery of the aftercoming head) can **tear the tentorium**, rupturing the great cerebral vein (of Galen) and causing intracranial haemorrhage.

## Clinical acumen

### Focused history (anatomy-relevant)

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Height and childhood illness** (rickets, polio, TB spine) | Short stature (below 150 cm) and bony deformity point to a contracted pelvis |
| **Previous pelvic fracture** (road traffic accident) | Distorted pelvis |
| **Previous labours**: length, instrumental delivery, birth weights, stillbirths from obstructed labour | **The best pelvimeter is a previous vaginal delivery of a good-sized baby**. A previous obstructed labour suggests CPD |
| **Previous CS**: why and what type of incision (classical or lower segment) | Scar rupture risk depends on the uterine anatomy |
| **Previous 3rd/4th degree tear, fistula or FGM** | Plan the mode of delivery and repair |
| **Leaking urine or faeces after a previous delivery** | Fistula (vesicovaginal or rectovaginal) |

### Examination

**Abdominal (in labour):** fifths of the head palpable above the brim (engagement). A **Bandl's ring**, a distended bladder and a tense tender lower segment are signs of obstruction.

**Vaginal examination: read the head**

1. Cervix: dilatation, effacement, application to the head.
2. Membranes: intact or ruptured; colour of the liquor.
3. **Presenting part:** vertex (smooth, sutures, fontanelles), face (mouth, nose, malar bones, gums) or breech (anus, sacrum, ischial tuberosities, genitalia).
4. **Position:** follow the **sagittal suture** to a fontanelle.
   - The **posterior fontanelle** (small, 3 sutures, Y) **anterior** means **OA** (good).
   - The **anterior fontanelle** (large, 4 sutures, diamond) **easily felt** means **deflexion** (OP or brow).
   - Sagittal suture **transverse** means OT (normal at the inlet; a **deep transverse arrest** if it is still transverse at or below the spines in the second stage).
5. **Station** relative to the ischial spines.
6. **Moulding** (0 to +3) and **caput**.
7. **Clinical pelvimetry:** can you reach the **sacral promontory** (a normal pelvis: not reached, diagonal conjugate over 12 cm)? The sacral curve (hollow or flat)? Are the **ischial spines prominent**? Sacrospinous notch width (2 fingers or more)? **Subpubic angle** (2 fingers or more, about 90°)? **Intertuberous diameter** (a closed fist, 4 knuckles, about 8.5 cm or more)?

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> **Signs of obstructed labour / CPD, so call a senior and prepare for CS:**
> - Head still **3/5 or more palpable** abdominally with a fully dilated cervix
> - **Moulding +3** and large caput
> - **Cervix oedematous**, poorly applied, dilatation stalls (crosses the action line on the partograph)
> - **Bandl's ring**, haematuria (bladder compression), maternal tachycardia, dehydration, fever
> - A **brow presentation** (anterior fontanelle, supraorbital ridges and nose felt) at term
> - Fetal distress

**Beware:** a large caput may reach the spines while the bony head is still high, giving a **false impression of descent**. Always correlate the VE with the **abdominal fifths**.

### Thinking like a clinician

- **Pelvis + head + powers:** before saying "CPD", ask whether the **powers** are adequate (augment if not, provided there is no obstruction), whether the **position** is right (OP and deflexion enlarge the diameter), and only then whether the passage is too small.
- **Anatomy predicts the mechanism:** the head enters **transverse** (widest inlet diameter), **flexes** (so 9.5 cm presents), **descends**, **rotates internally** at the spines/pelvic floor to OA (the outlet is widest AP), **extends** under the symphysis, **restitutes**, and **rotates externally**, then the shoulders are delivered.
- **Previous vaginal delivery of a 3.5 kg baby** is better evidence of an adequate pelvis than any X-ray pelvimetry (which is not recommended routinely).
- **Pitfall:** calling OP "OA" because the fontanelles were confused. Count the sutures meeting at the fontanelle (**3 = posterior**, **4 = anterior**), and feel for the size.
- **Pitfall:** pushing a brow presentation through with oxytocin. It will not deliver: 13 cm will not pass a 10–11 cm pelvis.
- **Surgery:** at CS, reflect the **bladder** down from the lower segment and make the incision **transverse in the LUS**. For lateral extensions, **place the fingers under the broad ligament and identify the ureter**.

## Clinical workup

The applied workup of this topic is the **"passage and passenger" assessment**: can this head get through this pelvis? You do it on every woman admitted in labour, again whenever progress is slow, and always before an instrumental delivery or augmentation. It is also the workup that tells you **when to stop and do a caesarean section** before the uterus ruptures or a fistula forms.

### Step 0: First 5 minutes

On arrival in labour (or when called to a "slow labour"):

1. **Vital signs:** pulse, BP, temperature, RR. A woman in **obstructed labour** is often **tachycardic, dehydrated (dry tongue, ketones), febrile** and exhausted.
2. **Fetal heart** with Doppler or Pinard (110–160 bpm), listening through and after a contraction.
3. **Quick abdominal look and feel** for the emergencies that change everything:
   - **Bandl's ring**, a tense tender lower segment, a distended bladder → **obstructed labour**.
   - Sudden pain then contractions stop, fetal parts easily felt, shock → **uterine rupture**.
   - Transverse lie with ruptured membranes or a prolapsed arm or cord → emergency CS.
4. **Any vaginal bleeding before delivery?** **No digital VE** until placenta praevia is excluded (scan or examination in theatre).
5. **If obstruction or rupture is suspected: call for help now**, two large-bore cannulae, Hb and group and cross-match, IV fluids, catheter, **stop any oxytocin**, and prepare theatre.

### Step 1: Focused history

| Ask | Why: the answer that changes the plan |
|---|---|
| **Parity and previous deliveries**: vaginal? size of the biggest baby? instrumental? | **A previous vaginal delivery of a 3.5 kg or bigger baby is the best proof of an adequate pelvis.** A primigravida has an "untested pelvis" |
| **Previous obstructed labour, stillbirth in labour, CS for "CPD" or "failure to progress", fistula** | Suggests a small pelvis. Previous CS for CPD → usually a repeat CS (discuss with a senior) |
| **Type of previous CS incision** (classical or lower segment) | Classical scar → rupture risk too high for labour |
| **Childhood rickets, polio, TB spine, limp; pelvic or hip fracture** (boda boda accidents) | Bony deformity → contracted or asymmetric pelvis |
| **Age** (adolescent) and **height** | Adolescent and under 150 cm → immature or small pelvis |
| **Onset of labour, time of rupture of membranes, liquor colour, time pushing** | Prolonged labour and long ROM → infection, fistula risk, moulding |
| **Estimated gestation and size** (GDM, big previous babies) | Macrosomia → relative CPD, shoulder dystocia |
| **Previous 3rd/4th degree tear, FGM** | Perineal plan (episiotomy, repair by an experienced person) |

### Step 2: Focused examination

**General:** height (**below 150 cm** is a risk marker), gait and limp, spinal deformity, signs of rickets (bowed legs), hydration, pallor.

**Abdominal (the passenger from above):**

1. Lie, presentation, and **estimated fetal weight** (clinical estimate, SFH).
2. **Fifths of the head palpable above the brim** (5/5 free; **2/5 or less = engaged**). This is the **most reliable measure of descent** because caput cannot fool it.
3. Contractions: frequency per 10 minutes and duration.
4. Bladder (distended?), Bandl's ring, tenderness of the lower segment.

**Vaginal examination: the passenger** (after hand hygiene, consent, and with a chaperone):

| Feature | How to assess | Normal / reassuring | Worrying |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cervix** | Dilatation, effacement, application | Well applied, dilating at about 1 cm/h in active labour | **Oedematous, loosely applied**, stalled dilatation |
| **Presenting part** | Vertex, face, brow, breech | Vertex | **Brow** (anterior fontanelle + supraorbital ridges + root of nose) |
| **Position** | Follow the sagittal suture to a fontanelle; **count its sutures (3 = posterior, 4 = anterior)** | OA (small Y-shaped posterior fontanelle anteriorly) | OP; sagittal suture still transverse at or below the spines in the second stage (deep transverse arrest) |
| **Attitude** | Which fontanelle is easiest to feel | Only the posterior fontanelle felt (well flexed, 9.5 cm) | Anterior fontanelle easily felt (deflexed, 11.5 cm) |
| **Station** | Bony leading part relative to the ischial spines | Descending with time (−2 → 0 → +2) | No descent; station "low" only because of **caput** |
| **Moulding** | Parietal bones at the sagittal suture; occipital bone at the lambdoid suture | 0 or +1 | **+3 (overlapping, irreducible)** |
| **Caput** | Scalp oedema over the presenting part | None or small | Large caput with a high head |
| **Liquor** | Colour and smell | Clear | Meconium, blood, offensive |

**Vaginal examination: the passage (clinical pelvimetry)**, best done at the first VE in labour or at 36 weeks:

| Feature | Technique | Adequate | Suggests a contracted pelvis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Diagonal conjugate** | Try to reach the sacral promontory with the middle finger | **Promontory not reached** (diagonal conjugate about 12.5 cm or more) | Promontory easily reached |
| **Sacral curve** | Run the fingers down the sacrum | Hollow, well curved | Flat or forward-projecting sacrum |
| **Side walls** | Sweep laterally | Parallel | Converging |
| **Ischial spines** | Feel both | Blunt, not prominent (interspinous about 10 cm or more) | **Prominent, close together** (android) |
| **Sacrospinous ligament** | Fingers from spine to sacrum | Admits **2 fingers or more** | Narrow |
| **Subpubic angle** | Two fingers under the pubic arch | Admits **2 fingers** (about 90°) | Narrow (below 2 fingers) |
| **Intertuberous diameter** | Closed fist between the ischial tuberosities (outside) | **4 knuckles** (about 8.5 cm or more) | Fewer than 4 knuckles |
| **Coccyx** | Press backwards | Mobile | Fixed, forward-angled |

> [!EXAM]
> In the OSCE, describe pelvimetry in the order **inlet → cavity → mid-pelvis → outlet**: promontory, sacral curve and side walls, spines and sacrospinous ligament, then subpubic angle, intertuberous diameter and coccyx. Finish with "**clinically adequate pelvis**" or "**clinically contracted at the ... level**".

### Step 3: Bedside tests

| Test | What you look for | How it changes the plan |
|---|---|---|
| **Partograph** (start in active labour, cervix 4 cm or more on the modified WHO partograph) | Cervicograph against the alert and action lines; descent in fifths ("O"); **moulding** and liquor with each VE | Crossing the **action line** → full clinician assessment for CPD. **Dilatation stalling + no descent + moulding +3 = obstruction** → CS, not oxytocin |
| **Doppler / Pinard FHR** | 110–160 bpm; decelerations after contractions | Fetal distress with obstruction → urgent CS |
| **Urine** (catheter or void) | **Ketones** (dehydration), **blood** (bladder compression in obstruction), volume | Haematuria in labour = bladder under pressure → obstruction until proven otherwise; catheterise |
| **HemoCue Hb** | Baseline before likely CS | Below 7 g/dL → blood ready before theatre |
| **Bedside ultrasound** (if trained) | Fetal spine and **orbits** show position (orbits anterior = OP); presentation; FHR | Confirms OP, face or brow when the VE is unclear because of caput |

### Step 4: Laboratory & imaging

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management (and Ugandan availability) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Obstetric ultrasound** | Uncertain presentation (brow, face, breech), high head at term in a primigravida, suspected macrosomia or placenta praevia | Presentation, position, estimated fetal weight, placental site, liquor | Brow at term or praevia → CS; macrosomia → senior plan. HC IV (often) and all hospitals |
| **Intrapartum ultrasound for head position** | Second stage, before instrumental delivery if position unclear | Occiput position (OA/OP/OT) | Wrong position is a common cause of failed ventouse. Mainly referral hospitals |
| **Hb and group and cross-match** | Obstructed labour, likely CS, previous CS | Hb; blood available | Anaemia plus likely haemorrhage → have 2 units ready. HC IV and hospitals |
| **Full blood count, blood culture** | Prolonged labour with fever | Raised WCC (interpret with labour norms), bacteraemia | Sepsis antibiotics (ampicillin + gentamicin ± metronidazole). Culture at regional/national referral |
| **Urea and electrolytes, creatinine** | Prolonged obstructed labour, dehydration, oliguria | Raised creatinine (above about 90 µmol/L in pregnancy), low potassium | Fluid resuscitation, catheter, watch renal function. Hospital labs |
| **X-ray / CT pelvimetry** | **Not routine** (no proven benefit); occasionally after a pelvic fracture or before planning birth with known deformity | Measured diameters | Rarely changes practice; labour itself is the best pelvimeter. CT at national referral/private centres |
| **Dye test (methylene blue) / examination under anaesthesia** | Leaking urine after obstructed labour | Fistula site and size | Continuous catheter for 10–14 days (small fresh fistula may heal); referral to a fistula repair centre (e.g. Mulago, Kitovu Hospital, or regional fistula camps) |

### Step 5: Putting it together

**Model one-line summary:**

> "Ms A.T., 17-year-old primigravida, **height 146 cm**, at term, **in labour for 14 hours**, membranes ruptured 9 hours ago with clear liquor. Pulse 108, T 37.6°C, ketones 2+. **Head 3/5 palpable**, cervix **7 cm for the last 4 hours** (crossed the action line), **oedematous**, OT position, station −1, **moulding +3, caput ++**. Pelvimetry: promontory reached, spines prominent, subpubic angle 1.5 fingers. FHR 150 with no decelerations."

**Problem list:** 1) arrest of the active phase with signs of **cephalopelvic disproportion**; 2) contracted pelvis clinically (adolescent, short stature); 3) maternal dehydration and early infection risk (tachycardia, ROM 9 hours); 4) fistula and uterine rupture risk.

**Working diagnosis:** "Obstructed labour secondary to **CPD** in a primigravida with a clinically contracted pelvis." **Plan:** no oxytocin; IV fluids, catheter, antibiotics, Hb and cross-match; **emergency lower-segment CS**; keep the catheter for 10–14 days after delivery.

**Worked example 1: reading a head that "looks low"**

| Finding | Reading |
|---|---|
| VE: "vertex at station +2" | Sounds as if the head is deep in the pelvis |
| But: **large caput ++**, **moulding +3**, sutures hard to feel | The fingertip is on **scalp oedema**, not bone |
| Abdomen: **head 3/5 palpable** | The widest diameter has **not** passed the brim, so the head is **not engaged** |
| **Conclusion** | The head is high and obstructed. **Not suitable for ventouse** (needs 1/5 or less palpable and bony station at or below the spines). Deliver by CS |

**Worked example 2: OP or deep transverse arrest, and what the anatomy tells you**

A multipara has been fully dilated for 2 hours (pushing for 1 hour). The head is **0/5–1/5 palpable**, bony station **+1**, the **sagittal suture is transverse**, moulding +1, contractions 4 in 10 minutes.

- The head is **engaged and at or below the spines** with little moulding, so this is **not obstruction**. It is a **deep transverse arrest**: the head has failed to rotate at the mid-pelvis (often an android pelvis with prominent spines or weak pelvic floor rotation).
- Management: an experienced operator may try **ventouse** (with a posterior/OP cup placed at the flexion point, allowing autorotation) or manual rotation; otherwise CS. Compare this with example 1, where the head was **3/5 palpable with +3 moulding**: that is obstruction, and any vaginal attempt would be dangerous.

> [!PEARL]
> The abdominal **fifths** tell you where the **bone** is; the VE station can be fooled by caput. When the two disagree, **believe the abdomen** and assume the head is higher than it feels.

## Differential diagnosis

### What is the presenting part on VE?

| Finding on VE | Distinguishing features | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Vertex, OA** | Sagittal suture in the oblique/AP diameter; the **small Y-shaped posterior fontanelle** anteriorly | Normal |
| **Vertex, OP** | **Diamond anterior fontanelle** felt anteriorly; posterior fontanelle posteriorly and hard to reach | Longer labour; backache; may rotate |
| **Vertex, deep transverse arrest** | Sagittal suture **transverse** at or below the spines in the second stage, no progress | Android pelvis, weak powers; needs rotation (ventouse, manual) or CS |
| **Brow** | Anterior fontanelle, **supraorbital ridges, root of the nose**; mentovertical 13–13.5 cm | CS at term unless it converts |
| **Face** | **Mouth with gums, nose, malar eminences** (a triangle); no fontanelles | Mento-anterior can deliver; mento-posterior means CS |
| **Breech** | **Anus in line with the ischial tuberosities** (a line, not a triangle); meconium on the glove; sacrum; genitalia | Breech management |
| **Caput mistaken for descent** | Soft, boggy swelling; sutures hard to feel; the abdomen still has 3/5 or more palpable | Obstruction |

### Scalp swellings in the newborn

| Condition | Crosses sutures? | Timing | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Caput succedaneum** | **Yes** | Present at birth, resolves in 1–3 days | Benign |
| **Cephalhaematoma** | **No** (limited to one bone) | Appears after hours; resolves over weeks | Jaundice; rarely an underlying fracture |
| **Subgaleal haemorrhage** | **Yes** (fluctuant, spreads to the neck and ears) | Hours after (often vacuum) delivery | **Hypovolaemic shock**. Monitor Hb, HR and head circumference |
| **Chignon** | Localised to the cup site | After vacuum | Benign; resolves in hours |

## Investigations

The investigations for this topic are mostly **clinical**: abdominal fifths, VE with clinical pelvimetry, and the partograph. They are set out, with their interpretation and Ugandan availability, in **Clinical workup, Steps 2–4** above. Remember that **X-ray or CT pelvimetry is not recommended routinely** (it does not improve outcomes), and that **labour itself is the best test of the pelvis**. After obstructed labour, a **methylene blue dye test** (with cystoscopy at a fistula centre) assesses a suspected vesicovaginal fistula.

## Management

This lecture underpins management in labour and surgery. The practical applications follow.

### 1. Suspected CPD or obstructed labour at a HC IV: step by step

1. **Recognise** it: poor progress on the partograph plus +3 moulding, caput, and a high head (3/5 or more palpable).
2. **Call for help** (medical officer, theatre team). Do **not** augment with oxytocin if obstruction is suspected (risk of rupture).
3. **Resuscitate**: IV access (2 large-bore cannulae), send blood for **Hb and grouping and cross-match**, start **IV fluids** (e.g. Ringer's lactate 1 L over 1–2 hours if dehydrated), and **catheterise** the bladder (it relieves bladder compression, lets you measure urine output, and helps prevent fistula).
4. **Antibiotics** if labour is prolonged, the membranes are ruptured or there is fever: e.g. **ampicillin 2 g IV 6-hourly + gentamicin 5 mg/kg IV daily** (+ **metronidazole 500 mg IV 8-hourly** if chorioamnionitis is suspected), or prophylactic **cefazolin 2 g IV** at CS.
5. **Deliver by caesarean section** (lower segment). Expect a **deeply impacted head**: an assistant can **push the head up vaginally**, or use the **reverse breech extraction (Patwardhan)** technique.
6. **Symphysiotomy** or **destructive procedures** (craniotomy for a dead fetus) are historical or rare options at some units; follow local expertise.
7. **After delivery:** keep the **urinary catheter for 10–14 days** after prolonged obstructed labour (it prevents or may heal early fistula). Watch for PPH (atony), sepsis and uterine rupture signs.

### 2. Pudendal block (for outlet instrumental delivery or perineal repair)

1. Explain, obtain consent, position in lithotomy.
2. **Lidocaine 1%, up to 10 mL each side** (maximum lidocaine without adrenaline **3 mg/kg**, about 200 mg in total, so 20 mL of 1% is already at the limit; if you also need perineal infiltration, dilute to **0.5%** as in the WHO MCPC manual so the total stays within the maximum).
3. Palpate the **ischial spine** vaginally with the index and middle fingers. Guide a long needle (with a guard, e.g. an Iowa trumpet) to **just below and medial to the tip of the spine**, through the **sacrospinous ligament**.
4. **Aspirate** (the internal pudendal artery and vein lie next to the nerve), then inject about 10 mL.
5. Repeat on the other side. Wait 3–5 minutes and test sensation.
6. **Complications:** intravascular injection (seizures, arrhythmia), haematoma, infection.

### 3. Episiotomy and perineal repair: anatomy-driven steps

1. **Indications:** a rigid perineum delaying delivery, instrumental delivery, fetal distress in the second stage. **Not routine.**
2. **Infiltrate** with lidocaine 1% (about 10 mL) along the planned line.
3. Cut a **mediolateral** incision starting at the fourchette and directed **about 60° from the midline** at crowning, during a contraction, while protecting the fetal head.
4. **Repair** (after checking for a 3rd/4th degree tear with a **rectal examination**), using **absorbable polyglactin (Vicryl Rapide) 2-0**:
   - Vaginal epithelium: a continuous suture, **starting 1 cm above the apex**, to the hymenal ring.
   - **Perineal muscles** (bulbospongiosus, transverse perineal): continuous or interrupted sutures to rebuild the **perineal body**.
   - Skin: **subcuticular**.
5. **Rectal examination after repair** to make sure no suture has gone through the rectum. Count swabs and needles.
6. **Aftercare:** analgesia (paracetamol 1 g 6-hourly, or diclofenac 50 mg 8-hourly if not contraindicated), perineal hygiene (sitz baths), and laxatives if there was a 3rd/4th degree tear. For OASIS (3rd/4th degree tears), give broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g. **cefuroxime + metronidazole**) and **repair in theatre by an experienced surgeon**.

### 4. Protecting the ureter and bladder at CS and hysterectomy

1. Open the **uterovesical peritoneum** and **reflect the bladder downwards** before incising the lower segment.
2. Make the uterine incision **transverse, curving slightly upwards laterally** to avoid the uterine vessels.
3. For **lateral extension with bleeding**: pack, call for help, and identify the ureter (palpate it at the pelvic brim or through the posterior leaf of the broad ligament). Place sutures **close to the uterus**.
4. **Uterine artery ligation (O'Leary)** for PPH: a stitch through the myometrium **2–3 cm medial** to the vessels at the level of the lower segment, which avoids the ureter.
5. At **hysterectomy**, clamp the **infundibulopelvic ligament** (if removing the ovaries) or the **ovarian ligament and tube** (if conserving them) close to the uterus, and the **uterine vessels at the level of the internal os, hugging the cervix**.
6. **Suspect injury** if there is haematuria, anuria or a flank mass after surgery, or leaking urine vaginally (fistula) days later.

### 5. Managing a newborn scalp swelling

| Swelling | Management |
|---|---|
| Caput | Reassure; resolves in days |
| Cephalhaematoma | Do **not aspirate** (infection risk). Monitor for **jaundice** (phototherapy if needed) and anaemia; resolves in weeks |
| Subgaleal haemorrhage | **Emergency**: admit to the neonatal unit, monitor HR, BP and head circumference, give **vitamin K 1 mg IM** (if not given), **volume replacement and blood transfusion**, and correct coagulopathy |

### 6. Emergency flow: uterine rupture suspected in obstructed labour

1. **Signs:** sudden severe pain, then cessation of contractions, maternal shock, fetal parts easily palpable, loss of station, fetal heart absent, vaginal bleeding, haematuria.
2. **Resuscitate:** call for help, 2 large-bore cannulae, **cross-match 2–4 units**, crystalloid, oxygen, catheter.
3. **Laparotomy** as soon as possible: deliver the baby and placenta, then **repair** the rupture (if it is simple, e.g. a lower segment tear, and she wants more children) or do a **subtotal/total hysterectomy** (if it is extensive or she is unstable). **Identify the ureters and bladder** because lateral tears extend into the broad ligament.
4. **Antibiotics**, tubal ligation if she consents (repair carries a high risk of repeat rupture), and a **catheter for 10–14 days** if the bladder was involved.

## Complications

| Anatomical problem | Complication |
|---|---|
| Contracted pelvis / CPD | Obstructed labour, uterine rupture, **VVF/RVF**, fetal asphyxia, intracranial haemorrhage, maternal death |
| Prolonged head compression on the pelvic floor | **Obstetric fistula**, foot drop (lumbosacral trunk or common peroneal nerve compression) |
| Perineal trauma | 3rd/4th degree tears leading to **faecal incontinence**; dyspareunia; prolapse later |
| Damage to the pelvic floor and cardinal/uterosacral ligaments | **Uterovaginal prolapse** (especially with grand multiparity, heavy work and early resumption of labour postpartum) |
| Ureteric injury at surgery | Ureterovaginal fistula, hydronephrosis, loss of the kidney |
| Excessive or rapid moulding | Tentorial tear, intracranial haemorrhage |
| Pudendal block | Haematoma, local anaesthetic toxicity |

## Ward-round & exam pearls

### Model VE report (labour ward)

> "On vaginal examination the vulva and vagina are normal. The cervix is **6 cm dilated, 80% effaced, soft and well applied** to the presenting part. Membranes are **ruptured with clear liquor**. The presenting part is the **vertex** at **station −1**. The **sagittal suture is in the right oblique diameter** with the **posterior fontanelle anterior on the left**, so **left occipito-anterior (LOA)**. **Moulding +1, no caput.** The pelvis feels **adequate**: sacral promontory not reached, spines not prominent, subpubic angle admits 2 fingers. **Impression: normal progress in the active first stage.**"

### Likely viva questions with answers

1. **Why does the head enter the pelvis transversely and deliver OA?** The inlet's widest diameter is transverse (13 cm) and the outlet's is AP (13 cm). The gutter of the levator ani rotates the occiput forwards.
2. **Which diameter engages in a well-flexed vertex, and how long is it?** Suboccipitobregmatic, 9.5 cm (with the biparietal diameter of 9.5 cm transversely).
3. **Why can't a brow deliver vaginally at term?** The mentovertical diameter (13–13.5 cm) exceeds every pelvic diameter.
4. **What is the obstetric conjugate, and how do you estimate it?** Promontory to the nearest point on the back of the symphysis (about 11 cm): diagonal conjugate (about 12.5 cm) minus 1.5 cm.
5. **Where is the ureter at risk during CS or hysterectomy?** At the pelvic brim, near the infundibulopelvic ligament, and **under the uterine artery 1.5–2 cm lateral to the cervix**.
6. **What is the main support of the uterus?** The cardinal (Mackenrodt's) and uterosacral ligaments with levator ani.
7. **How do you tell caput from cephalhaematoma?** Caput crosses sutures and is present at birth. Cephalhaematoma is limited by the sutures and appears later.
8. **Nerve roots of the pudendal nerve?** S2, S3, S4 ("S2, 3, 4 keeps the pelvic floor off the floor").

> [!EXAM]
> Drawing a quick **table of skull diameters with presentations** and a **diagram of the pelvic inlet** earns marks in written papers. Practise until you can draw both in under 2 minutes.

### OSCE tips

- On a **pelvis model**, point out the sacral promontory, ischial spines, sacrospinous ligament, subpubic arch and ischial tuberosities, and name the diameters of each plane.
- On a **fetal skull model**, show the fontanelles (count the sutures), demonstrate flexion versus deflexion, and name the presenting diameter for each attitude.
- **Demonstrate moulding grades** with your fingers on the model sutures.

## Mnemonics & memory aids

- **"In Transverse, Out Anteroposterior"**: the inlet is widest transversely and the outlet is widest AP.
- **Skull diameters "9.5, 10, 11.5, 13"**: **S**uboccipito**B**regmatic 9.5 (**S**mall and **B**est), suboccipito**F**rontal 10, **O**ccipito**F**rontal 11.5 (**O**P), **M**ento**V**ertical 13 (**M**ost **V**ast, brow).
- **Fontanelles**: **A**nterior is **A** bigger diamond (4 sutures); **P**osterior is **P**etite (3 sutures, Y).
- **"Water under the bridge"**: the ureter runs under the uterine artery.
- **"S2, 3, 4 keeps the pelvic floor off the floor"** (pudendal nerve, pelvic floor).
- **Ovarian veins: "Right goes Right to the IVC, Left takes a Left turn to the renal vein."**
- **Tube parts, medial to lateral: "In Is A Fine tube"** = **In**terstitial, **Is**thmus, **A**mpulla, **F**imbriae (with the infundibulum). Ectopics are commonest in the **A**mpulla.
- **Uterine supports: "a CUP holds the uterus"**: **C**ardinal, **U**terosacral, **P**ubocervical ligaments, sitting on the **P**elvic floor (levator ani). The round and broad ligaments are not supports.

### Classic exam traps

- **"The pelvic inlet is widest AP."** → The inlet is widest **transversely** (13 cm); the **outlet** is widest AP. That is why the head enters OT and delivers OA.
- **"The fontanelle I feel is large, so it is the posterior one near the symphysis: OA."** → **Count the sutures**: 4 = anterior (diamond), 3 = posterior (Y). Feeling the anterior fontanelle easily means **deflexion/OP**, not OA.
- **"The head is at +2, so ventouse is fine."** → Check the **abdominal fifths**. A big caput can reach +2 while the bony head is still 3/5 palpable. Ventouse needs **1/5 or less** palpable.
- **"Poor progress, so start oxytocin."** → First exclude **obstruction** (moulding +3, high head, Bandl's ring, haematuria). Oxytocin into an obstructed uterus causes **rupture**.
- **"Brow presentation: augment and wait."** → Mentovertical 13–13.5 cm exceeds every pelvic diameter: **CS at term** unless it converts spontaneously.
- **"The round and broad ligaments hold the uterus up."** → The **cardinal and uterosacral ligaments** and **levator ani** are the supports.
- **"The ureter runs above the uterine artery."** → The ureter runs **under** it ("water under the bridge"), 1.5–2 cm lateral to the cervix.
- **"Cephalhaematoma crosses the sutures."** → **Caput** and **subgaleal haemorrhage** cross sutures; a cephalhaematoma is **limited by them**.

### Questions seniors ask

**How do you know the head is engaged?** Abdominally, **2/5 or less** is palpable above the brim; vaginally, the bony leading part is at or near the spines. Engagement means the biparietal diameter (9.5 cm) has passed the inlet.

**Show me how you do clinical pelvimetry.** Promontory (not reached), sacral curve (hollow), side walls (parallel), spines (not prominent), sacrospinous ligament (2 fingers), subpubic angle (2 fingers, about 90°), intertuberous diameter (4 knuckles), coccyx (mobile).

**On VE you feel a diamond-shaped fontanelle anteriorly. What is the position, and why does it matter?** The anterior fontanelle anteriorly means **occipito-posterior** with **deflexion**: the occipitofrontal diameter (11.5 cm) presents, so expect a longer labour, backache and a higher chance of instrumental delivery or CS.

**What are the signs of obstructed labour?** Prolonged labour, **head 3/5 or more palpable** at full dilatation, **moulding +3**, large caput, oedematous cervix, **Bandl's ring**, haematuria, maternal tachycardia, fever and dehydration, and fetal distress.

**Where can you injure the ureter at CS?** Under the uterine artery 1.5–2 cm lateral to the cervix (lateral extensions, uterine artery ligation), at the **pelvic brim**, and near the **infundibulopelvic ligament**.

**Why do we keep a catheter for 10–14 days after obstructed labour?** Prolonged compression of the bladder base between head and pubis causes ischaemic necrosis. Continuous drainage rests the bladder and can let a small early fistula heal.

**Why does the head rotate to OA?** The occiput is the first part to meet the gutter-shaped **levator ani**, which guides it forwards; the **outlet is widest AP**, so the head's longest diameter aligns AP to exit.
